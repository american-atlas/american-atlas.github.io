Ida B. Wells

Journalist who exposed lynching and fought for civil rights and suffrage

1862–1931

Ida B. Wells was born into slavery in Mississippi in 1862 and became the leading crusader against lynching in American history.1,2 After a Memphis mob lynched three Black grocers, one of them her close friend, in 1892, she investigated lynchings across the South. In Southern Horrors and A Red Record she exposed the charge of rape used to justify them as a myth.1,2,3,6 A white mob destroyed her newspaper, and she carried her campaign to the North and to Britain.1,2 In Chicago she helped found Black women’s clubs, helped organize the NAACP, and fought for women’s suffrage. In 2020 she was awarded a posthumous Pulitzer Prize.1,5

Why it matters

Wells used investigative journalism, statistics, and the reports of white newspapers themselves to expose lynching and the excuses made for it.2,3,4,6 She linked the fight against racial violence to the fight for women’s votes, and her career shows how Black women shaped both the civil rights and suffrage movements.1,2

Early life and context

Ida Bell Wells was born enslaved in Holly Springs, Mississippi, on July 16, 1862, the oldest daughter of James and Elizabeth (Lizzie) Wells.2,1 She and her parents were freed at the end of the Civil War.2 During Reconstruction her parents were active in the Republican Party, and her father helped found Rust College, a historically Black college, where she studied.1,2 In the fall of 1878 yellow fever killed both of her parents and one of her siblings. At sixteen she became the main support of her six surviving brothers and sisters, persuading a school official that she was eighteen so she could get a job as a teacher.2,1 In 1882 she moved with her sisters to Memphis, Tennessee, to live with an aunt, and she taught in the city’s segregated schools.1

In May 1884, riding the train with a first-class ticket, she was ordered out of the ladies’ car. She refused, bit a crew member, and was dragged off the train while white passengers, she later wrote, “stood on the seats so that they could get a good view and continued applauding the conductor.”2,1 She sued the railroad and won $500 in a lower court, but the Tennessee Supreme Court reversed the decision.1,2 (The National Park Service places the ride between Memphis and Nashville, while the National Women’s History Museum describes it as her commute to a school in Shelby County, on May 4, 1884.)1,2

The case launched her career as a writer. Under the pen name “Iola” she wrote for Black newspapers on race and politics, earning the nicknames “Princess of the Press” and “The Brilliant Iola.”2,1 In 1889 she became a partner and editor of the Memphis Free Speech and Headlight.2 In 1891 she was fired from her teaching job after criticizing conditions in the city’s Black schools, and she turned to journalism full time.1,2

Major actions and achievements

The Memphis lynching. In 1892 a white mob lynched Thomas Moss, Calvin McDowell, and a third man (the National Park Service names him Will Stewart, the National Women’s History Museum Henry Steward). They owned the People’s Grocery, which had drawn customers from a white-owned store. After a clash with white men, they were jailed, and a mob took them from their cells and killed them.1,2 Moss was Wells’s close friend.2 She investigated, wrote articles condemning the killings, and at the risk of her own life began traveling the South to document other lynchings.1,2

“The old thread-bare lie.” On May 21, 1892, Free Speech published an editorial listing eight recent lynchings and declaring, “Nobody in this section of the country believes the old thread-bare lie that Negro men rape white women.”3 White newspapers called for violence against the writer, and a mob destroyed the paper’s office and press. Wells was away, in New York according to the National Park Service or visiting Oklahoma according to the National Women’s History Museum, and with her life threatened she stayed in the North.1,2,3 From the North she published a long report in T. Thomas Fortune’s New York Age and then the pamphlet Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases (1892).1,2 Quoting the white press’s own reports, she described how lynch mobs formed and exploded the excuses offered for them, above all the claim that Black men were raping white women, which the American Yawp calls the myth of the Black rapist.3,2,6 “A Winchester rifle should have a place of honor in every black home,” she wrote, “and it should be used for that protection which the law refuses to give.”3

A Red Record. In 1893 Wells moved to Chicago, where she and Frederick Douglass protested the exclusion of Black Americans from the World’s Columbian Exposition with the pamphlet The Reason Why the Colored American Is Not in the World’s Columbian Exposition.2 She lectured in Britain in 1893 and 1894, inspiring the formation of the London Anti-Lynching Committee.2 In 1895 she published A Red Record, a statistical history of lynchings for 1892–1894. “The first demand,” she explained, “is for facts and figures.”2 Later, she and her allies hired a white private detective to investigate the lynching of Sam Hose in Georgia and published his report as Lynch Law in Georgia.4 In 1898 she took the anti-lynching campaign to President William McKinley.1

Clubs, the NAACP, and the vote. In 1895 she married the Chicago lawyer and editor Ferdinand L. Barnett, with whom she had four children. She kept her own name alongside his, which few women then did.1,2 She helped found the National Association of Colored Women and other Black women’s organizations, and in 1909 the Negro Fellowship League in Chicago, which offered housing, job help, education, and legal advice to working-class Black residents.1,2 In 1909 she presented “Lynching, Our National Crime” to the conference that led to the NAACP.2 On January 30, 1913, she founded the Alpha Suffrage Club, which organized Black women in Chicago to elect candidates who would serve their community. Its work helped win the Illinois Equal Suffrage Act that June.1 At the 1913 women’s suffrage parade in Washington, organizers told Black women to march at the back to avoid offending white Southerners. Wells refused, waited on the sidelines, and joined the march when the Chicago contingent of white women passed.1,2

Beliefs and ideas

Wells believed that exposing the facts was the most powerful weapon against injustice, and that Black Americans had the right to defend themselves when the law would not.2,3 She held that the fight for women’s rights must include Black women. She respected Susan B. Anthony’s pioneering work but criticized her for not putting Black women’s concerns first in order to keep white women’s support.2 She was wary of reform organizations she found too cautious. The Library of Congress notes her skepticism of the early NAACP’s white leadership and moderate stance.4

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Wells’s outspokenness put her at odds with allies as well as enemies. After her 1892 editorial, a white Memphis newspaper called its unknown writer a “black scoundrel” and warned that “we have had enough of it.”3 The sources describe her role in the NAACP differently. The NAACP lists her among the signers of the 1909 call that founded it, and the National Park Service considers her a founder. The National Women’s History Museum notes that she was left off the list of the “Founding Forty” chosen at the conference. The Library of Congress says she helped organize the association but, skeptical of its white leadership and moderate stance, became inactive after 1912.5,1,2,4 In 1915 she joined William Monroe Trotter’s delegation protesting segregation to President Woodrow Wilson. In 1918 she protested to Wilson against an army order telling Black soldiers to stay out of public places where they were resented. Her letter on behalf of the Negro Fellowship League called it as vicious and undemocratic an order as any issued in the armies fighting Germany and urged Wilson to “protect American soldiers in Democracy at home before sending them abroad in Democracy’s War.”2

Legacy

Wells ran unsuccessfully to be a delegate to the 1918 Republican National Convention and for the Illinois State Senate in 1930.2 She died of kidney disease in Chicago in March 1931. The National Women’s History Museum gives the date as March 15, the National Park Service as March 25.2,1 Her autobiography, Crusade for Justice, was published in 1970, and only then, the Museum notes, did her achievements begin to be fully recognized.2 Her Chicago home became a National Historic Landmark in 1974.1 In 2020 the Pulitzer Prize board gave her a special citation “for her outstanding and courageous reporting on the horrific and vicious violence against African Americans during the era of lynching.”1
