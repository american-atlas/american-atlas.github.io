Concept·Industrialization and the Gilded Age

The Gilded Age

An era of industrial wealth, inequality, and corruption, c. 1870–1900

c. 1870s – c. 1900s

“The Gilded Age” names the decades after Reconstruction, when the United States became the world’s leading industrial economy. The term comes from an 1873 satirical novel by Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner, and it describes a glittering surface of wealth and innovation covering deep inequality and corruption. Railroads, steel, oil, and finance created vast fortunes, while millions of workers and immigrants crowded into cities and fought for decent wages and conditions.

Key facts

Date

Name
From The Gilded Age: A Tale of Today (1873) by Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner
Emblematic figures

Growth
By 1900, about 190,000 miles of railroad; Chicago grew from about 30,000 people in 1850 to 300,000 by 1870
Responses
Labor unions, Populism, and later Progressivism

Why it matters

The Gilded Age created modern industrial America, and the inequalities and conflicts that reformers in the Populist and Progressive movements set out to address.

Definition

A gilded object is covered with a thin layer of gold over a cheaper material. By calling their era “gilded” rather than golden, Twain and Warner mocked a society whose dazzling wealth and technological marvels masked massive social inequities and deep-seated corruption. Historians have adopted the label for roughly the last three decades of the nineteenth century.

Historical context

After the Civil War, industrial capitalism transformed the country. The transcontinental railroad and a vast rail network created national markets. Industrialists such as Andrew Carnegie and John D. Rockefeller built giant corporations, and financiers such as J. P. Morgan consolidated them. Inventors like Thomas Edison electrified cities. Millions of immigrants, increasingly from Southern and Eastern Europe, and rural Americans moved into fast-growing industrial cities; in 1900, nearly 80 percent of Chicago’s population were immigrants or their children.

The benefits were unequal. Workers faced long hours, low pay, dangerous conditions, and periodic depressions, and their strikes were often crushed by force. Politics was marked by patronage and corruption, while the federal government largely refused to regulate business, or used its power against strikers. In the South, the end of Reconstruction gave way to sharecropping and Jim Crow; in the West, Native nations were confined to reservations.

Examples

  • The fortunes and philanthropy of Carnegie and Rockefeller, and debates over whether they were “robber barons” or entrepreneurs.
  • The Haymarket affair (1886), the Homestead strike (1892), and the Pullman strike (1894).
  • The rise of the People’s Party and Populism among farmers.
  • Mass immigration through Ellis Island, and exclusion at Angel Island.
