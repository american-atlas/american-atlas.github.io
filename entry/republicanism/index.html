Republicanism

The founders’ ideal of self-government by virtuous citizens

In the American founding era, republicanism meant government without a king, resting on the people and run by their elected representatives, but it also meant a set of fears and hopes about liberty. Republicans worried that power corrupts, that luxury and dependence destroy virtue, and that free governments survive only if citizens put the public good ahead of private interest. These ideas shaped the Revolution, the Constitution, and early American politics.

Key facts

Core idea
Liberty as freedom from arbitrary power, protected by self-government under law
Intellectual roots
Cicero and Roman historians, Machiavelli, the English republicans, Montesquieu, the eighteenth-century “commonwealthmen”
Not to be confused with
The Republican Party, founded in 1854

Why it matters

Republicanism gave Americans the language they used to justify independence and design their governments. Its insistence that liberty means freedom from domination also gave later reformers — abolitionists, labor activists, suffragists — a vocabulary for challenging the exclusions the founding generation left in place.

Definition

Political philosophers use “republicanism” in two related senses. Historically, it names a tradition of writers — from Roman authors like Cicero through Machiavelli, seventeenth-century English republicans, and Montesquieu to many Americans of the founding generation — who stressed civic virtue, political participation, the dangers of corruption, mixed government, and the rule of law. In its modern philosophical form, it defines liberty as non-domination: being free means not living at the mercy of anyone’s arbitrary power, whether a king, a master, or an unaccountable official.

Historical context

Revolutionary Americans drew on this tradition to interpret British policy. Taxes laid by a Parliament in which they had no voice, standing armies in peacetime, and royal officials who answered to London looked like the first steps toward slavery — a word they used constantly, and with little sense of irony in a society built partly on actual slavery. Independence, in this view, was necessary to preserve liberty from corruption.

After independence, the question was whether a republic could survive across a large territory. Classical thinkers had held that republics must be small. In the Federalist Papers, James Madison turned that argument around, contending that a large, diverse republic would better control factions. The new state constitutions and the federal Constitution tried to build republican government that did not depend on citizens always being virtuous — through representation, elections, and separated powers.

Examples

  • The Declaration of Independence’s claim that governments derive “their just powers from the consent of the governed.”
  • The Constitution’s guarantee to every state of “a Republican Form of Government” (Article IV) and its ban on titles of nobility.
  • Thomas Jefferson’s belief that independent farmers made the best republican citizens, because they depended on no one for their living.
  • Early republican fears of debt, banks, and standing armies, which fueled opposition to Alexander Hamilton’s program.
