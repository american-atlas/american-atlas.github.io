Concept·Revolution and Independence

Republicanism

The founders’ ideal of self-government by virtuous citizens

In the founding era, republicanism meant government without a king, deriving its powers from the people and run by their elected representatives.3 It also meant a set of fears and hopes about liberty. Republicans believed that power corrupts and that a free people must be virtuous, putting the public good above their own interests, or they would lose their freedom.2 These ideas, widely held in the colonies though only by a fringe in Britain, shaped how Americans saw British policy, why they rebelled, and how they designed their new governments.2,4 Philosophers today describe the heart of the tradition as liberty understood as freedom from arbitrary power.1

Key facts

Core idea
Liberty as freedom from arbitrary power, protected by self-government under law
Intellectual roots
Cicero and the Latin historians, Machiavelli, the English republicans, Montesquieu, the eighteenth-century commonwealthmen
Key virtue
Putting the public good above private self-interest
In the Constitution
Article IV guarantees every state “a Republican Form of Government”
Not the same as
The later Republican Party, or Jefferson’s Democratic-Republicans, though both took the name

Why it matters

Republicanism gave Americans the language they used to justify independence and to design their governments, and its fear of concentrated power helped shape the separation of powers and the federal system.2,4,5 It also assigned roles: women, under the ideal of Republican Motherhood, were valued chiefly for raising virtuous citizens.7 Its idea that liberty means freedom from arbitrary power remains influential in political philosophy today.1

Definition

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes two meanings. Historically, republicanism names a loose tradition of writers, from Machiavelli and the English republicans Milton, Harrington, and Sidney to Montesquieu, the English commonwealthmen, and “many Americans of the founding era.” They stressed “civic virtue and political participation, the dangers of corruption, the benefits of a mixed constitution and the rule of law,” and they drew heavily on classical examples from Cicero and the Roman historians.1 In modern political philosophy, republicanism names a theory built on that tradition, in which the highest value is political liberty “understood as non-domination or independence from arbitrary power.” In this view a person is free not merely when no one interferes with them, but when no one has the power to interfere with them at will.1

In Federalist No. 39, James Madison offered a working definition for Americans: a republic is “a government which derives all its powers directly or indirectly from the great body of the people, and is administered by persons holding their offices during pleasure, for a limited period, or during good behavior.”3

Historical context

Colonial political culture drew on the ideas of Britain’s opposition “country” party. The American Yawp explains that this ideology “stressed the corrupting nature of power and the need for those involved in self-governing to be virtuous,” and warned citizens to be “ever vigilant against the rise of conspiracies, centralized control, and tyranny.” Only a small fringe in Britain held these views, but in the colonies they were widely accepted.2

That outlook shaped how colonists read British policy after 1763. Taxes from a Parliament in which they had no voice and troops stationed among them looked like steps toward tyranny.2 Boycotts of British goods turned virtue into daily practice: homespun cloth became “a marker of one’s virtue and patriotism,” and British luxuries became symbols of oppression.2

After independence, republicanism raised a hard question. Classical learning held that a republic had to be small and its people alike, or citizens could not govern themselves. Madison disagreed and argued for “an extended republic” spanning many peoples and regions.4 In Federalist No. 51 he explained how a large republic could protect liberty without relying only on virtue: “If men were angels, no government would be necessary.” Instead, power would be divided and checked.5

Examples

  • The Declaration of Independence’s claim that governments derive “their just powers from the consent of the governed.”8
  • The Constitution’s guarantee that “The United States shall guarantee to every State in this Union a Republican Form of Government” and its ban on titles of nobility.6
  • Republican Motherhood. Historians use this term for the early American belief that women were essential to the republic because they passed on “values of independence and virtue to their children,” so that each generation would cherish the ideals of the Revolution.7
  • Jefferson’s supporters celebrated his election in 1800 as saving the nation’s republican principles, linking the republican virtue of Washington to the democratic liberty Jefferson championed, against what they saw as the military pomp of the Federalists.7
  • Madison’s defense of the new Constitution as “strictly republican” in Federalist No. 39.3
