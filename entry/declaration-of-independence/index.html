The Declaration of Independence

The Continental Congress explains why thirteen colonies are breaking with Britain

July 4, 1776 · Philadelphia, Pennsylvania

The Declaration of Independence, adopted by the Second Continental Congress on July 4, 1776, announced that the thirteen American colonies were “Free and Independent States” and explained to the world why they were breaking with Great Britain.1,2 Drafted mainly by Thomas Jefferson, it is best remembered for its preamble: “We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal,” with rights to “Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness.”1,2 The men who adopted it did not mean it to end slavery or give everyone political rights. Yet, as the American Yawp observes, most of the conflicts of American history since then have been struggles over what it means to believe that “all men are created equal.”6

Why it matters

The Declaration made the United States an independent nation in the eyes of the world and opened the way to the French alliance of 1778.4 Its preamble became the founding statement of American ideals and the standard by which Americans have judged their country ever since.6

Historical circumstances

By 1776 the colonies and Britain had been at odds for more than a decade over taxes, trade, and the powers of Parliament, and fighting had begun at Lexington and Concord in April 1775.4,6 The Second Continental Congress, which met in May 1775, gradually took on the work of a national government. It created the Continental Army, a continental currency, and a post office for the “United Colonies.”3 Most delegates still hoped for reconciliation.4 But in August 1775 King George III declared the colonists to be in “open and avowed rebellion.” Parliament then made all American ships and cargoes forfeit to the Crown, and in May 1776 Congress learned that the king had hired German soldiers to fight in America. Many Americans concluded that Britain was treating them as a foreign enemy.3

Public opinion moved quickly. In January 1776 Thomas Paine published Common Sense, a forty-six-page pamphlet that denounced monarchy and mocked the idea of a continent “perpetually governed by an island”; it sold by the thousands.6,3 In April Congress opened American ports to the trade of other nations, and on May 10 it urged the colonies to form new governments of their own, a step the American Yawp calls “the Congress’s first declaration of independence.”3,6 Independence also had a practical purpose. The colonies needed foreign help, and France would not consider an alliance until they declared themselves independent.4

On June 7, 1776, Richard Henry Lee of Virginia moved “That these United Colonies are, and of right ought to be, free and independent States.” Some delegates were not yet ready, so on June 11 Congress postponed the vote for three weeks and appointed a Committee of Five to draft a declaration: John Adams of Massachusetts, Roger Sherman of Connecticut, Benjamin Franklin of Pennsylvania, Robert R. Livingston of New York, and Thomas Jefferson of Virginia.3 The committee “unanimously pressed on myself alone to undertake the draught,” Jefferson recalled in 1823. He wrote it between June 11 and June 28, and Adams and Franklin made a few changes.3,1 On July 2 Congress adopted Lee’s resolution by a vote of twelve colonies to none, with New York abstaining. That vote was the legal act of independence.6,3 Congress then spent July 3 and part of July 4 revising Jefferson’s draft. The National Archives’ milestone page says the work took “most of July 4th” and ended in the afternoon, while its longer history says revisions ran “into the late morning.”1,3 Among its changes, Congress struck out a passage blaming the king for the Atlantic slave trade. Jefferson wrote that the clause “reprobating the enslaving the inhabitants of Africa” was struck out to please South Carolina and Georgia, and that Northern delegates, whose people had been “pretty considerable carriers” of slaves, also felt “a little tender under those censures.”5,6

That night John Dunlap printed the first copies, signed only by John Hancock as president of Congress and Charles Thomson as secretary. Twenty-six copies of the “Dunlap broadside” survive.3 On July 19, after New York’s convention had approved it, Congress ordered the text “fairly engrossed on parchment” under a new title, “The unanimous declaration of the thirteen united States of America.” Delegates began signing it on August 2. Hancock signed first, in large letters, and fifty-six delegates eventually signed.3,1 Contrary to a common belief, it was not signed by all the delegates on July 4. A few never signed: John Dickinson, who still hoped for reconciliation, and Robert R. Livingston, a member of the drafting committee, who thought it premature.3 The signers’ names were kept secret for months for fear of British reprisals, until Congress ordered an “authentic copy” with their names, printed in January 1777 by Mary Katherine Goddard of Baltimore.3

Principal ideas

The National Archives divides the Declaration into five parts: an introduction, a preamble, a two-part body, and a conclusion.3

The introduction explains that “a decent respect to the opinions of mankind” requires the colonists to “declare the causes which impel them to the separation.”2 The Declaration was addressed not only to Americans but to the world, including the foreign governments whose help the new nation needed.4

The preamble sets out a philosophy of government in a few sentences: that “all men are created equal,” that they have “unalienable Rights” including “Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness,” that governments exist “to secure these rights” and derive “their just powers from the consent of the governed,” and that when a government becomes “destructive of these ends, it is the Right of the People to alter or to abolish it.”2 These ideas, the National Archives notes, were already considered “self-evident” by most eighteenth-century Englishmen. What was new, the American Yawp argues, was that the preamble went beyond the rights of British subjects and appealed instead to natural law.3,6

The grievances form the longest part of the document: a list of “repeated injuries and usurpations” by King George III, meant to prove “a design to reduce them under absolute Despotism.”2,3 The king had dissolved colonial assemblies, kept standing armies among the colonists in peacetime, imposed taxes “without our Consent,” denied them trial by jury, cut off their trade “with all parts of the world,” and was “transporting large Armies of foreign Mercenaries.”2 The list also charged that he had “endeavoured to bring on the inhabitants of our frontiers, the merciless Indian Savages,” a line that reflected the colonists’ hostility toward Native nations.2 The Declaration then reminds the world that the colonists had petitioned the king and appealed to their “Brittish brethren” in vain.2,3

The conclusion repeats Lee’s resolution: “these United Colonies are, and of Right ought to be Free and Independent States.” As independent states, they had “full Power to levy War, conclude Peace, contract Alliances, establish Commerce.” The signers ended by pledging “to each other our Lives, our Fortunes and our sacred Honor.”2

A promise larger than its authors

The Declaration had no legal force as law, and its signers did not intend it to abolish slavery or to extend political rights to all. Jefferson himself had once tried and failed to win permission to free enslaved people in Virginia, and Congress struck his attack on the slave trade from the text.5,6 Yet, as the American Yawp argues, the Revolution’s language of equality “helped highlight” the nation’s inequalities and became a shared aspiration for later movements, from abolitionism and women’s rights to the civil rights movement.6

In 1848 the women and men at the Seneca Falls Convention rewrote the preamble in their Declaration of Sentiments: “We hold these truths to be self-evident; that all men and women are created equal.”7 In 1852 Frederick Douglass asked a Rochester audience, “What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July?” He answered that the holiday revealed “the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim.”8 Abraham Lincoln argued in his debates with Stephen Douglas that slavery violated the Declaration’s assertion that all men are created equal, and that Black Americans were entitled to “all the natural rights enumerated in the Declaration.”10 At Gettysburg in 1863 he dated the nation from 1776, not from the Constitution: “Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal.”9

The parchment’s journey

The signed parchment traveled with Congress during the Revolution, to Baltimore, Lancaster, York, Princeton, Annapolis, Trenton, and New York.3 When the British attacked Washington in August 1814, a State Department clerk, Stephen Pleasonton, had it packed into linen bags and carried by cart and wagon to Leesburg, Virginia, while the White House and other government buildings burned.3 Years of display wore it badly. It went to Philadelphia for the 1876 Centennial and was kept at Fort Knox during World War II, and since 1952 it has been displayed in the Rotunda of the National Archives in Washington.3
