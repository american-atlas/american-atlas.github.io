The Environmental Movement

From Silent Spring to Earth Day and the EPA

1962 – c. 1973

In the 1960s, growing alarm about pollution, pesticides, and the destruction of open space produced a mass environmental movement. Rachel Carson’s Silent Spring (1962) warned of the dangers of pesticides to wildlife and people. The first Earth Day, April 22, 1970, was, in the words of the American Yawp, the largest demonstration in history. A burst of lawmaking followed: the National Environmental Policy Act, the creation of the Environmental Protection Agency in 1970, and new laws to protect air, water, and endangered species.

Why it matters

The environmental movement made protecting nature and public health a permanent responsibility of the federal government.

Goals

Environmentalists sought clean air and water, controls on pesticides and toxic chemicals, protection of wilderness and wildlife, and government accountability for the environmental effects of its projects.

Strategies

The movement grew from Americans’ postwar enjoyment of the outdoors and from science-based warnings like Carson’s. Dramatic events, such as the Cuyahoga River near Cleveland bursting into flames, drew attention. Activists organized mass demonstrations such as Earth Day and lobbied for legislation at every level of government.

Opposition

Chemical companies attacked Silent Spring, suggesting it was the product of an emotional woman rather than a scientist. Industries resisted the cost of regulation; President Nixon, while creating the EPA, impounded billions of dollars Congress had authorized for the Clean Air Act and subjected environmental rules to cost-benefit analysis.

Achievements

President Nixon signed the National Environmental Policy Act in 1970, requiring environmental impact statements for federal projects, and created the Environmental Protection Agency. Amendments to the Clean Air Act required reductions in automobile emissions. Laws protecting water, endangered species, and natural areas followed.

Unresolved questions

Debates over the economic costs of regulation, already visible in the Nixon years, have kept environmental policy contested.
