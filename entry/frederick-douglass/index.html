Frederick Douglass

Escaped slave, orator, editor, and the foremost Black leader of the nineteenth century

1818–1895

Frederick Douglass escaped from slavery in Maryland in 1838 and became the most important leader of the movement for African American civil rights in the nineteenth century.1,2 His Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave (1845) became a bestseller, and his speeches, above all “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?” (1852), made him the nation’s most famous abolitionist orator.1,6 He edited his own newspaper, supported women’s rights from the Seneca Falls Convention onward, recruited Black soldiers during the Civil War, and pressed Abraham Lincoln face to face for equal treatment.1,2,3 After the war he held federal office under five presidents.1

Why it matters

Douglass turned his own life into the most powerful argument against slavery that Americans ever heard, and then spent thirty years after emancipation insisting that freedom meant nothing without citizenship, the vote, and equal rights.1,6 He judged the nation by its own founding words, and he believed in building alliances across racial and political lines to make them true.1,6

Early life and context

Frederick Augustus Washington Bailey was born into slavery on the Eastern Shore of Maryland in February 1818.1,2 He barely knew his mother, who was forced to work on a distant farm and died when he was about seven, and he never knew his father. He later chose February 14 as his birthday, because on his mother’s last visit she had held him on her lap and given him a heart-shaped ginger cake.1,2 Fifteen of his close relatives, including his sister Sarah, were “sold south” during his childhood.2

At eight he was sent to Baltimore to work for the family of Hugh Auld. Sophia Auld began teaching him the alphabet, and he went on to teach himself to read and write in the streets of the city.1,2 At twelve or thirteen he bought The Columbian Orator, a collection of speeches on natural rights and liberty.1,2 When he was fifteen his enslaver, Thomas Auld, sent him back to the Eastern Shore as a field hand. Sent in 1834 to work for Edward Covey, a “slave-breaker” who whipped him repeatedly, he finally fought back and was not whipped again.1,2 He taught other enslaved people to read, and in 1836 he and several others planned an escape. The plot was discovered, and Frederick was jailed and sent back to Baltimore, where he learned the trade of ship caulking and was savagely beaten by white apprentices.1,2

In Baltimore he met Anna Murray, a free Black woman who used her own money to help him escape. On September 3, 1838, dressed as a sailor, he boarded a northbound train, and in less than twenty-four hours he reached New York City.1,4 (The American Yawp dates his escape to 1837; the National Park Service gives September 3, 1838.)10,1 Anna joined him, and they married on September 15. They settled in New Bedford, Massachusetts, where he took the name Douglass and worked as a caulker and laborer. The couple had five children: Rosetta, Lewis, Frederick Jr., Charles, and Annie.1,2,4

Major actions and achievements

Abolitionist. In August 1841, at an antislavery convention on Nantucket, Douglass spoke to a large, mostly white audience, and the Massachusetts Anti-Slavery Society hired him as a lecturer.2 He toured the North and Midwest. Mobs attacked him, as at Pendleton, Indiana, where his hand was broken in 1843, and he was thrown off a train for refusing to ride in a “Jim Crow” car.2 Because some audiences doubted that so polished a speaker had ever been enslaved, he published the Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave in May 1845, naming the people and places of his enslavement.1,2 The book became a bestseller, but it exposed him to recapture.1 He sailed for Britain in August 1845 and spent almost two years lecturing in England, Ireland, and Scotland, and in 1846 British supporters raised £150 to buy his freedom from Hugh Auld.1,2

Editor and organizer. Back in the United States, Douglass moved his family to Rochester, New York, and on December 3, 1847, published the first issue of his own newspaper, The North Star, despite the bitter opposition of his former ally William Lloyd Garrison.2 In 1848 he attended the first women’s rights convention at Seneca Falls and began his lifelong support for women’s suffrage.2 He broke with Garrison in 1851, rejecting Garrison’s view that the Constitution was a proslavery document and that abolitionists should stay out of politics.2,6 He supported the Liberty Party and later the new Republican Party.2 He and Anna also opened their home to freedom seekers, and Douglass acted as a “station master” of the Underground Railroad in Rochester, helping hundreds to freedom.2,4 In 1855 he published a second autobiography, My Bondage and My Freedom, which also attacked racial segregation in the North.1

The Fourth of July. On July 5, 1852, Douglass spoke to the Ladies’ Anti-Slavery Society of Rochester in Corinthian Hall. He insisted on speaking the day after Independence Day.6,2 “What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July?” he asked. “I answer: a day that reveals to him, more than all other days in the year, the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim.”6 Yet he did not reject the nation’s founding documents; he held that the Constitution, rightly read, was not a proslavery document.6

John Brown. Douglass met the militant abolitionist John Brown in 1847, and Brown stayed at his Rochester home in 1858 while planning a slave revolt. In August 1859, at a stone quarry near Chambersburg, Pennsylvania, Brown told him of his plan to attack Harpers Ferry, and Douglass refused to join.2 After the raid, he fled to Canada to avoid arrest as an accomplice and then went on to England.2

The Civil War. Douglass worked to make emancipation an aim of the war and urged that Black men be allowed to fight. “We are striking the rebels with our soft white hand,” he complained, “when we should be striking them with the iron hand of the black man.”1,3 In 1863 he became a recruiter for the Union Army, and two of his sons, Lewis and Charles, joined the 54th Massachusetts Infantry; Lewis was badly wounded in the regiment’s assault on Fort Wagner in July.2,3,7 Angry that Black soldiers received less pay, no promotions, and no protection as prisoners of war, he stopped recruiting and went to see Lincoln at the White House. The National Park Service’s account of their meetings dates that first visit to August 10, 1863, while its chronology places it in July.3,2 “I know who you are Mr. Douglass,” Lincoln said. “Sit down. I am glad to see you.” Douglass left unconvinced on some points but respecting Lincoln’s “humane spirit,” and resumed recruiting.3 In August 1864, expecting to lose the election, Lincoln asked Douglass to organize a network to help enslaved people escape from the Confederate states, a plan made unnecessary by Lincoln’s reelection.3 At the White House reception after the second inauguration, Lincoln greeted him as “my friend Douglass” and told him, “there is no man in the country whose opinion I value more than yours.”3

Reconstruction and public office. After the war Douglass argued that freedom would be empty without the rights of citizenship, and he denounced President Andrew Johnson’s lenient Reconstruction policy.1,2 He saw the Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments adopted between 1865 and 1870.1 In 1872, after his Rochester home burned, he moved to Washington, D.C.1,2 He served as assistant secretary of a commission to Santo Domingo, sat on the board of Howard University, and in 1874 became president of the Freedman’s Bank shortly before it failed.1,2 Under five presidents he served as U.S. Marshal for the District of Columbia (1877–1881), Recorder of Deeds (1881–1886), and minister to Haiti (1889–1891), at a time when violence and fraud severely restricted Black political activity.1 He bought Cedar Hill, an estate on a hill in Anacostia overlooking the capital, and lived there until his death; the National Park Service gives the year of purchase as 1877 in one place and 1878 in another.5,2

Beliefs and ideas

Douglass believed that literacy was the road to freedom, a lesson he learned as a boy in Baltimore.1 He believed in the moral force of plain truth: “The feeling of the nation must be quickened; the conscience of the nation must be roused … the hypocrisy of the nation must be exposed.”6 Unlike the Garrisonians, he came to see the Constitution as an antislavery charter and political action as a legitimate weapon.2,6 He supported women’s rights throughout his life and believed in dialogue and alliances across racial and ideological lines.1

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Douglass’s independence often put him at odds with allies. His decision to start his own newspaper and enter politics led to a bitter public split with Garrison, though the two camps renewed their friendship during the war.2 He was a sharp critic of Lincoln early in the war and later spoke of him with deep ambivalence. At the dedication of the Freedmen’s Memorial in 1876 he said that Lincoln had been, “in his prejudices, … a white man,” and that white Americans were Lincoln’s children while Black Americans were “at best only his step-children.”3

His commitment to Black voting rights strained his alliance with the women’s movement. In 1869 he broke with feminist leaders who refused to support the Fifteenth Amendment unless it also gave women the vote, arguing that the ballot was a “question of life and death” for Black men in the South.2,9 In 1872 the Equal Rights Party nominated him for vice president on Victoria Woodhull’s ticket without his knowledge or consent; he campaigned instead for Ulysses S. Grant.1,2 In 1884, two years after Anna’s death, he married Helen Pitts, a white activist from an abolitionist family who had worked as a clerk in his office. The marriage stirred controversy because she was white and twenty years younger.1,4

Legacy

Douglass remained active to the end. In January 1894 he delivered his last great address, “Lessons of the Hour,” attacking the lynching of Black Southerners.2 On February 20, 1895, he attended a meeting of the National Council of Women in Washington, returned home to Cedar Hill, and died of a heart attack that evening, at seventy-seven.1,2 He was buried in Mount Hope Cemetery in Rochester.2 Helen Pitts Douglass devoted her remaining years to preserving Cedar Hill. Congress chartered the Frederick Douglass Memorial and Historical Association at her urging in 1900, and it received the property on her death in 1903.4,11 Cedar Hill is now the Frederick Douglass National Historic Site, and about 70 percent of the objects displayed there belonged to Douglass or his household.5
