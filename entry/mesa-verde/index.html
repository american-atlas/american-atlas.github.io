Mesa Verde

Cliff dwellings of the Ancestral Pueblo people

c. 1180–1300 · Southwestern Colorado

Mesa Verde, in southwestern Colorado, has some of the largest and best-preserved cliff dwellings in North America.1 People lived in the area for about 12,000 years, and Ancestral Pueblo farmers built villages on the mesa tops for centuries.3 Then, between about 1180 and 1300, they built stone villages tucked into the cliff alcoves, most likely to be near the springs as droughts made water scarce.1 Within a few decades of the late 1200s they had moved south to join communities in present-day New Mexico and Arizona, where their Pueblo and Hopi descendants live today.3 In 1906 Mesa Verde became the first national park created to protect cultural heritage.2

Why it matters

Mesa Verde shows how Ancestral Pueblo communities adapted to a demanding environment, and its creation as the first national park devoted to cultural heritage began federal efforts to protect the archaeological record of Native America. It remains a sacred place for the 27 Pueblos, Tribes, and Nations connected to it.2,3

About the place

The cliff dwellings sit in alcoves that water and frost carved out of the Cliff House Sandstone. Rain and snow seep down through the porous sandstone until they hit a layer of shale, then flow sideways and emerge from the canyon walls as seeps and springs, the only permanent water on the mesa.5 The same process loosens blocks of sandstone, which the builders used for their homes.5 Living rooms can be recognized by their hearths and grinding bins, and narrow rooms served as granaries.1 The park preserves nearly 5,000 ancestral sites, and rangers lead tours into Cliff Palace, Balcony House, Long House, and other dwellings.2,6

History

Before the cliff dwellings. Human history at Mesa Verde goes back about 12,000 years, to hunters who used spear points to kill mammoths, ground sloths, and giant bison.3 Between about 500 and 1300 CE, tens of thousands of Ancestral Pueblo people lived across the Four Corners region. Over those centuries they shifted from a mix of farming, hunting, and gathering to a diet in which corn, beans, and squash made up 90 percent of what they ate, supported by dams, terraces, and reservoirs.3 They traded for ocean shells, macaw feathers, and gemstones, and made the black-on-white pottery for which Mesa Verde is known.3 At Step House people built pithouses around 700 CE and returned to build a masonry village around 1200, five centuries later.1

Building in the cliffs. Most cliff dwellings were built between 1180 and 1300. They are among the last structures built in the region, and their residents were among the last to leave.1 Why build there? The Park Service says most scholars point to water: tree rings show long droughts, reservoirs on the mesa tops were becoming less reliable, and the dwellings were placed near seep springs.1 People carved small basins into the rock to collect the water.5 The American Yawp emphasizes defense instead, calling the dwellings “highly defensible.”7

The villages. Most dwellings were small, averaging eight to ten rooms.1 Balcony House had 38 rooms and two kivas for over thirty people. Long House had about 150 rooms and 21 kivas around a large plaza, and Spruce Tree House about 130 rooms.1 Cliff Palace, the largest cliff dwelling in North America, had about 150 rooms and 23 kivas for over 100 people. The Park Service notes that it had one kiva for every three rooms, twice the usual share, and suggests it became a public center because of its size: “they became important because they were big, they weren’t big because they were important.”1 Sources date its construction slightly differently: the Park Service gives 1190 to 1280, while the Yawp says it was built from 1190 and expanded until 1260.6,7

Leaving. People had always moved between communities. In the mid-1200s they began to move south, and over several decades they left Mesa Verde entirely.3 The Park Service points to “persistent drought, limited resources, and social change,” and adds that for Pueblo people migration is an important cultural activity: people “may have decided it was simply time to move on and continue their people’s journey.”3 They joined or founded villages along the Rio Grande, the Little Colorado River, and the Hopi Mesas.3 Ute, Navajo, and Apache peoples also used Mesa Verde as hunting and gathering grounds.3

Rediscovery and protection. The 1859 San Juan Exploring Expedition was the first to officially use the name Mesa Verde. The photographer William Henry Jackson later took the first photographs of a cliff dwelling in the region.4 One December day, the local rancher Richard Wetherill and his brother-in-law Charles Mason, guided by the Ute Acowitz, came upon Cliff Palace while looking for lost cattle. The Wetherills went on to enter 182 cliff dwellings and to collect and sell artifacts.4 Their father wrote to the Smithsonian asking that the area be made a national park, fearing that “the tourists, will destroy them.”4 The Swedish scientist Gustaf Nordenskiöld excavated and photographed many sites and shipped about 600 objects to Europe.4 The Colorado Cliff Dwellings Association, led by Virginia McClurg and Lucy Peabody, campaigned for protection. After several failed bills, Congress created Mesa Verde National Park, signed by President Theodore Roosevelt on June 29, 1906.4,2 In 2006 the park reburied all Native American human remains found within its boundaries after twelve years of consultation with associated tribes.4
