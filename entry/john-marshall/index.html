John Marshall

The chief justice who made the Supreme Court a co-equal branch of government

Virginia; Washington, D.C.

John Marshall was the fourth Chief Justice of the United States, presiding over the Supreme Court from 1801 until his death in 1835.1,3 A Revolutionary War officer and Virginia lawyer, he took over a weak tribunal whose first justices had complained of its “limited stature,” and his leadership, the Court’s own history says, was “central to the development of its prominent role in American government.”3 In Marbury v. Madison he established the Court’s power to strike down unconstitutional laws.5 In McCulloch v. Maryland and Gibbons v. Ogden he gave the federal government broad power.6,7 More than any other judge, he defined what the Constitution would mean in practice.

Why it matters

Marshall turned a weak, little-respected court into a co-equal branch of government. His opinions on judicial review, federal power and commerce remain foundations of American constitutional law.3,5,7

Early life and context

Marshall was born on September 24, 1755, in Virginia and grew up on his father’s estate, Oak Hill. At a local academy he befriended a future president, James Monroe.2,1 When the Revolution came he served as a lieutenant in the Culpeper County Minutemen and then as an officer in the Continental Army’s Eleventh Virginia Regiment, rising to captain.1,2

In 1780 he studied at the College of William and Mary, then practiced law in Fauquier County and, from 1783, in Richmond, where he spent most of his adult life.1,2 He served several terms in the Virginia legislature, and in 1788 he was a delegate to the Virginia convention that ratified the Constitution.1

Major actions and achievements

  • The XYZ Affair. In 1797 President John Adams sent Marshall, Elbridge Gerry and Charles Cotesworth Pinckney to France to stop French seizures of American ships. Agents of Foreign Minister Talleyrand, later identified only as X, Y and Z, demanded a loan and a bribe before talks could begin.4 The affair led to the undeclared Quasi-War with France, and Marshall came home praised nationally for refusing to give way.4,2
  • To the Court. After a term in Congress, Marshall became Adams’s secretary of state in 1800. Weeks before leaving office, Adams nominated him chief justice; the Senate confirmed him on January 27, 1801, and he took the oath on February 4.1,3
  • Judicial review. In Marbury v. Madison (1803) he wrote that “a Law repugnant to the Constitution is void,” the first time the Court struck down a law passed by Congress.5 After 1810 the Court extended judicial review to state laws.8
  • National power. In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) he upheld Congress’s implied power to charter a national bank and barred states from taxing it: “the power to tax involves the power to destroy.”6 In Gibbons v. Ogden (1824) he struck down New York’s steamboat monopoly, ruling that Congress, not the states, regulates interstate commerce.7

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Jeffersonian Republicans saw Marshall as a Federalist using the bench against them. Jefferson complained that judicial review “would make the Judiciary a despotic branch.”8 Marshall’s ruling for the Cherokee in Worcester v. Georgia (1832), holding that Georgia’s laws did not apply in Cherokee territory, was ignored by Georgia and not enforced by the federal government, and it did not prevent the Cherokee’s forced removal on the Trail of Tears.9 The case exposed the limits of a court that cannot enforce its own judgments.

Legacy

Marshall died in Philadelphia, where he had gone for medical treatment, on July 6, 1835, at seventy-nine, and was buried in Richmond.2,1 He served thirty-four years and five months.3 The National Archives writes that after Gibbons the federal government “increasingly exercised its authority by legislation and judicial decision over the whole range of the nation’s economic life,” and that the Court’s power to invalidate laws contrary to the Constitution “has never been seriously challenged.”7,5

Small disagreements in the record

Even Marshall’s basic biography varies by source. The Federal Judicial Center gives his birthplace as Prince William County, Virginia, while the American Battlefield Trust says Fauquier County.1,2 The Judicial Center dates his service as a Minuteman lieutenant to 1775–1776 and his captaincy to 1777; the Battlefield Trust says he enlisted in 1776 and was a captain “by July of 1778.”1,2 The Battlefield Trust also says he completed a law degree at William and Mary, while the Judicial Center lists only his attendance there in 1780.2,1

The Liberty Bell story

When Marshall died, the Liberty Bell in Philadelphia was rung. A local legend says that this was when the bell cracked; the American Battlefield Trust calls it a myth.2
