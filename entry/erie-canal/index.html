The Erie Canal

The 363-mile waterway that linked the Great Lakes to the Atlantic, 1817–1825

1817–1825 · Albany to Buffalo, New York

Built between 1817 and 1825, the Erie Canal ran 363 miles across upstate New York from Albany on the Hudson River to Buffalo on Lake Erie.1,2 At the time it was the longest artificial waterway and the greatest public works project in North America.1 Dug largely by hand and climbing hills through 83 locks, it cut shipping costs by 90 percent and opened the interior of the continent to settlers and trade.1,2 It made New York City the nation’s leading port and financial center and launched a national canal-building boom.1,4 It also sped the dispossession of the Haudenosaunee nations whose homelands it crossed.5

Why it matters

The Erie Canal was the centerpiece of the transportation revolution that tied the young nation together. By connecting the Midwest to the Atlantic, it made New York the “Empire State” and showed what public investment and engineering could achieve, at a heavy cost to the Native nations along its route.3,1,5

Historical background

After the War of 1812 Americans looked to strengthen the nation through “internal improvements,” government spending on roads and canals; President Madison called for national roads and canals in his seventh annual message.4 Overland hauling by ox-drawn wagon was slow and costly.1 In New York, canals and locks on the Mohawk River, built by a private company, already let freight boats travel west from Schenectady.6

DeWitt Clinton, a former state legislator, U.S. senator and mayor of New York City, became the canal’s “most persistent and effective champion.” He served on the commission that made the first surveys for a canal across the state and led the political fight to build it.1

Causes and contributing factors

  • Markets in the West. Farmers, loggers, miners and manufacturers in the Great Lakes region needed a cheap route to eastern markets.1,4
  • State enterprise. New York State built and paid for the canal itself, at a cost of $7,143,789, which it recovered within ten years.2,6
  • Clinton’s vision. Clinton predicted that the canal would make New York America’s leading state.1

What happened

Construction began at Rome, New York, in 1817.2 Because few engineers or contractors had ever built a canal, the project became “the nation’s first practical school of civil engineering.”1 Some laborers were Irish immigrants, but most were American-born. For eight years they worked through heat, mud and freezing cold, felling trees and digging mostly with hand tools and animal power, for 80 cents to a dollar a day.1,2 They invented machines to pull stumps, developed a hydraulic cement that hardened underwater, and blasted rock with hand drills and black powder.1

The hardest work was the Lockport Flight, a staircase of five locks blasted through the solid rock of the Niagara Escarpment, raising and lowering boats 49 feet.1 The canal crossed rivers on 18 aqueducts and climbed 571 feet through 83 locks.2 In the fall of 1825 Governor Clinton rode the first boat to travel its full length, the Seneca Chief, from Buffalo to New York Harbor for a “Wedding of the Waters” ceremony.5

Immediate consequences

Packet boats carried passengers from Albany to Buffalo in five days, less than half the stagecoach time, and freight costs fell by 90 percent compared with ox-drawn wagons.1,2 Midwestern produce flowed east to Albany and down the Hudson to New York City, while manufactured goods and settlers flowed west.1,4 The canal gave rise to so many towns that it was called the “Mother of Cities”; the populations of Syracuse, Rochester and Buffalo quadrupled between 1830 and 1850.1,3

Long-term significance

The canal’s success “launched a canal-building boom,” the American Yawp writes; by 1840 Ohio had two all-water links from Lake Erie to the Ohio River.4 The Erie captured most of the Great Lakes trade, “securing New York City’s position as the nation’s largest and most economically important city.”4 It carried more westbound immigrants than any other canal across the Appalachians, and ideas traveled along it too: abolitionism, women’s suffrage, utopian experiments and new religious movements all flourished on its corridor.1 Enlarged three times, the canal system has operated continuously since 1825, longer than any other constructed transportation system in North America; its old towpath is now a 365-mile bike path.3,1

The canal and the Haudenosaunee

The canal ran through the homelands of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy and the Mohican Nation. The Erie Canalway’s own history states that the canal boom “dramatically accelerated the dispossession and disruption” of Native life, during years when state and federal policy promoted “Indian removal” from New York.5 The irony did not stop at the boat’s name, Seneca Chief. In 1811 Clinton had predicted that “before the passing away of the present generation, not a single Iroquois will be seen in this state.” He was wrong: more members of the Six Nations live in New York today than at the end of the Revolution.5

How long was it?

The Erie Canalway gives the original canal’s length as 363 miles, while the American Yawp describes a “350-mile-long human-made waterway.”2,4

Later life

The canal was enlarged in the mid-1800s to 7 feet deep with 110-foot locks, reached its peak tonnage in 1880 and ended tolls in 1882. Between 1905 and 1918 the Barge Canal replaced it, with locks for boats up to 300 feet long. The later opening of the St. Lawrence Seaway let ships sail from the Great Lakes straight to the Atlantic, and commercial traffic declined.6,2
