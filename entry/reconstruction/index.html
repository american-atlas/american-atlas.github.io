Reconstruction

The struggle to rebuild the Union and define freedom after the Civil War

1865–1877 · The former Confederate states and the nation

Reconstruction was the era in which the United States tried to reunite the nation after the Civil War and decide what freedom would mean for four million formerly enslaved people.1 Congress wrote the Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments into the Constitution, abolishing slavery, making Black Americans citizens, and forbidding racial discrimination in voting.4,5,6 Black Southerners built schools and churches, searched for lost family members, voted, and held office, as many as 2,000 of them.1,10 Terrorist violence, economic depression, and Northern retreat brought Reconstruction to an end by 1877, opening the way to Jim Crow.1,11

Why it matters

Reconstruction rewrote the Constitution to make Black Americans citizens and voters, and its defeat showed how fragile those rights were without the will to enforce them.1,6

Historical background

When the Civil War ended in 1865, much of the South lay in ruins. “We had passably good roads,” one South Carolinian told a Northern reporter, “and the next week they were all gone… miles and miles of iron have actually disappeared.”1 The great questions of the peace were just as unsettled. Would the defeated states return as equals or be governed as conquered territory? Who would rebuild their governments? And what rights did freedom give to formerly enslaved people?1

In a sense, Reconstruction had begun during the war itself. In November 1861 Union forces won the Battle of Port Royal Sound and took control of the coast of South Carolina around Beaufort. The planters fled, leaving behind more than ten thousand enslaved people, and the Lincoln administration launched the “Port Royal Experiment” to help them become self-sufficient. On St. Helena Island, one of the country’s first schools for freed people opened; at nearby Camp Saxton, where some of the first African Americans joined the U.S. Army, elaborate ceremonies were held on New Year’s Day 1863 to announce and celebrate the Emancipation Proclamation.2

President Abraham Lincoln began planning for reunion in late 1863. Under his “ten percent” plan, once a tenth of a state’s voters had sworn an oath of loyalty, loyal Unionists could form a new state government; such governments arose in Louisiana, Tennessee, and Arkansas.1 The Emancipation Proclamation freed people only in areas still in rebellion, leaving more than seven hundred thousand people in bondage in Delaware, Kentucky, Maryland, and Missouri and in Union-held parts of the South.1 To end slavery everywhere, Congress passed the Thirteenth Amendment on January 31, 1865, and the states ratified it on December 6 of that year.4 The amendment abolished slavery “except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted.”1 In March 1865 Congress also created the Freedmen’s Bureau within the War Department to manage abandoned lands and help formerly enslaved people toward self-sufficiency.7

Causes and contributing factors

Lincoln did not live to carry out his plans. After his assassination in April 1865, Vice President Andrew Johnson became president.1 A Tennessee Democrat who had been the only Southern senator to keep his seat when his state seceded, Johnson was devoted to the Union but held deeply racist views, and he had no intention of forcing Southern states to grant Black Americans equal rights.3 Taking advantage of the eight months when Congress was in recess, he rushed through his own program. He handed out thousands of pardons, restored former Confederates’ property, and appointed provisional governors for the Southern states.3,8 Those states had only to cancel their ordinances of secession, repudiate their Confederate debts, and ratify the Thirteenth Amendment; on everything else they could do as they pleased. Only planters with more than $20,000 in property had to apply to Johnson personally for a pardon.1

The new Southern governments quickly passed laws known as Black Codes, beginning in South Carolina and Mississippi. The codes allowed African Americans to own property, marry, and make contracts, but barred them from juries and militias, refused to accept their testimony against white people, apprenticed Black orphans to their former enslavers, and established harsh vagrancy laws.1 Mississippi required every freedman to carry papers proving he had work; a man without them could be arrested and fined, and if he could not pay, the sheriff could hire him out to anyone willing to pay the tax.1 One scholar has called such systems “slavery by another name,” and the Miller Center describes the Black Codes as having “essentially maintained slavery under another name.”1,3

The codes, together with mob violence against Black Southerners, turned Northern opinion against Johnson. When the president declared the Southern states restored, congressional Republicans refused to seat their representatives.1 Some Republicans, like Congressman Thaddeus Stevens, believed in racial equality, but most, the American Yawp argues, were moved chiefly by party interest: giving the vote to hundreds of thousands of Black men was the only way to protect Republican power in the South.1,3

What happened

In 1866 Congress took control. It extended the Freedmen’s Bureau and passed the Civil Rights Act, which for the first time defined all persons born in the United States, except Native Americans, as citizens and protected their “fundamental rights”; both laws passed over Johnson’s vetoes.1,3 To ensure that the Civil Rights Act was constitutional, Congress approved the Fourteenth Amendment on June 13, 1866.5 Its first section declared that “All persons born or naturalized in the United States” were citizens and forbade any state to deny any person “life, liberty, or property, without due process of law” or “the equal protection of the laws.” Its main author, Congressman John A. Bingham of Ohio, intended it to make the Bill of Rights binding on the states as well.5 Johnson opposed it and took his case to the voters in a speaking tour during the 1866 elections, attacking his opponents in abusive language; one observer estimated that he lost a million Northern votes.3 Republicans won a veto-proof majority.8

The Reconstruction Acts of 1867 dissolved the governments of ten Southern states and divided them into five military districts, whose commanders reported to General Ulysses S. Grant.8,1 To return to the Union, a state had to write a new constitution giving Black men the vote and ratify the Fourteenth Amendment.1 When Johnson fired Secretary of War Edwin Stanton in defiance of the Tenure of Office Act, the House impeached him by a vote of 126 to 47 in February 1868; the Senate fell one vote short of convicting him.3 The Fourteenth Amendment was ratified on July 9, 1868.5 That November, some 400,000 formerly enslaved men voted for the first time, and Grant, campaigning under the slogan “Let us have peace,” defeated the Democrat Horatio Seymour by 214 electoral votes to 80.8 The Fifteenth Amendment, which forbade any state to deny the vote “on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude,” passed Congress in February 1869 and was ratified on February 3, 1870.6

The result was the first moment of mass democratic participation for African Americans. In 1860 only five Northern states had allowed Black men to vote on equal terms with white men; within a few years the South was governed by Republican-led states with Black men in power for the first time in American history.1 Hundreds of Black delegates helped write the new state constitutions, which established public schools in a region where they had barely existed; by the end of Reconstruction every Southern state had one.1,3 Historians estimate that as many as 2,000 Black men held office, from local levee commissioner to U.S. senator. The American Yawp textbook puts the number at “more than two thousand.”10,1 At the local level they served as tax assessors, voter registrars, justices of the peace, school superintendents, sheriffs, and jurors, which gave Black citizens access to a fair hearing in court, public jobs, and public schools.10 Republican clubs, Union League chapters, and Black churches organized the voters, and Black women, who could neither vote nor hold office, debated the issues, mobilized voters, and accompanied men to the polls as a show of solidarity.10 Almost 800 Black men served in state legislatures, and for a time they made up a majority of the South Carolina House of Representatives. P. B. S. Pinchback served as governor of Louisiana for thirty-four days, the only Black governor of any state until 1989.1

On January 20, 1870, the Mississippi legislature elected Hiram R. Revels, a North Carolina-born preacher, to the U.S. Senate. Democrats argued that he had not been a citizen for the nine years the Constitution requires, but on February 25 the Republican majority voted to seat him, and he became the first Black member of Congress.9 On December 12, 1870, Joseph H. Rainey of South Carolina became the first Black member of the House, and within five years fourteen more Black members followed them to Capitol Hill.9 The abolitionist Wendell Phillips hailed Revels as “the fifteenth amendment in flesh and blood.”9

Freedom was also lived far from the capitals. Above all, freedpeople wanted land. In January 1865 General William T. Sherman’s Special Field Order No. 15 set aside coastal land in Georgia and South Carolina for them, but Sherman lacked the authority to give it away, and in 1866 land that former Confederates had abandoned was returned to them.1 The Freedmen’s Bureau, led by General Oliver O. Howard, issued rations and clothing, ran hospitals, supervised labor contracts, helped found schools, legalized marriages made in slavery, and provided transportation for people trying to reunite with their families.7 Freedpeople placed newspaper advertisements searching for relatives sold away, and kept placing them until the turn of the twentieth century.1 Many dropped the names their enslavers had given them and took new ones, like Freeman or Lincoln.1 Schools filled with students from three to eighty years old, and churches became the center of Black community life; by the 1930s, nearly 40 percent of 663 Black churches surveyed traced their roots to the years after emancipation.1

Reconstruction also opened, and then split, the movement for women’s rights. “Now in the reconstruction,” Elizabeth Cady Stanton declared in May 1866, “is the opportunity, perhaps for the century, to base our government on the broad principle of equal rights for all.” That year women’s rights advocates and abolitionists formed the American Equal Rights Association.1 But the Fourteenth Amendment wrote the word “male” into the Constitution for the first time, and the Fifteenth did not forbid discrimination by sex. Frederick Douglass insisted that the ballot was a “question of life and death” for Southern Black men, and the movement broke in two. Stanton and Susan B. Anthony formed the National Woman Suffrage Association, while supporters of the Fifteenth Amendment founded the American Woman Suffrage Association.1

White Southern resistance was fierce and often violent. In 1865 the investigator Carl Schurz found nearly unanimous agreement among white Southerners that “You cannot make the negro work, without physical compulsion.”1 White mobs attacked Black residents and Republicans in Memphis and New Orleans in 1866, and later in Laurens, South Carolina (1870), Colfax, Louisiana (1873), Yazoo City, Mississippi (1875), and Hamburg, South Carolina (1876).1 The Ku Klux Klan, organized in Pulaski, Tennessee, in 1866, had spread to nearly every former Confederate state by 1868, alongside similar groups such as the Knights of the White Camellia and the White Line.1 Of the Black delegates to the 1868 state constitutional conventions, one in ten had suffered physical abuse.3 In Panola County, Mississippi, twenty-four Klan-style murders took place between August 1870 and December 1872.1 Congress answered with the Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871, which made it a federal crime to deprive African Americans of their civil rights and allowed the use of troops against the Klan, and for a time federal courts and troops worked to put an end to the Klan and similar groups.1 But the violence continued. In Vicksburg, Mississippi, in 1874, when the Black sheriff Peter Crosby refused white demands that he resign, white men opened fire on the Black citizens who had gathered in protest, killing twenty-nine.10

Immediate consequences

Northern commitment faded. After the banking house of Jay Cooke and Company failed in September 1873, the country sank into a six-year depression, and economic questions pushed Reconstruction off the national agenda.1 Democrats won the House of Representatives in 1874.1 In the South, white Democrats calling themselves Redeemers promised rule by white men; by 1871 they had already taken back Tennessee, Virginia, and Georgia.1 In 1875 Mississippi Democrats carried out the “Mississippi Plan,” a campaign of violence to drive Black voters from the polls; the state’s Republican governor begged for federal help, and national Republicans ignored him.1

The presidential election of 1876 ended the experiment. The Democrat Samuel J. Tilden won the popular vote by about 250,000 and stood one electoral vote short of victory, while both parties claimed Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina, where violence and intimidation had kept Black voters away from the polls. Republican-controlled returning boards in those states then threw out enough Democratic ballots to give Hayes the lead.11 Congress created a fifteen-member Electoral Commission, which voted eight to seven along party lines to award every disputed state to the Republican Rutherford B. Hayes. The count was completed in the early hours of March 2, 1877, and Hayes won by 185 electoral votes to 184.11 Historians still debate what was promised behind the scenes. The traditional account, followed by the American Yawp, describes a Compromise of 1877 in which Democrats accepted Hayes in exchange for the withdrawal of federal troops and economic favors for the South; the Miller Center’s historians consider negotiations a virtual certainty but doubt that any deal went beyond what Hayes had already promised in his letter of acceptance.1,11 Either way, in April 1877 the last troops were ordered away from the Southern statehouses, and white Democrats returned to power across the South.1

With federal support withdrawn, the number of Black officeholders declined significantly.10 Only six new Black members of Congress were elected after 1874, and when George Henry White of North Carolina left the House in 1901, no African American would sit in Congress again until 1928.9 In the 1890s Southern states wrote literacy tests, “grandfather clauses,” and other devices into their laws to strip Black men of the vote, and in 1896 the Supreme Court approved “separate but equal” segregation in Plessy v. Ferguson.6

Long-term significance

For generations, the defeated side told the story. White Democrats portrayed Reconstruction as a disastrous interlude of corrupt “Negro rule,” and that partisan view shaped textbooks, scholarship, and films such as The Birth of a Nation (1915). Its message, that African Americans were unfit to hold office, was “demonstrably false but dangerously pervasive.”10 It grew alongside the “Lost Cause” version of the Civil War, which claimed that the Confederacy had fought for constitutional principles rather than slavery.8,1

Historians today see Reconstruction as a moment of revolutionary possibility followed by violent backlash.1 Its economic promises went largely unmet: lacking land, many Black farmers turned to sharecropping, which often trapped families in debt, and under the convict-lease system, which lasted until World War II, arbitrary arrests under vagrancy laws often led to decades of forced, unpaid labor.1 But its constitutional achievements endured. Black and white citizens kept arguing, in petitions, courts, and dissenting opinions, for the promise of the Fourteenth Amendment, and those arguments became the basis for change in the twentieth century.5 The Fifteenth Amendment found new force in the Voting Rights Act of 1965.6 Birthright citizenship, equal protection of the laws, and the right to vote regardless of race all date from these years.5,6
