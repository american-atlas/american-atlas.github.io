United States v. Nixon

The Supreme Court orders the president to release the Watergate tapes

1974 · Washington, D.C.

In United States v. Nixon, decided July 24, 1974, the Supreme Court unanimously rejected President Richard Nixon’s claim that “executive privilege” allowed him to withhold White House tape recordings subpoenaed in the Watergate prosecutions. The Court recognized a limited privilege but held that it must yield to the needs of the criminal justice system. The tapes Nixon was forced to release led to his resignation sixteen days later.

Why it matters

United States v. Nixon affirmed that no one, including the president, is beyond the reach of the courts.

Historical context

A grand jury indicted seven of Nixon’s closest aides in the Watergate affair. The special prosecutor, Leon Jaworski, subpoenaed recordings of Oval Office conversations. Nixon refused, claiming executive privilege — the right to keep confidential communications within the executive branch — and argued the courts had no power to review his claim.

The legal question

Is the president’s executive privilege entirely immune from judicial review?

The ruling

No. The Court held that neither the separation of powers nor the general need for confidentiality could sustain an absolute, unqualified presidential privilege in the face of a criminal subpoena. Nixon had to produce the tapes.

The Court’s reasoning

The Court acknowledged a limited privilege, strongest in military and diplomatic matters, but gave priority to “the fundamental demands of due process of law in the fair administration of justice.” A generalized claim of confidentiality could not override the need for evidence in a criminal trial, and it was the courts’ role to decide such questions.

Consequences

The released tapes included the June 1972 “smoking gun” conversation in which Nixon approved using the CIA to obstruct the FBI investigation. His political support collapsed, and he resigned on August 9, 1974.

Later interpretation

How later courts, scholars, and the public have judged the case. This is not part of the ruling itself.

The case established that the president is not above the law and that claims of executive privilege are subject to judicial review.
