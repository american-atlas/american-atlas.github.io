United States v. Nixon

The Supreme Court orders a president to surrender the Watergate tapes, 1974

1974 · Washington, D.C.

In United States v. Nixon, decided July 24, 1974, the Supreme Court unanimously rejected President Richard Nixon’s claim that “executive privilege” allowed him to withhold White House tape recordings subpoenaed for the trial of his aides in the Watergate cover-up.1 The Court recognized that presidents have a real but limited privilege to keep their conversations confidential. It held that a “generalized” claim of confidentiality must yield to the needs of a criminal trial.1 Among the tapes Nixon was forced to release was the June 23, 1972, “smoking gun,” which proved he had obstructed the FBI’s investigation. Within sixteen days he resigned.4,6

Why it matters

United States v. Nixon showed that the president is not above the law. A unanimous Court ordered him to produce the evidence that ended his presidency.1,4

Historical context

By 1974 the Watergate scandal had moved from the break-in at the Democratic National Committee to the White House itself. In July 1973 a former aide, Alexander Butterfield, had revealed that Nixon secretly recorded his Oval Office conversations.3 Nixon refused to turn the tapes over, arguing that the confidentiality of presidential conversations was at stake. In October 1973 he fired the first special prosecutor, Archibald Cox, in the “Saturday Night Massacre,” and public outrage forced him to release some tapes.3,2

On March 1, 1974, a federal grand jury indicted seven men, among them former Attorney General John Mitchell and the former White House aides H. R. Haldeman and John Ehrlichman, for conspiracy to defraud the United States and to obstruct justice.1,3 The grand jury also named the president as an “unindicted coconspirator.”1 On April 18 the new special prosecutor, Leon Jaworski, obtained a subpoena ordering Nixon to produce tapes and documents of precisely identified meetings, for use at the trial set for September.1 The White House instead released more than 1,250 pages of edited transcripts.3 Nixon’s lawyers moved to quash the subpoena. They claimed an absolute executive privilege and argued that a dispute inside the executive branch was not something a court could decide.1 When the district court ordered the materials turned over, both sides asked the Supreme Court to hear the case at once, and the justices agreed to hear it before the court of appeals ruled.1

The legal question

Does the president have an absolute privilege, rooted in the separation of powers, to withhold confidential communications from a judicial subpoena in a criminal case? And is the courts’ power to decide that question limited by the fact that the special prosecutor worked inside the executive branch?1

The ruling

The Court ruled 8 to 0 against the president. Justice William Rehnquist took no part, and Chief Justice Warren Burger wrote the opinion.1,4 The Court first held that the dispute was a real case the courts could hear, because the Attorney General had given the special prosecutor independent authority by regulation.1 It then held that the subpoena met the legal requirements and that the tapes probably contained evidence relevant to the charges.1 On the central question, it held that “neither the doctrine of separation of powers nor the generalized need for confidentiality of high-level communications, without more, can sustain an absolute, unqualified Presidential privilege of immunity from judicial process under all circumstances.”1 The president’s “generalized assertion of privilege must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial.” The district court was to review the tapes privately in chambers before passing them to the prosecution.1

The Court’s reasoning

Burger began with Marbury v. Madison. Each branch must interpret the Constitution in its own work, he wrote, but “it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is,” including on a president’s claim of privilege.1 The judicial power could no more be shared with the executive than the president could share his veto with the courts.1

The Court did not deny that a privilege existed. A president and his advisers “must be free to explore alternatives in the process of shaping policies and making decisions,” and the privilege was “fundamental to the operation of Government and inextricably rooted in the separation of powers.”1 But it was presumptive, not absolute. It had to be weighed against “our historic commitment to the rule of law” and the principle that “the public… has a right to every man’s evidence.”1 The Court noted that Nixon claimed no military, diplomatic, or sensitive national security secrets. He relied only on a general interest in confidentiality, and that interest would not be badly harmed by a private review by a judge.1 An absolute privilege, by contrast, “would plainly conflict with the function of the courts under Art. III.”1

Consequences

Nixon complied. The New York Times, quoted by the Miller Center, wrote that the decision “adds to the feeling that the last act of Richard Nixon’s drama is at hand.”3 Three days after the ruling, on July 27, the House Judiciary Committee approved the first of three articles of impeachment, for obstruction of justice.3 The released tapes included Nixon’s June 23, 1972, conversation with H. R. Haldeman, in which he agreed to have the CIA tell the FBI to stop its investigation of the break-in, falsely claiming it would expose CIA secrets.4 After the transcript was published, Republican leaders in Congress told Nixon they could not protect him from impeachment or conviction. Senator Barry Goldwater said there were at most eighteen senators who might vote to acquit.4,2 On August 8 Nixon announced his resignation, which took effect at noon the next day.6 Mitchell, Haldeman, and Ehrlichman were later convicted and sent to prison.4

Later interpretation

How later courts, scholars, and the public have judged the case. This is not part of the ruling itself.

The case remains the leading decision on executive privilege. It confirmed that presidents have a constitutionally based privilege of confidentiality, but that in a criminal case a general claim of confidentiality must give way to specific need. It left open how the balance would come out for military, diplomatic, or national security secrets.1 It also reaffirmed, in the words of Marbury, that the courts decide what the law is, even against a sitting president.1,5
