The Mexican-American War

The United States conquers half of Mexico, 1846–1848

May 13, 1846 – February 2, 1848 · Texas, northern Mexico, New Mexico, California, and central Mexico

Within a year of annexing Texas, the United States went to war with Mexico over a disputed border and President James K. Polk’s ambition to acquire California and the Southwest.1,3 American armies occupied New Mexico, California, and northern Mexico, landed at Veracruz, and captured Mexico City in September 1847.3 In the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, Mexico gave up more than 525,000 square miles, the National Archives says 55 percent of its territory.2 Many Americans opposed the war as an aggression, and the victory immediately reopened the question of whether slavery would spread into the new lands, pushing the country toward civil war.5,7

Why it matters

The Mexican-American War made the United States a continental nation at the cost of half of Mexico. It also made the expansion of slavery the question that would tear the Union apart.2,5

Who fought

The United States, under President James K. Polk, fought Mexico, whose government changed hands several times during the war.3,6 Mexican civilians and the Native nations of the borderlands, above all the Comanche, who had long raided northern Mexico, were caught up in the war, and the American Yawp sees the war partly as the culmination of that violence.5

Political background

Texas won its independence from Mexico in 1836. Mexico had invited American settlers into Texas in the 1820s, but they largely ignored its ban on slavery and its requirement that newcomers become Catholic. Mexico never recognized the new republic.5 For nearly a decade the United States hesitated to annex Texas, because Mexico threatened war and Northerners opposed adding a slave state.1,5 President John Tyler’s annexation treaty was defeated in the Senate in June 1844, but with the support of President-elect Polk, who had won on a platform of expansion, Congress passed a joint resolution for annexation on March 1, 1845. Texas accepted on July 4 and was admitted as a state on December 29, 1845.1,5 The sources date Mexico’s break in diplomatic relations differently: the State Department links it to the annexation treaty of April 1844, while Polk told Congress that relations were suspended in March 1845.1,6 Mexico called annexation “an act of aggression, the most unjust which can be found recorded in the annals of modern history.”5

The two countries also disagreed about Texas’s border. Mexico drew the border at the Nueces River, while Texans claimed the Rio Grande, roughly 150 miles farther west. The American Yawp notes that neither claim was realistic, because the strip between them was actually controlled by Native Americans.5,1 Polk wanted more than Texas. By the end of his term he meant for the United States to stretch from coast to coast, in possession of Oregon and California.3

Major phases

The road to war (1845–1846). In July 1845 Polk sent General Zachary Taylor’s army into Texas. The State Department says he was ordered into the disputed lands, while the Yawp places his 4,000 men at Corpus Christi, just northeast of the Nueces.1,5 In November Polk sent John Slidell to Mexico with an offer to buy the disputed border land along with New Mexico and California.1 The Mexican government, under pressure from opponents who had seized on the Texas question, refused to receive him, and a military revolt then brought General Paredes to power.6,3 The Yawp calls the mission an “empty gesture” to satisfy those who insisted on diplomacy before war.5 On January 13, 1846, Polk ordered Taylor to the Rio Grande.6 On April 24 Mexican cavalry attacked a detachment of Taylor’s troops north of the river and killed eleven Americans.5,3 Polk told Congress on May 11 that Mexico “has passed the boundary of the United States, has invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil.”6 Congress declared war on May 13. Only a few members, notably John Quincy Adams and John C. Calhoun, voted against it, and Congress called for 50,000 volunteers.5

Northern Mexico and the West (1846–1847). Taylor won at Palo Alto and Resaca de la Palma and took Monterrey in September 1846.3 General Stephen Watts Kearny marched from Fort Leavenworth and occupied Santa Fe on August 18, 1846. Part of his force went on to California, where American settlers under John C. Frémont had already declared an independent “Bear Flag” republic.3 Polk allowed the exiled General Antonio López de Santa Anna to return to Mexico after he promised to make peace for $30 million. Instead, Santa Anna took command of the Mexican army and became president.3 He attacked Taylor at Buena Vista on February 22, 1847, and was beaten.3

The road to Mexico City (1847). General Winfield Scott landed and captured the port of Veracruz in March 1847, then fought his way two hundred miles inland. The Miller Center notes that his army was outnumbered and cut off from its supply bases, and European observers predicted disaster. Scott’s army held Mexico City by September 14, 1847.3 Scott’s men occupied the capital for more than four months while the two governments negotiated.5

Peace (1847–1848). Polk sent Nicholas Trist, chief clerk of the State Department, to negotiate. Worried that Trist would not press hard enough and that he had grown close to Scott, a Whig presidential hopeful, Polk recalled him in October 1847.1 Some expansionist Democrats were now calling for the annexation of “All Mexico.”1,3 Trist ignored the recall. “I decided today at noon to attempt to make a treaty; the decision is altogether my own,” he wrote to his wife on December 4.2 He negotiated with Mexican commissioners Luis Gonzaga Cuevas, Bernardo Couto, and Miguel Atristain, and the treaty was signed on February 2, 1848. The National Archives places the signing at Guadalupe Hidalgo, where the Mexican government had fled, while the State Department says Mexico City.2,1

Turning points

  • The march to the Rio Grande (January 1846): Polk’s order sent Taylor into the land both nations claimed, which made a clash almost certain.6,5
  • Buena Vista (February 1847): Taylor’s victory over Santa Anna’s much larger army made him a Whig hero and a future president.3,4
  • Scott’s landing at Veracruz (March 1847): the amphibious landing opened the road to the Mexican capital.3
  • The fall of Mexico City (September 1847): led the Mexican government to negotiate.2

Outcome

Under the treaty, Mexico gave up all claims to Texas, recognized the Rio Grande as the border, and ceded the land that became California, Nevada, Utah, New Mexico, most of Arizona and Colorado, and parts of Oklahoma, Kansas, and Wyoming.2 The United States paid $15 million and took over debts the Mexican government owed to American citizens.2 Mexicans living in the ceded lands could move to Mexico or stay. Those who stayed and did not choose within a year to keep Mexican citizenship would become U.S. citizens.2

The sources describe the size of Mexico’s loss differently: the National Archives says 55 percent of its territory, the American Yawp roughly half, and the Miller Center over one-third.2,5,3 They also disagree on the Senate vote on March 10, 1848, which the National Archives gives as 34 to 14 and the Miller Center as 38 to 14. According to the Miller Center, half of the no votes came from Democrats who wanted more Mexican land and half from Whigs who wanted none.2,3 The Senate struck out Article X, which had guaranteed Mexican land grants.2

Consequences for American society

The war was costly. More than 13,000 Americans died, but only 2,053 from combat, and the rest died of disease. Mexico suffered nearly 50,000 casualties.3 The American Yawp writes that disease killed seven times as many American soldiers as combat; the Miller Center’s figures suggest a ratio closer to five to one.5,3 Harsh discipline and clashes with civilians led many volunteers to desert.5 The war was the first covered by large numbers of reporters, and the divided press fought over the news.3,5

At home it was bitterly controversial. Most Whigs opposed it. Abraham Lincoln, a first-term Whig congressman, condemned it as unconstitutional and challenged Polk to show him “the spot” where American blood had been shed.3 Ralph Waldo Emerson predicted: “We will conquer Mexico, but it will be as the man who swallows the arsenic which will bring him down in turn. Mexico will poison us.”5 On August 8, 1846, Representative David Wilmot proposed an amendment to a war appropriations bill that would have banned slavery in any territory acquired from Mexico.7,8 The House passed it, but the Senate did not vote before adjourning. The House passed it again in February 1847, and the Senate voted it down.8 The fight over the Wilmot Proviso divided Congress along sectional lines and helped lead to the Compromise of 1850 and the founding of the Republican Party.8

The new lands drew settlers and fortune seekers. On January 24, 1848, just before the treaty was signed, James W. Marshall found gold at John Sutter’s sawmill at Coloma, setting off the California Gold Rush.5,9 Mexicans in the ceded territories, stripped of the land-grant guarantees of Article X, faced competition for land and abuse alongside Native Americans.2,5 The war made Zachary Taylor president and trained many of the Civil War’s commanders.5,4 With the Gadsden Purchase, which the National Archives dates to 1853 and the Yawp to 1854, it set the southern border of the present-day United States.2,5
