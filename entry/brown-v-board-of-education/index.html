Brown v. Board of Education

The Supreme Court strikes down school segregation

1954 · Washington, D.C.

In Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka, decided May 17, 1954, a unanimous Supreme Court held that racial segregation in public schools violates the Fourteenth Amendment’s guarantee of equal protection of the laws.1 “Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal,” Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote, overturning the “separate but equal” doctrine of Plessy v. Ferguson in public education.1 The victory crowned decades of NAACP litigation led by Thurgood Marshall. But the Court’s vague order to desegregate “with all deliberate speed” met fierce resistance, and most Southern schools were not integrated until the late 1960s and early 1970s.1,4,5

Why it matters

Brown ended the constitutional legitimacy of legal segregation and gave the civil rights movement its foundation in law, even though its promise took decades to fulfill.1,4

Historical context

In 1896, in Plessy v. Ferguson, the Supreme Court had allowed states to segregate the races as long as facilities were “separate but equal.” In practice Black schools, especially in the South, received far less money, worse buildings, and older books.4 In Clarendon County, South Carolina, in 1950, taxpayers spent $179 on each white student and $43 on each Black student.4

Beginning in the 1930s, the NAACP’s lawyers, led first by Charles Hamilton Houston and then by his former student Thurgood Marshall, attacked segregation case by case.4,3 They first argued that states failed to provide even equal facilities, and they won cases opening graduate and law schools, such as Sweatt v. Painter (1950), which forced the University of Texas Law School to admit a Black student.3,4 In June 1950, after those victories, Marshall and the NAACP’s lawyers decided to stop asking for equal schools and to attack segregation itself.6

The case that bears the name Brown began in Topeka, Kansas, where a state law since 1879 had allowed larger cities to run separate elementary schools. In 1950 the Topeka NAACP organized a suit on behalf of thirteen families.2 Oliver Brown, the only father among the plaintiffs, became the lead name. His seven-year-old daughter Linda had to walk six blocks to catch a bus to the all-Black Monroe School a mile away, though a white school was much closer.2,6 A federal court ruled against the families in 1951, holding that Topeka’s Black and white schools were equal, but its presiding judge added a finding that segregation had a detrimental effect on Black children, which the NAACP used on appeal.2,6

The Supreme Court combined Brown with four other NAACP cases. Briggs v. Elliott came from Clarendon County, South Carolina, where Harry Briggs and other parents had petitioned for equal schools. Davis v. County School Board came from Prince Edward County, Virginia, where Black students had gone on strike over their overcrowded high school. The Delaware cases were brought by Louis Redding for Sarah Bulah and Ethel Belton. Bolling v. Sharpe came from Washington, D.C., where, because the city was federal territory, the lawyers relied on the Fifth Amendment instead of the Fourteenth.6,4,2 The NAACP chose cases from different places to show that segregation was not only a Deep South problem.4

The legal question

Does the segregation of children in public schools solely on the basis of race, even where the buildings and other “tangible” factors may be equal, deprive Black children of the equal protection of the laws guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment?1

The ruling

Yes. Speaking for a unanimous Court, Chief Justice Earl Warren held: “We conclude that, in the field of public education, the doctrine of ‘separate but equal’ has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.”1 The plaintiffs, he wrote, were “deprived of the equal protection of the laws guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment.”1 The Court postponed the question of how to carry out its ruling. A year later, on May 31, 1955, in a second decision known as Brown II, it ordered the states to begin desegregation plans “with all deliberate speed.”1

The Court’s reasoning

The Court first heard arguments in December 1952 (one National Park Service account says spring 1952).6,2 Unable to decide, it asked the lawyers to return and address whether the authors of the Fourteenth Amendment had intended to abolish school segregation, and what remedy should follow if the Court struck it down.6 The NAACP assembled historians, including John Hope Franklin and C. Vann Woodward, to research these questions.6 Opposing them was John W. Davis, a former Democratic presidential candidate and one of the nation’s most respected lawyers.6 In September 1953 Chief Justice Fred Vinson, who had stood in the way of a unanimous decision, died and was replaced by California governor Earl Warren. After the case was reargued in December 1953, Warren brought all nine justices to agreement.2,6

Warren found the historical evidence about the Fourteenth Amendment inconclusive and decided the case instead on the importance of education in modern life.4,1 “Today, education is perhaps the most important function of state and local governments,” he wrote; “it is the very foundation of good citizenship.”1 To separate Black children from others “solely because of their race generates a feeling of inferiority as to their status in the community that may affect their hearts and minds in a way unlikely ever to be undone.”1 To support that conclusion, the Court cited social science research. Among the studies was the work of the psychologists Kenneth and Mamie Clark, whose “doll tests” found that most Black children shown identical Black and white dolls preferred the white one. The Clarks concluded that prejudice and segregation had taught the children a sense of inferiority.6,4

Consequences

The decision, the National Park Service writes, eventually forced the desegregation of public schools in twenty-one states, but at first it met fierce resistance.2 The Court’s vague instruction to proceed “with all deliberate speed,” the American Yawp argues, “left the actual business of desegregation in the hands of those who opposed it.”4 In Virginia, Senator Harry F. Byrd Sr. led a campaign of “Massive Resistance” to block integration with new state laws.2 In 1957, when nine Black students tried to enter Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas, Governor Orval Faubus sent the National Guard to keep them out, and white mobs gathered. President Eisenhower, who never publicly endorsed the decision, sent soldiers of the 101st Airborne Division to protect the Little Rock Nine.5,6 In the fall of 1958 Faubus closed all four of Little Rock’s public high schools rather than integrate them, until the Supreme Court ordered them reopened in December 1959.5

Real integration came only after Congress acted. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 threatened to cut off federal funds to districts that refused to desegregate, and later court decisions ended evasions such as “freedom of choice” plans.4 The results were dramatic. In 1968, fourteen years after Brown, about 80 percent of Black schoolchildren in the South attended schools that were 90 to 100 percent nonwhite; by 1972 only 25 percent did.4

Later interpretation

How later courts, scholars, and the public have judged the case. This is not part of the ruling itself.

For Black Americans, the decision brought hope that they had “a friend” in the Supreme Court, and the civil rights movement broadened from lawsuits to the boycotts, sit-ins, and marches that followed.2 The American Yawp concludes that Brown’s significance lies “less in immediate tangible changes,” which were slow and partial, “than in the idealism it expressed and the momentum it created.”4 Critics at the time objected that the Court had relied on social science data rather than legal precedent, and advocates of judicial restraint accused it of writing new law. Supporters of judicial activism replied that the Court had rightly adapted the Constitution to new problems. Under Warren, the Court went on for fifteen years to reshape race relations, criminal justice, the political process, and the relationship between church and state.1 Thurgood Marshall, who argued the case, became the first African American justice of the Supreme Court in 1967.3
