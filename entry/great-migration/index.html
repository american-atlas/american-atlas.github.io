The Great Migration

Millions of Black Southerners move North and West, 1910s–1970s

c. 1910s – c. 1970s

The Great Migration was one of the largest movements of people in American history. From the 1910s to the 1970s about six million Black Southerners left the South for cities in the North, Midwest, and West.1 They fled racial violence and the oppression of Jim Crow and went looking for jobs, schooling, and freedom.1 The two world wars opened industrial jobs to them.1,2 The migrants transformed American cities, politics, and culture, from Chicago and Detroit to Harlem and Oakland. But in the North and West they also met mob violence, housing discrimination, and segregated neighborhoods that shaped inequality for generations.1,3,4

Why it matters

The Great Migration remade the map of Black America and the culture and politics of American cities. It showed millions of people choosing to leave a system of oppression, and it carried both the hopes and the conflicts of the South into the rest of the nation.1,3

Goals

Migrants sought what the South denied them. The National Archives describes their goals as escaping racial violence, pursuing economic and educational opportunity, and gaining freedom from the oppression of Jim Crow.1 The American Yawp writes that thousands of Black Southerners traveled north “to escape the traps of southern poverty.”2 In the 1940s, the Yawp adds, the 1.5 million who left the South were indirectly protesting racism and violence by leaving.5

Strategies

The first wave. When the United States entered World War I in 1917, men went to Europe and left industrial jobs empty. Immigration from Europe had fallen, and people of color from elsewhere in the world were barred by law, so Northern industry turned to Black Southerners for its labor.1,2 From World War I to World War II about two million Black Southerners moved to cities such as New York, Chicago, Detroit, and Pittsburgh.1

A city within a city. New York’s Black population grew 257 percent, from 91,709 in 1910 to 327,706 in 1930, while its white population grew only 20 percent.3 By 1930 Harlem had grown to 164,000 people, mostly African Americans, and nearly half of the 98,620 Black immigrants from abroad lived there as well.3

The second wave. World War II expanded defense industries and brought more jobs. Between 1940 and 1950 about 1.5 million Black Southerners left, the largest number in any decade of the migration.5,1 Many now went west, to Oakland, Los Angeles, and San Francisco, and to Portland and Seattle. Within twenty years of the war, about three million more had moved.1 The National Archives’ two figures, two million and three million, add up to less than its six-million total for the whole migration, a reminder that these numbers are estimates.1

Opposition

The migrants found jobs but not equality.1 White Northerners and returning veterans fought to take back jobs and neighborhoods from new Black arrivals.2 In the “Red Summer” of 1919, white mobs attacked Black neighborhoods in at least twenty-five cities, including Chicago and Washington, D.C. Black residents defended themselves, often with force.2 The National Archives traces that violence to white anger at the changing makeup of their communities.1 In 1943 riots broke out in Mobile, Beaumont, and Harlem, and the bloodiest, in Detroit, killed twenty-five Black and nine white Americans.5

After World War II, migrants met restrictive covenants and redlining. Banks marked Black neighborhoods with a red line on maps and limited loans there. The Federal Housing Administration and the Veterans Administration refused to back mortgages in those areas, and new suburbs such as Levittown sold only to white buyers.1,4 The National Archives calls these practices a foundation of the racial wealth gap that still exists.1

Achievements

The migration built powerful Black urban communities. In Harlem, which James Weldon Johnson called “the Culture Capital,” a generation energized by race pride, wartime service, and city life produced the Harlem Renaissance and the “New Negro” movement.3 Harlem writers published some twenty-six novels and ten volumes of poetry between 1922 and 1935.3 Marcus Garvey’s movement, the Yawp notes, built a large following and inspired later Black nationalism.3 During World War II the NAACP grew tenfold, from 50,000 to 500,000 members.5 The war, the Yawp writes, ignited in African Americans an urgency for equality that they carried into the years of the civil rights movement.5

Unresolved questions

Migrants escaped legal segregation in the South but met segregation in housing and jobs in the North and West. The National Archives notes that this laid a foundation for the racial disparities in wealth that persist in the United States.1,4
