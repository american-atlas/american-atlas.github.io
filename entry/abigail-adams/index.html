Abigail Adams

Political partner, letter writer, and early voice for women’s rights

1744–1818

Abigail Adams was the wife of the second president, John Adams, the mother of the sixth, John Quincy Adams, and one of the sharpest political minds of the Revolutionary generation.1,2 Through a lifetime of letters she advised her husband, reported on the war around Boston, and ran the family farm and finances alone for years at a time.1,3 In March 1776 she urged him to “Remember the Ladies” in the new nation’s laws, warning that women would “not hold ourselves bound by any Laws in which we have no voice, or Representation.”4 She also wrote against slavery and argued for women’s education.1,3 As First Lady she was so influential that critics called her “Mrs. President.”1

Why it matters

Abigail Adams’s letters are among the richest records of the Revolutionary era, and they show how a woman with no formal role shaped events through intelligence, management, and argument. Her “Remember the Ladies” letter is one of the earliest and best-known American appeals for women’s rights.1,3,4

Early life and context

Abigail Smith was born in Weymouth, Massachusetts, in November 1744, the second child of Elizabeth Quincy Smith and the Reverend William Smith, pastor of the town’s North Parish Congregational Church.1 The Park Service and the National Women’s History Museum give different days for her birth, November 11 and November 22.1,3 New England schools usually admitted only boys, so she was taught at home and educated herself in her father’s library, regretting all her life that formal education had been closed to her.1,3 Her grandfather Colonel John Quincy, once speaker of the Massachusetts House, gave her a sense of public duty.1

She married John Adams, a young lawyer from Braintree, on October 25, 1764, with her father presiding.1 They lived in the farmhouse John had inherited in Braintree, and their children were Abigail (“Nabby”), John Quincy, Susanna, who died in infancy, Charles, and Thomas Boylston.1 In Boston their circle included Samuel Adams, John Hancock, James Otis, and Joseph Warren. When John agreed to defend the British soldiers accused in the Boston Massacre of 1770, a decision that angered many patriots, she supported him.1

Major actions and achievements

Running the farm and the family. When John left for the First Continental Congress in 1774, Abigail stayed in Braintree to run the farm and teach their children, a role the National Women’s History Museum calls “deputy husband.”1,3 Married women then had few property rights, yet she began to call the family property her own, made profitable investments in war bonds, ran a small business importing European goods, and bought land her husband had not approved.3 The American Yawp describes her managing planting and harvest in the middle of severe labor shortages and inflation.5

Witness to the war. Her letters reported British and American movements around Boston. On June 17, 1775, she took her son John Quincy to the top of Penn’s Hill to watch the Battle of Bunker Hill.1

Abroad and in the capital. John Adams went to France in 1778 and stayed in Europe for most of the next decade.1 Abigail joined him and spent four years in France and England while he served as minister to Great Britain; the Park Service dates her stay from 1784 to 1788, while the National Women’s History Museum writes 1783 to 1788.1,3 During his vice presidency and presidency she moved between Braintree and the successive capitals of New York, Philadelphia, and Washington. In November 1800 she and John became the first residents of the Executive Mansion, today’s White House.1

Beliefs and ideas

“Remember the Ladies.” In a long letter written between March 31 and April 5, 1776, between news of Boston and an epidemic among her neighbors’ children, Abigail wrote: “I long to hear that you have declared an independency -- and by the way in the new Code of Laws which I suppose it will be necessary for you to make I desire you would Remember the Ladies, and be more generous and favourable to them than your ancestors. Do not put such unlimited power into the hands of the Husbands. Remember all Men would be tyrants if they could.”4 She used the patriots’ own argument against them: women, too, would refuse to obey laws “in which we have no voice, or Representation.”4 John replied that he could only laugh at her “extraordinary Code of Laws,” joking that “We know better than to repeal our Masculine systems.”6

Slavery. In the same letter she doubted that “the passion for Liberty cannot be Eaquelly Strong in the Breasts of those who have been accustomed to deprive their fellow Creatures of theirs.”4 Elsewhere she wished “that there was not a slave in the province.”1

Women’s education. She was among the first advocates of equal education for women and of women’s property rights.2 “If we mean to have Heroes, Statesmen and Philosophers,” she wrote, “we should have learned women.”3 Like many of her generation she embraced what historians call Republican Motherhood, the belief that mothers shaped the virtue of future citizens.3,7

Controversies, limits, and criticism

As First Lady, Abigail was widely believed to steer her husband’s decisions, and opponents mocked her as “Mrs. President.” The Park Service notes that she actually disagreed with his policy of neutrality toward France, but the belief that she set his policies weakened him politically.1 She supported the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798, which aimed to restrict foreign influence and to weaken the opposition press, because she felt the press was undermining her husband; the acts proved very unpopular and contributed to his defeat in 1800.1,3

Legacy

After the 1800 election the Adamses retired to Quincy, where Abigail advised her son John Quincy as he rose to be a senator, minister to Russia, and secretary of state.1 She died of typhoid fever on October 28, 1818. John wrote, “I wish I could lay down beside her and die too.”1 Her grandson Charles Francis Adams published her letters in 1848.3 The Park Service quotes President Harry Truman as saying she “would have been a better President than her husband.”1
