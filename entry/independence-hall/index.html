Independence Hall

Where the Declaration and the Constitution were debated and signed

Chestnut Street, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania

Built as the Pennsylvania State House, Independence Hall is where the Second Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence in 1776 and where the Constitutional Convention wrote the Constitution in 1787.1,3 The National Park Service calls it “the birthplace of America.”1 After the founding it became a shrine to the Revolution, visited by Lafayette in 1824 and by Abraham Lincoln in 1861, and today it is part of Independence National Historical Park and a UNESCO World Heritage Site.1,3

Why it matters

Few buildings anywhere have hosted as many founding political acts. Independence Hall is also a record of how Americans remember their founding: it was neglected, stripped, restored, and turned into a shrine, and generations from Lafayette’s hosts to Lincoln to the Park Service have used it to say what the Revolution meant.1,2,3

About the place

Independence Hall is a red-brick building in the Georgian style, which the Park Service says looks more like a country house than an urban public building.2 Its plan includes a 105-foot main block joined by covered arcades to two wing buildings, and its tower and steeple were added around 1750.2 The rotted wooden steeple was removed in 1781, and in 1828 the architect William Strickland built a new one with a clock.2 Inside, the Assembly Room has been restored with tables for each colony, though no one knows exactly how the delegates were seated.3

History

The State House. Speaker Andrew Hamilton oversaw construction, and the master builder Edmund Woolley drew the designs.2 Work began in 1732. The Pennsylvania Assembly was already meeting there in 1735, and the main building was finished in 1748.2 It housed all three branches of Pennsylvania’s colonial government.1

The Second Continental Congress. In May 1775, a month after the fighting at Lexington and Concord, the Pennsylvania legislature lent its Assembly Room to the Second Continental Congress.3 There the delegates approved George Washington as commander in chief, voted to create the Marine Corps, and drafted the Olive Branch Petition to the king, which went unanswered.3 On July 2, 1776, they voted for independence, and on July 4 they approved the Declaration of Independence. The Declaration was signed in the same room on August 2.3 The Articles of Confederation officially took effect there in 1781.1

The Constitutional Convention. In May 1787 the legislature lent the room again, this time to delegates from twelve states who came to repair the Articles of Confederation.3,5 Their debates over the balance between large and small states and over the slave trade were sometimes heated.3 In four months they wrote a new Constitution, which Washington called “little short of a miracle.”3 As the delegates signed, Benjamin Franklin looked at the sun carved on the back of Washington’s chair and said he was happy to know that it was “a rising and not a setting sun.”3

From State House to shrine. Congress met next door in Congress Hall from 1790 to 1800, while Philadelphia was the temporary capital.4 The state government moved away in 1799, and in 1818 Pennsylvania sold the building to the city.3 When the Marquis de Lafayette was welcomed in the Assembly Room in 1824, his hosts called it “the hall of Independence”; decades passed before the whole building took that name.3 The city had removed the room’s original paneling, which angered the public, and in 1831 the architect John Haviland was hired to restore it.2 A redecoration in 1854–55 made the room a shrine, at a time when the nativist movement was using Revolutionary symbols in its campaign to keep immigrants out of public office.3 The building was also the scene of fugitive slave hearings.1

Lincoln. On his way to his inauguration in 1861, President-elect Abraham Lincoln stood in the Assembly Room and said he would rather be “assassinated on the spot” than give up the ideals of the Declaration of Independence.3 Four years later his body lay there for two days after his assassination, and upwards of 85,000 mourners filed past.3
