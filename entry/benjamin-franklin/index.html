Benjamin Franklin

Printer, scientist, diplomat, and the Revolution’s elder statesman

1706–1790

Benjamin Franklin rose from a Boston candlemaker’s son to become the best-known American of the eighteenth century, as a printer, writer, scientist, and statesman.2,3 In Philadelphia he published Poor Richard’s Almanack and founded a library, a fire company, a hospital, and the academy that became the University of Pennsylvania.1,2 His experiments with electricity won him international fame.12 He represented the colonies in London for nearly two decades, helped draft the Declaration of Independence, won the French alliance, and helped negotiate the peace with Britain.1,3,9 At eighty-one he was the oldest delegate to the Constitutional Convention. An enslaver earlier in life, he ended it as president of an abolition society.15,16,5

Why it matters

Franklin signed the three documents that established the nation, the Declaration of Independence, the Treaty of Paris, and the Constitution, and the State Department’s historians call him the most distinguished scientific and literary American of the colonial era.1,3 His life became a model of the self-made American, and his civic projects, scientific work, and diplomacy helped shape both colonial society and the new nation.1,12,3

Early life and context

Benjamin Franklin was born in Boston on January 17, 1706, to Josiah Franklin, a maker of candles and soap, and Abiah Folger. Lehigh University’s exhibit calls him the tenth child, and the Library of Congress the tenth son.2,7 He had only about two years of schooling. At ten he went to work in his father’s shop. He was then apprenticed to his brother James, a printer who started the New England Courant, one of the first American newspapers. Lehigh puts the start of the apprenticeship at age thirteen, while the National Park Service dates it from 1718, when he was twelve.2,1 (The Library of Congress exhibit names the brother as a half-brother, Peter. The National Park Service and Lehigh both name James.)7,1,2 He taught himself by reading widely, and in 1722 he began writing for his brother’s paper under the pen name “Silence Dogood.”1

In 1723 Franklin struck out on his own and went to Philadelphia, where he found work as a journeyman printer.7,2 By 1730 he had his own printing shop and owned the Pennsylvania Gazette. He had a son, William, and had married Deborah Read Rogers.7,2 In 1732 he began Poor Richard’s Almanack, written under the name Richard Saunders, which became one of the colonies’ best-sellers for twenty-five years, often selling more than 10,000 copies.1,2,13 He became the official printer for several colonies and printed their paper money, using mica and leaf imprints to foil counterfeiters.1,13 He also launched printing partnerships from South Carolina to New York and the West Indies.1,2

Franklin turned his energy to civic improvement. He founded the Library Company, the first successful lending library in the colonies (1731), the Union Fire Company (1736), the American Philosophical Society, Pennsylvania Hospital (1751), and the Philadelphia Contributionship, the colonies’ first insurance company (1752). He pressed for paved, lighted, and patrolled streets.1,13 He also founded the academy that became the University of Pennsylvania, dated 1749 by the National Park Service and 1751 by Lehigh.1,2 As postmaster of Philadelphia and, from 1753, deputy postmaster general for North America, he improved mail routes across the colonies.1

Major actions and achievements

Scientist and inventor. Franklin’s electrical experiments, published as Experiments and Observations on Electricity, set out a theory of positive and negative charge, argued that lightning was electrical, and proposed a tall, grounded rod to protect buildings. In 1753 the Royal Society of London awarded him its Copley Medal.12,1 He also designed a more efficient wood stove, bifocal spectacles, and the glass armonica, for which Mozart and Beethoven composed music.2,12,1 He charted the Gulf Stream, studied storms and the northern lights, and wrote on the causes of the common cold.1,12 At his death the French statesman Turgot wrote that “he seized the lightning from the sky and the scepter from the hand of tyrants.”12

Uniting the colonies. In 1754, before the French and Indian War, Franklin proposed the Albany Plan to unite the colonies for common defense. He printed the woodcut “Join, or Die,” showing the colonies as a snake cut into pieces, which later became a symbol of the Revolution.7,1 Congress did not act on a plan of confederation he presented in July 1775, but the National Park Service credits his 1754 plan with laying the groundwork for the Articles of Confederation.9,1

Agent in London. Franklin went to London in 1757 as agent of the Pennsylvania assembly and stayed, apart from one brief return, until 1775, eventually speaking for several colonies.8,1 He briefly accepted the Stamp Act of 1765 but turned against it when he learned of the anger at home, and he helped win its repeal in 1766.7,1 On January 29, 1774, he was publicly attacked before the Privy Council, largely for obtaining copies of Massachusetts governor Thomas Hutchinson’s letters, which advised restricting the colonists’ liberties. In London, the Library of Congress writes, he gradually moved from loyalty to the British Empire to revolution.8

Revolutionary and diplomat. Back in Philadelphia in May 1775, Franklin joined the Second Continental Congress and served on the committee that drafted the Declaration of Independence.9,1 In 1776 Congress sent him to France as one of its commissioners. The French embraced him as the embodiment of New World Enlightenment, and his image appeared on medallions, rings, and snuffboxes. His popularity and skill, together with the American victory at Saratoga, helped bring France into an alliance with the United States in 1778.3,10 In 1779 he became the first American minister received by a foreign government, and he helped negotiate the Treaty of Paris of 1783, which ended the war.3,1,10,9 When Jefferson replaced him in 1785, Jefferson told the French foreign minister, “No one can replace him, Sir; I am only his successor.”3

The Constitution. Back home, Franklin served as president of Pennsylvania from 1785 to 1788.1 At the Constitutional Convention of 1787 he was the oldest delegate. When the debates grew bitter he proposed daily prayers, and he worked to restore harmony.15,11 On the last day, September 17, his colleague James Wilson read his speech urging the delegates to sign despite their doubts, so that the new government would “astonish our enemies.”15

Beliefs and ideas

Franklin believed in self-improvement, hard work, thrift, and practical knowledge put to public use, values spread through Poor Richard’s Almanack and his many civic projects.1,13 He was a man of the Enlightenment who prized scientific inquiry and the free exchange of ideas. Through the American Philosophical Society he kept scientific correspondence going across the Atlantic even during the Revolution.13,3 In politics he favored union among the colonies and later among the states, and he called the principle that colonists could be taxed only by their own elected representatives the “prime Maxim of all free Government.”17 He had a sense of humor to match. In a 1784 letter to his daughter he complained that the bald eagle on a new society’s emblem looked more like a turkey, “a much more respectable Bird.”11

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Franklin’s household held enslaved people for decades. Benjamin Franklin House records that the family bought at least seven people, among them Peter, Jemima, King, Othello, and George, and that Franklin’s Pennsylvania Gazette profited from advertisements for the sale of enslaved people and for runaways.5,16 When he went to London in 1757 he took two enslaved men, Peter and King. King escaped in 1758.5,4 The sources disagree about the end of his slaveholding. The National Park Service says he no longer owned any enslaved people when he died, while Benjamin Franklin House says the household held enslaved people until 1790.4,5

His views changed over time. In London he came under the influence of the Quaker abolitionist Anthony Benezet and of the 1772 Somerset decision against slavery in England, and he began to attack slavery anonymously.5,11 He kept publicly silent on the issue while in France. In 1787 he became president of the Pennsylvania Society for Promoting the Abolition of Slavery.5,16 The sources date that society’s founding to April 14, 1775 (National Archives and the Constitution Center) or 1774 (Benjamin Franklin House).16,6,5 His last public act was to sign, on February 3, 1790, the society’s petition to the First Congress asking it to “devise means for removing the Inconsistency from the Character of the American People” and end the slave trade. The House sent it to a committee, which reported that the Constitution barred Congress from prohibiting the importation or emancipation of slaves before 1808, and then tabled it. The Senate took no action.16,6

Legacy

Franklin died in Philadelphia on April 17, 1790, at his house in Franklin Court, and was buried in the graveyard of Christ Church.2,4 His will left funds in trust to lend money to young tradesmen. Two trade schools, the Franklin Union in Boston and the Franklin Institute in Philadelphia, were later founded with the money, and in 1990, as he had directed, the remaining funds, then worth more than $7 million, were distributed to schools and scholarships.14 His house was torn down in 1812, and a steel “ghost structure” now marks its outline above the Benjamin Franklin Museum.4 In Paris fashionable ladies copied the fur cap he wore instead of a wig, and his image, the Library of Congress notes, introduced him to France in 1777; it has remained one of the most familiar faces of the founding era.3,13
