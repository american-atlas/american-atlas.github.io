Abolitionism

The movement to end slavery in the United States

1775–1865

Abolitionism was the movement to end slavery in the United States. Early antislavery reformers, many of them Quakers, worked for gradual emancipation and an end to the slave trade.1,4 In the 1830s a new generation, led by Black activists and white evangelicals such as William Lloyd Garrison, demanded immediate, uncompensated emancipation and equal rights for Black Americans.1,2 A small and often hated minority, abolitionists used newspapers, lectures, petitions, and the Underground Railroad to make slavery the central moral question of American politics.1,5 Their cause triumphed only through the Civil War and the Thirteenth Amendment.1,4

Why it matters

Abolitionists turned slavery from an accepted institution into the great moral issue of American politics. Their agitation helped bring about the political crisis of the 1850s, and the Civil War that followed ended slavery.1

Goals

Early antislavery reformers sought gradual emancipation and an end to the slave trade. Many white reformers also supported “colonization,” the plan to send free Black Americans to Africa.1 Most Black Americans rejected colonization, insisting that America, not Africa, was their only homeland.2 From the 1830s the movement’s radical wing, following the lead of Black activists, demanded immediate, uncompensated emancipation, political and social equality, and citizenship for Black Americans.2,1 The American Anti-Slavery Society rested its cause “upon the Declaration of our Independence, and upon the truths of Divine Revelation,” tying abolition to both national and Christian redemption.1

Strategies

Petitions and early societies. Quakers questioned slavery as early as the late seventeenth century and worked with British reformers against the slave trade.1 In February 1790, Benjamin Franklin, as president of the Pennsylvania Society for Promoting the Abolition of Slavery, signed a petition asking the First Congress to “devise means for removing the Inconsistency from the Character of the American People.” Congress tabled it, citing the Constitution’s protection of the slave trade until 1808.4 After the Revolution most Northern states adopted gradual emancipation laws.1

Immediatism. The Second Great Awakening convinced many evangelical Protestants that slavery was the most God-defying of sins.1 Black writers led the way. In his 1829 Appeal to the Colored Citizens of the World, David Walker exposed the hypocrisy of American claims to freedom and Christianity, attacked colonization, and warned that God’s justice would bring violence on the slaveholding nation.1 Walker and James Forten convinced William Lloyd Garrison, who had supported colonization and gradual abolition, that colonization was racist.1,2 In 1831 Garrison launched The Liberator in Boston, declaring in its opening editorial: “I am in earnest—I will not equivocate—I will not excuse—I will not retreat a single inch—AND I WILL BE HEARD.”3 He published it every week for thirty-five years.2 In 1833 reformers from ten states founded the American Anti-Slavery Society, which had about 2,000 chapters and nearly 200,000 members by 1840.1,3 Its strategy was “moral suasion”: convincing enslavers through appeals to conscience to free the people they held.1

Words and images. Using new steam presses, abolitionists flooded the free states with pamphlets and newspapers and spoke from lecture platforms across the North.1 In 1835 they mailed antislavery literature to Southern enslavers, and in 1836 they sent thousands of petitions to Congress.1 Slave narratives exposed the cruelty of bondage. Frederick Douglass’s 1845 Narrative was reprinted in nine editions and translated into several languages, and his tour of Britain won support across the Atlantic.1,6

Women in the movement. In the 1830s women formed antislavery societies in Boston, New York, and Philadelphia, raised funds, and used their right to petition. The Grimké sisters, Angelina and Sarah, daughters of a South Carolina slaveholding family, lectured to mixed audiences of men and women. When they were attacked for speaking in public, they began to argue for women’s rights as well.1 When the World Anti-Slavery Convention in London refused in 1840 to seat women delegates, Lucretia Mott and Elizabeth Cady Stanton resolved to fight for women’s rights, a resolve that led to the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848.1

Politics. In 1839 abolitionists split. Garrison and his followers held that the Constitution was a proslavery document and refused to join party politics. Others, arguing that the Constitution could be used against slavery, founded the Liberty Party under James G. Birney.1 Douglass, once Garrison’s ally, broke with him in 1851 and supported political action.6 Their work helped prepare the ground for the antislavery Republican Party of the 1850s.1

Resistance and escape. In the 1840s and 1850s abolitionists increasingly helped freedom seekers escape through networks known as the Underground Railroad, to the North, Canada, Mexico, and beyond, a form of civil disobedience by people of all races.5,1 After the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, armed crowds in Northern cities tried to rescue people seized under the law.1 A few, like John Brown, turned to violence; his raid on Harpers Ferry in 1859 convinced many Americans that slavery was pushing the nation to the brink.1

Opposition

Abolitionists were a small minority, detested by most white Americans in the North as well as the South.1 Critics attacked them as fanatics who would tear the Union apart, and many were outraged that women spoke in public and that Black and white activists worked together.1 Northern mobs smashed abolitionist printing presses and killed the antislavery editor Elijah Lovejoy. In 1838 a mob burned the newly built Pennsylvania Hall, where the Anti-Slavery Convention of American Women was meeting.1 Southern states, blaming abolitionists for Nat Turner’s rebellion of 1831, purged antislavery voices from the region.1 In Congress, Whigs and Democrats joined in 1836 to pass the “gag rule,” which blocked all discussion of antislavery petitions in the House.1 Even within the movement, conflict was bitter. In 1840, when Abby Kelley was elected to the American Anti-Slavery Society’s business committee, opponents of women’s leadership left to form a rival society.1

Achievements

Abolitionists made slavery impossible to ignore.1 Their example of Black and white men and women working together offered, the American Yawp writes, “a powerful, if imperfect, model of interracial coexistence.”1 Although immediatists remained a minority, they prepared the ground for the antislavery Republican Party, and it is hard to imagine Abraham Lincoln’s election in 1860 without them, both as allies and as radicals against whom he could appear moderate.1 The movement trained a generation of women activists and gave birth to the women’s rights movement.1 During the Civil War, the Women’s Loyal National League, led by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony, gathered one of the largest petition drives in American history for an amendment abolishing slavery; Senator Charles Sumner presented its first 100,000 signatures in February 1864.4 Congress passed the Thirteenth Amendment in January 1865, and it was ratified in December. Garrison published the last issue of The Liberator on December 29, 1865.4,3

Unresolved questions

Abolitionists disagreed about tactics, whether moral persuasion, politics, or force; about whether the Constitution was proslavery; and about women’s place in the movement.1 Many white abolitionists also shared the racial prejudices of their time. Even Harriet Beecher Stowe’s Uncle Tom’s Cabin, which did so much for the cause, reinforced racist stereotypes.7 Most important, the end of slavery did not bring the equality many abolitionists had demanded. Garrison himself kept campaigning; in 1873 he declared, “I am still for immediate, unconditional, everlasting emancipation from oppression of everyone on the face of the earth.”2 That struggle continued through Reconstruction and long after.
