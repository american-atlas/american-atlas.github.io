The Vietnam War

America’s longest war of the Cold War era, 1964–1973

August 7, 1964 – January 1973 · Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia

The Vietnam War was a long struggle over the future of Vietnam in which the United States fought to keep a non-communist South Vietnam from being reunited with the communist North.1,4 It grew out of Vietnam’s war for independence from France and the American belief that if one country fell to communism, its neighbors would follow.1,6 American involvement deepened from advisers in the 1950s to more than 500,000 troops by 1968.9 Unable to win, the United States withdrew under the Paris Peace Accords of January 1973, and in April 1975 North Vietnamese forces took Saigon and reunified the country.3 The war killed 58,220 Americans, divided American society, and shook Americans’ trust in their government.5,2

Why it matters

The Vietnam War was a defeat for American Cold War policy and a turning point in American history. It divided a generation, undermined faith in government, and made Americans wary of foreign wars for decades.2,1

Who fought

The United States and South Vietnam (the Republic of Vietnam), with smaller allied contingents, fought North Vietnam (the Democratic Republic of Vietnam) and the Viet Cong, communist guerrillas in the South.4,9 North Vietnam was led by Ho Chi Minh and received material support from the Soviet Union and China.6,3 The fighting spread into neighboring Laos and Cambodia.3,2 The Miller Center suggests seeing the conflict through three lenses: a civil war within the South, a war of reunification waged by the North, and, in Washington’s eyes, a front in the global struggle against communism.9

Political background

Vietnam had been ruled by France since the nineteenth century and was occupied by Japan during World War II.6 In 1945 the nationalist leader Ho Chi Minh declared Vietnam’s independence, quoting the American Declaration of Independence. He had earlier asked the United States for help, and the State Department’s historians note that, despite his communist leanings, he was not at first anti-American.1,6 But France fought to restore its colony, and after the communist victory in China in 1949 the United States helped pay for the French war effort with funds, arms, and advisers.8,6 President Eisenhower explained American fears with the image of falling dominoes: if one country fell to communism, the rest would follow.6

On May 7, 1954, after a four-month siege, the French garrison at Dien Bien Phu fell to Ho Chi Minh’s Viet Minh forces.6 The Geneva Conference that followed divided Vietnam temporarily at the seventeenth parallel and called for elections in 1956 to reunite the country.6,9 The communists seemed likely to win, so the U.S.-backed leader of the South, Ngo Dinh Diem, refused to hold the vote, with American support.4,9 By 1959 the Viet Cong, aided by the North, had begun a large-scale insurgency in the South.4 Diem’s government was corrupt and unpopular; in November 1963 his own generals overthrew and killed him with the tacit approval of the Kennedy administration. By then about 16,000 American military advisers were serving in South Vietnam.4,9,8

Major phases

Escalation (1964–1965). In August 1964 two American destroyers reported attacks by North Vietnamese boats in the Gulf of Tonkin, on August 2 and again on August 4. The first attack happened, but a National Security Agency study released in 2007 concluded that the second never did.7,4 The Maddox had been supporting secret South Vietnamese raids on the North, a fact the public did not know.7 Johnson presented the incidents as unprovoked aggression, and on August 7 Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, authorizing him “to take all necessary measures to repel any armed attack.” Only two senators, Wayne Morse and Ernest Gruening, voted no.7 The resolution became the legal basis for the war.4,7 On February 13, 1965, Johnson began Operation Rolling Thunder, a bombing campaign against the North, and on March 8 two battalions of Marines, 3,500 men, landed near Da Nang.9,4 By November 1965 more than 175,000 American troops were in Vietnam.9

The ground war (1965–1968). Under General William Westmoreland, American forces fought both the Viet Cong and the regular North Vietnamese Army. Progress was measured not by territory taken but by “body counts.”8 Johnson chose a “middle way,” rejecting calls to invade the North or threaten nuclear weapons for fear of drawing in China or the Soviet Union, but Hanoi was willing to absorb the bombing and keep fighting indefinitely.9 Officials kept announcing progress, but reporters in Saigon called the daily military briefings “the Five o’Clock Follies.”2 Television brought the war into American homes, making it the first “living room war.”2 By the end of 1968, 536,000 Americans were in Vietnam and more than 30,000 had died.9

Tet (1968). In January 1968 communist forces attacked more than one hundred American and South Vietnamese sites across South Vietnam, including the American embassy in Saigon. American and South Vietnamese forces threw back the Tet Offensive and inflicted heavy losses on the Viet Cong, but the attacks showed that the enemy could still strike anywhere after years of war.2 The gap between official optimism and reality, the “credibility gap,” became impossible to ignore.2 On March 31, 1968, Johnson announced that he would not seek reelection.2

Vietnamization (1969–1973). President Richard Nixon promised “peace with honor.” He gradually withdrew American troops while building up South Vietnam’s army, a policy called “Vietnamization,” and pursued secret talks in Paris, where Henry Kissinger met North Vietnam’s Le Duc Tho.3,2 At the same time he secretly bombed North Vietnamese bases in Cambodia, and in April 1970 he sent American and South Vietnamese forces into Cambodia.3 After North Vietnam’s Easter Offensive in 1972, Nixon mined the North’s harbors and launched heavy bombing. Kissinger and Le Duc Tho reached an agreement in October, but South Vietnam’s President Nguyen Van Thieu rejected it, and in December Nixon ordered the “Christmas Bombing” of the North.3 The Paris Peace Accords were signed on January 27, 1973, allowing the United States to withdraw and bring its prisoners of war home.3

The fall of Saigon (1975). Neither Vietnamese side honored the agreement, and the war went on. Congress was unwilling to send American forces back, and the Watergate scandal weakened Nixon.3 In March 1975 North Vietnam launched a major offensive, and on April 30 its tanks rolled through the gates of the Presidential Palace in Saigon. Vietnam was united under a communist government.3,2

Turning points

  • Dien Bien Phu (1954): the French defeat led to the partition of Vietnam and deepening American involvement.6
  • The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964): gave Johnson a free hand to escalate the war.7
  • The Tet Offensive (1968): a military setback for the communists but a political turning point that turned many Americans against the war.2
  • Johnson’s withdrawal from the 1968 race: marked the end of escalation.2
  • The Paris Peace Accords (1973): ended direct American participation.3

Outcome

North Vietnam won. After the American withdrawal in 1973, the war continued until North Vietnamese forces captured Saigon on April 30, 1975, and Vietnam was reunited under a communist government.3,2 For the world’s greatest military power, the American Yawp observes, it was a stunning failure.2

Consequences for American society

The war tore American society apart. As the draft took young men to Vietnam, protesters burned draft cards, occupied buildings, and filled the streets; by 1967 antiwar demonstrations drew hundreds of thousands.2 Revelations deepened the anger. In 1969 the journalist Seymour Hersh reported that American soldiers had massacred hundreds of civilians at the village of My Lai.2 When Nixon sent troops into Cambodia in 1970, campus protests erupted. Police and National Guard troops killed six students, four of them at Kent State University in Ohio on May 4 and two at Jackson State in Mississippi.3,2 In 1971 the Pentagon Papers, a secret Defense Department history leaked to the press, showed how presidents from Truman to Johnson had misled the public about the war.2 Many other Americans, however, still regarded the war as just and resented the protesters, and Nixon appealed to this “silent majority.”2,3

The war also drained government finances and cut short the promise of Johnson’s Great Society.9,2 Congress tried to rein in presidential war-making. It repealed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution in 1971, and in 1973 it passed the War Powers Resolution, which limited the president’s power to wage war without congressional consent.7,2 Above all, the war, together with Watergate, left many Americans deeply distrustful of their government and its role in the world.2
