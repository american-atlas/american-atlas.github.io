The Presidency

The executive branch and the office of president

1789 · The White House, Washington, D.C.

Article II of the Constitution vests “the executive Power” in a president, elected for a four-year term. The president is head of state, leader of the executive branch, and commander in chief of the armed forces, and must “take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed.” From George Washington’s precedents to Franklin D. Roosevelt’s transformation of the office, the presidency has grown into the most powerful position in American government, checked by Congress, the courts, and the voters.

Key facts

Date
1789
Location
The White House, Washington, D.C.
Constitutional basis
Article II
Term
Four years; limited to two elected terms by the Twenty-second Amendment (1951)
Powers
Commander in chief; makes treaties with two-thirds Senate approval; appoints judges and officers with Senate consent; may veto legislation
Removal
Impeachment by the House and conviction by the Senate for “Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors”
Succession
The vice president becomes president if the president cannot serve

Why it matters

The presidency has become the center of American government and politics, embodying the nation at home and abroad — and the tension between energetic leadership and constitutional limits.

Origins

The Constitutional Convention of 1787 replaced the Articles of Confederation with a Constitution that created a single executive — a president chosen by electors from each state for a four-year term, together with a vice president. George Washington became the first president in 1789.

Powers and limits

Article II makes the president “Commander in Chief of the Army and Navy” and gives the power, “by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate,” to make treaties, “provided two thirds of the Senators present concur,” and to appoint ambassadors, judges, and other officers. The president may veto bills, which Congress can override by a two-thirds vote of each house. The president is supported by the vice president and the cabinet, whose members require Senate approval. Congress may remove a president through impeachment and conviction.

How it changed over time

The office expanded in times of crisis. Abraham Lincoln used war powers to preserve the Union and issue the Emancipation Proclamation. Theodore Roosevelt and Woodrow Wilson made the president a leader of reform and of world affairs. Franklin D. Roosevelt, who won four elections, made the president a chief legislator, built the modern White House staff, and used radio fireside chats to speak directly to citizens; the Twenty-second Amendment later limited presidents to two terms. The Vietnam War and Watergate brought a backlash: Congress passed the War Powers Resolution in 1973, and in United States v. Nixon the Supreme Court held that a president must obey a subpoena; Richard Nixon became the only president to resign.
