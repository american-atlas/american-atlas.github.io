The Presidency

The executive branch, from Washington’s precedents to the modern office

1789 · The White House, Washington, D.C.

Article II of the Constitution vests “the executive Power” in a president elected for four years, who commands the armed forces and must “take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed.”1 The framers argued for months over how to choose such an officer and how much power to give him.2 George Washington shaped the office by his example: he created the cabinet and stepped down after two terms.4 Crises later expanded it, above all under Abraham Lincoln and Franklin D. Roosevelt, until it became the most powerful position in American government, still checked by Congress, the courts and the voters.5,6,9

Why it matters

The presidency has become the center of American government and politics, embodying the nation at home and abroad. Its history is a long tension between Hamilton’s “energy in the executive” and the founders’ fear of a king.3,5,8

Origins

Under the Articles of Confederation there was no national executive at all. At the Constitutional Convention of 1787 the delegates struggled to design one. They considered choosing the president by Congress, by the people, by the state governors, by electors, and even by lottery.2 Late in the convention they settled on electors chosen in each state, a compromise that, according to the House historians, “preserved states’ rights, increased the independence of the executive branch, and avoided popular election.”2

Defending the plan in Federalist No. 70, Alexander Hamilton argued that “Energy in the Executive is a leading character in the definition of good government,” and that its ingredients were “first, unity; secondly, duration; thirdly, an adequate provision for its support; fourthly, competent powers.”3 The Miller Center adds that the delegates could create such an office partly because everyone expected Washington to fill it: Americans “knew they could trust him to wield immense power because he had already done so once.”4

Powers and limits

Article II makes the president “Commander in Chief of the Army and Navy” and gives the power, “by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate,” to make treaties, “provided two thirds of the Senators present concur,” and to appoint ambassadors, judges and other officers.1 The president may veto bills, which Congress can override by a two-thirds vote of each house, and can be removed through impeachment by the House and conviction by the Senate.1,9 The president is supported by the vice president and the cabinet, whose members require Senate approval.9

How it changed over time

Washington’s precedents. “I walk on untrodden ground,” Washington wrote. “There is scarcely any part of my conduct which may not hereafter be drawn into precedent.”10 He set those precedents deliberately. Every president since has worked with a cabinet, and every one until Franklin Roosevelt followed his example of retiring after two terms.4

The first contested elections. The original rules had electors cast two undifferentiated votes. In 1796 this made the rivals John Adams and Thomas Jefferson president and vice president; in 1800 Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr tied, and the House needed six days and 36 ballots to choose Jefferson. The crisis led to the Twelfth Amendment.2

Lincoln’s war powers. The Miller Center calls Lincoln “the most activist President in history.” After Fort Sumter he called out the militias, expanded the army and navy, spent $2 million without an appropriation from Congress, blockaded Southern ports, suspended habeas corpus in several places, and issued the Emancipation Proclamation.5

Roosevelt’s modern presidency. Franklin D. Roosevelt served from March 1933 to April 1945, the longest tenure in American history. Through radio fireside chats he built a direct bond with the public; he became chief legislator as well as chief executive, and the Executive Reorganization bill of 1939 gave the White House a permanent professional staff.6 The Twenty-second Amendment, passed by Congress in 1947 and ratified in 1951, then limited presidents to two elected terms.7

Backlash and limits. After the Vietnam War, Congress passed the War Powers Resolution of 1973. During Watergate, Richard Nixon cited executive privilege to withhold his tape recordings; the House Judiciary Committee approved articles of impeachment in July 1974, and Nixon resigned before the full House could vote, the only president ever to do so.8

Customs as well as laws

The Miller Center stresses how much of the presidency rests on custom rather than law. Washington retired on purpose, it writes, because he “recognized the structural importance of leaving office willingly”: Americans needed to learn how to elect, transfer power to and inaugurate a new president, and that had to start with his example.4 The two-term tradition he began lasted nearly 150 years before Roosevelt broke it and the Twenty-second Amendment wrote it into the Constitution.4,7
