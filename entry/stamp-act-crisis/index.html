The Stamp Act Crisis

The first great colonial protest against taxation by Parliament

March 1765 – March 1766 · British North American colonies; London

In March 1765 Parliament passed the Stamp Act, which required newspapers, legal documents, diplomas, and even playing cards in the colonies to be printed on stamped paper showing a tax had been paid.1,2 It was the first direct tax Parliament had ever laid on the colonists.1 Colonists answered with resolutions, a congress of nine colonies, boycotts of British goods, and crowd violence, until no one would serve as a stamp distributor.1 Parliament repealed the act early in 1766 but declared its power to bind the colonies “in all cases whatsoever.”1,4 The crisis gave colonists the argument, “no taxation without representation,” and the methods of resistance that would carry them toward independence.1,3

Why it matters

The Stamp Act crisis was the moment colonists first acted together against Parliament and spelled out the principle, no taxation without representation, that would lead them to independence. Parliament’s answer, that it could bind them “in all cases whatsoever,” defined the conflict that followed.1,3,4

Historical background

Britain won the Seven Years’ War but the war doubled the national debt to 13.5 times Britain’s annual revenue, and it now faced the cost of securing and defending a much larger empire.1 King George III, crowned in 1760, brought into government men who, the American Yawp writes, held “an authoritarian vision of empire in which colonies would be subordinate.”1 The Proclamation of 1763 forbade settlement west of the Appalachians. In 1764 the Sugar Act tightened enforcement of duties on molasses and sent smugglers to vice-admiralty courts without juries, and the Currency Act barred colonies from printing paper money during a postwar recession.1 Some colonists began to fear “a pattern of increased taxation and restricted liberties.”1

Causes and contributing factors

Until 1765 the colonies had paid only “external” taxes, such as customs duties that regulated trade. The Stamp Act was different: it created a new, direct, “internal” tax to raise revenue. The Maryland lawyer Daniel Dulany drew the line clearly, admitting Parliament’s right to regulate trade but denying its right to impose an internal tax “without their consent for the single purpose of revenue.”1 The act also touched far more people than the Sugar Act had, from printers and lawyers to college graduates and sailors who played cards, which helped make resistance broad and popular.1 Colonists insisted that as Englishmen they could be taxed only by their own elected representatives. British officials answered that they were “virtually represented” in Parliament, like English districts that elected no members; one colonial pamphleteer called that a “monstrous idea.”1

What happened

The Yawp describes three kinds of resistance, roughly divided by class.1

  • Legislatures. On May 30, 1765, Virginia’s House of Burgesses passed the Virginia Resolves, claiming for colonists all the liberties of the people of Great Britain. Newspapers printed them with extra, more radical resolutions that the Burgesses had not passed, and these helped push other assemblies further.1 In October 1765 delegates from nine colonies met in New York City as the Stamp Act Congress. Its declaration professed loyalty to the king but held that “no taxes be imposed on them, but with their own consent, given personally, or by their representatives.”1,3
  • Merchants. Merchants in the port cities signed nonimportation agreements; in New York “upwards of two hundred principal merchants” refused to buy British goods. By January 1766 London merchants were telling Parliament they faced ruin.1
  • Crowds. In Boston, crowds burned the stamp distributor Andrew Oliver in effigy and tore down a building he owned. He resigned the next day, and a week later a crowd wrecked much of the home of Lieutenant Governor Thomas Hutchinson.1 Notices in New York warned that the first man to use stamped paper should “take care of his House, Person, & Effects.” By November 16 all twelve stamp distributors had resigned, so the act could not be enforced.1 Groups calling themselves the Sons of Liberty formed in most colonies.1

Immediate consequences

Under this pressure Parliament repealed the Stamp Act. The Yawp dates the repeal to February 1766, while the Declaratory Act passed with it is dated March 18, 1766, so the repeal is often given as March.1,4 To save face, Parliament passed the Declaratory Act at the same time, asserting its “full power and authority to make laws and statutes … to bind the colonies and people of America … in all cases whatsoever.”4,1 Colonists, busy celebrating, paid it little attention; New Yorkers even raised a lead statue of King George III. The Yawp suggests that colonists may never have felt prouder to be part of the British Empire than in 1766.1

Long-term significance

Britain still needed revenue, and in 1767 the Townshend Acts imposed new duties on goods such as glass, paint, and tea.1 Colonists resisted again, this time more widely: women organized spinning clubs and signed boycott lists, and Committees of Correspondence linked the colonies.1 The Stamp Act crisis had given colonists a shared principle, that only their own representatives could tax them, and a set of tools, from intercolonial congresses to boycotts and street protest, that they would use against the Townshend duties, the Tea Act, and the Coercive Acts.1,3
