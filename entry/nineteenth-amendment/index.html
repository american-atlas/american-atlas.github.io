The Nineteenth Amendment

Women’s right to vote, 1920

August 18, 1920

The Nineteenth Amendment provides that “the right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex.”1 First introduced in Congress in 1878, it passed in June 1919 and was ratified on August 18, 1920, when Tennessee became the thirty-sixth state to approve it.1 It capped more than seventy years of organizing that began at Seneca Falls in 1848, and it was widely called the “Susan B. Anthony Amendment.”2,4 Discriminatory state laws, however, kept many women of color from voting long afterward.1

Why it matters

The Nineteenth Amendment made women’s suffrage national. It was the work of several generations who, in the National Archives’ words, “lectured, wrote, marched, lobbied, and practiced civil disobedience.”1 Few of the movement’s early leaders lived to see it, and its promise remained incomplete for many women of color.1,4

Historical circumstances

The organized movement for women’s votes began at the women’s rights convention in Seneca Falls, New York, in 1848, organized by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott.2 After the Civil War, the Fifteenth Amendment barred racial discrimination in voting but not discrimination by sex, which split the suffrage movement into rival associations.6,4 In 1878 a woman suffrage amendment was introduced in Congress, beginning a campaign for passage that lasted more than four decades.1 The two main organizations merged in 1890 as the National American Woman Suffrage Association (NAWSA).4

Suffragists pursued several strategies at once. Some campaigned state by state, and the West led the way. The American Yawp says six western states had adopted woman suffrage by 1911, while the National Archives counts nine western states by 1912.3,1 Others challenged male-only voting laws in court, and some turned to picketing, silent vigils, and hunger strikes.1 Women’s clubs, the Woman’s Christian Temperance Union, the Women’s Trade Union League, and Black women’s organizations such as the National Association of Colored Women all took up the cause.3 Black suffragists such as Ida B. Wells, who founded the Alpha Suffrage Club in Chicago in 1913, pressed for Black women’s votes even when white suffragists pushed them aside.5

Principal ideas

The amendment is two sentences long. Section 1 reads: “The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex.” Section 2 gives Congress power to enforce it by appropriate legislation.1 Its wording follows the form of the Fifteenth Amendment of 1870, which forbade denying the vote “on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude.”1,6 Like that amendment, it did not grant the vote outright. It forbade one specific ground for denying it.1

The final push

On the eve of World War I, NAWSA adopted a dual strategy of winning state suffrage laws while pressing Congress for a federal amendment.3,4 A newer, more militant group, the National Woman’s Party led by Alice Paul, took to the streets. Beginning in January 1917 its members picketed the White House, and more than 150 women were arrested and imprisoned.3 By 1916 almost all the major suffrage organizations were united behind a constitutional amendment.1 New York adopted woman suffrage in 1917, and in January 1918 President Woodrow Wilson declared his support for the amendment, shifting the political balance.1,3

Passage and ratification

The House passed the amendment on May 21, 1919, and the Senate followed two weeks later.1 Ratification by three-fourths of the states took fourteen months. On August 18, 1920, Tennessee became the thirty-sixth state to ratify, and on August 26 Secretary of State Bainbridge Colby certified the amendment.1 (The National Park Service describes it as “signed into law” on August 26, but constitutional amendments are not signed by the president. The date marks Colby’s certification.)2,1 The National Park Service notes that the last state ratified the amendment in 1984.2

A divided movement

The suffrage movement was not always united. The Yawp notes that some suffragists, even outside the South, argued that white women’s votes were needed to maintain white supremacy.3 At the 1913 suffrage parade in Washington, organizers asked Black women to march at the back. Ida B. Wells refused, waited on the sidelines, and joined the march when the Chicago contingent passed.5

Who still could not vote

Ratification “did not ensure full enfranchisement,” the National Archives emphasizes. Many women, especially African American women and other women of color, remained unable to vote for decades because of discriminatory state voting laws.1 The Archives notes that decades of struggle to include African Americans and other minority women in the promise of voting rights still lay ahead.1
