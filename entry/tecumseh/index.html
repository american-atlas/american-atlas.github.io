Tecumseh

Shawnee leader who tried to unite Native nations against American expansion

Tecumseh was a Shawnee leader who, with his brother Tenskwatawa, “the Prophet,” tried to build an alliance of Native nations from the Great Lakes to the Gulf of Mexico to stop American expansion.1,4 He traveled from Canada to Georgia calling for unity, resistance and “the restoration of sacred power,” and he refused to concede any more land.4 After American troops destroyed his headquarters at Prophetstown in 1811, his confederacy fought alongside Britain in the War of 1812.2 His death at the Battle of the Thames on October 5, 1813, broke the confederacy apart.3

Why it matters

Tecumseh led the last great attempt to unite the Native nations east of the Mississippi against American expansion. With his death, the American Battlefield Trust writes, “many tribes who followed his leadership peeled away,” and organized resistance in the Old Northwest ended.3,4

Early life and context

Tecumseh was born in 1769 while his parents, Pukeshinwau of the Kispoko Shawnee Panther Clan and Methoataaskee of the Piqua Shawnee Turtle Clan, were staying in a village in the Scioto River valley of present-day Ohio. His parents came from Shawnee communities living among the Creeks on the Tallapoosa River in present-day Alabama, and he always said that valley was his home.1 His name, Tekoomsē, was given while a great comet crossed the sky; the Shawnee called comets “sky panthers.”1

In 1774, when Tecumseh was six, his father was killed at the Battle of Kanawha near Point Pleasant in present-day West Virginia. His oldest brother, Cheeseekau, trained him as a warrior, and his sister Tecumapease taught him the customs and spirituality of his people.1 He grew up amid constant war with settlers pushing into the Ohio Valley. He fought in the Native confederacy that destroyed an American army on the Wabash in 1791, saw Cheeseekau killed, and was at the defeat at Fallen Timbers in August 1794. He refused to sign the Treaty of Greenville of 1795, which gave up most of present-day Ohio.1,4

Major actions and achievements

  • The Prophet’s revival. Tenskwatawa preached that the Master of Life had charged the brothers with returning Native peoples to “the one true path” and ridding their communities of the corrupting influence of American trade and culture.4 The brothers founded towns at Greenville and then Prophetstown, in present-day Indiana, in defiance of the Greenville treaty.4
  • A confederacy. Tecumseh turned the revival into a political and military alliance. He offered Native peoples, the American Yawp writes, “a distinctly Native American identity” under a common spirituality. When the 1809 Treaty of Fort Wayne required Indiana tribes to sell three million acres, he organized a confederation to resist.4,2
  • The southern journey. On September 19, 1811, Tecumseh, Tenskwatawa and their cousin Seekaboo arrived at the Creek town of Tukabatchee to call for unity and the reclaiming of Native land.1 A great comet visible that year, later called Tecumseh’s Comet, was taken as a sign.1 The Creek prophet Hillis Hadjo, who traveled with him, carried his ideas to the Red Stick Creeks.4
  • The British alliance. While Tecumseh was in the South, Governor William Henry Harrison marched about 1,000 men against Prophetstown. Tenskwatawa attacked them on November 7, 1811, against Tecumseh’s warnings, and lost; Harrison burned the town.2 Tecumseh returned three months later to find it in ruins and allied his remaining forces with Britain, where they played an important part in British successes around the Great Lakes.2

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Not all Native leaders followed Tecumseh, and his movement deepened divisions. The Yawp notes that the brothers used their message to strengthen their own authority over rival leaders, and that Tenskwatawa denounced opponents and those who sought accommodation with the Americans as witches.4 Among the Creeks, some warriors who fought with him were later killed on the orders of the Creek National Council for their raids.1 Much of what is known about Tecumseh comes from American and British sources, and accounts of his death differ: an 1846 Currier lithograph shows Colonel Richard M. Johnson shooting him, while the Encyclopedia of Alabama records that his body was carried from the field and buried in secret.3,1

Legacy

At the Thames, British General Henry Procter retreated and fled, leaving Tecumseh and his warriors to make a last stand in a swamp. When he fell, his fighters scattered and his confederacy, “built up of numerous Native American tribes, collapsed.”3 His opponent Harrison later rode the fame of Tippecanoe to the presidency as the Whig candidate of 1840.5

A long tradition of resistance

The Yawp places Tecumseh’s confederacy at the end of a long line of Native movements: the Delaware prophet Neolin and the Ottawa leader Pontiac in the 1760s, and the “Western Confederacy” that defeated two American armies in the early 1790s before losing at Fallen Timbers. Tecumseh’s experience in that war, the Yawp suggests, “probably influenced his later efforts to generate solidarity.”4
