The Great Depression

The deepest economic collapse in American history, 1929–1941

August 1929 – 1941 · Nationwide and worldwide

The Great Depression was the longest and deepest economic downturn in the history of the United States and the modern industrial economy.2 Beginning in 1929, it brought a stock market crash, thousands of bank failures, collapsing farm prices, and unemployment that reached about a quarter of the workforce.1,2,6 The Federal Reserve’s own historians now say its mistakes made the slump far worse.2 Herbert Hoover’s reliance on voluntary action gave way to Franklin D. Roosevelt’s New Deal, which transformed the federal government, but full recovery came only with World War II.1,6,8

Why it matters

The Great Depression was the worst economic disaster in American history. It discredited the belief that the economy would right itself without government help and gave rise to the New Deal and the modern federal government.2,1,6

Historical background

The 1920s looked like an age of prosperity. Automobiles, telephones, and radios spread across the country, and ordinary men and women bought stocks, often on margin: they put down about 10 percent of the price and borrowed the rest.3 The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose sixfold, from 63 in August 1921 to 381 in September 1929. Just after the peak, the economist Irving Fisher declared that stock prices had reached “what looks like a permanently high plateau.”3

Beneath the boom lay serious weaknesses. The gains were unequal: per capita income rose 10 percent for all Americans during the decade, but 75 percent for the wealthiest.1 By 1929, the Miller Center notes, 5 percent of Americans held nearly a third of the nation’s money and property, while more than 80 percent had no savings at all.8 Industrial wages stagnated while profits poured into the stock market, and the market for cars and appliances was nearing saturation. Even in good years, unemployment hovered around 7 percent.1,8 Farmers had been in trouble since 1920–1921, when prices for cotton, grain, and other crops collapsed; by the late 1920s farm incomes had shrunk to a third of the national average, and many farmers could not repay loans taken out in better times.1,8 Abroad, the international gold standard, rebuilt after World War I, made the world’s economies rigid and closely linked, so that a shock in one country spread quickly to the others.7,2

Causes and contributing factors

Historians and economists still debate the Depression’s causes, and most see several working together.7,8,1 The Miller Center lists structural weaknesses in agriculture and industry, a fragile international economy, and a speculative, unstable financial sector, all of which created a badly unequal distribution of wealth: “a consumer economy in which few consumed.”8

Monetary policy turned a downturn into a catastrophe. In 1928 and 1929 the Federal Reserve raised interest rates to curb stock speculation, and because of the gold standard its tight money pushed other nations into recession too.2,3 Then, when banks began to fail, the Fed did not act as a lender of last resort. Some of its leaders even believed that weak banks should be allowed to fail. Hoover’s Treasury secretary, Andrew Mellon, who sat on the Federal Reserve Board, held this “liquidationist” view.2 From the fall of 1930 through the winter of 1933, the money supply fell by nearly 30 percent, and prices fell with it. That deflation raised the real burden of debts and drove banks, firms, and families into bankruptcy.2,4 In 2002, Ben Bernanke, then a Federal Reserve governor, admitted publicly that the Fed’s mistakes had contributed to the “worst economic disaster in American history.”2

Trade policy made things worse. In June 1930 Hoover signed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff, which raised rates on farm and factory imports to historic levels.8 Other countries retaliated, and between 1929 and 1932 world trade fell from $36 billion to $12 billion; American exports fell by 78 percent.1 Some Democrats later claimed that the tariff had caused the Depression, a charge the Miller Center calls politically motivated, since the slump was already well under way. Most economists agree, however, that it deepened the crisis.8

What happened

The crash. On Thursday, October 24, 1929, stock prices suddenly plunged. Leading bankers met at the offices of J. P. Morgan and ostentatiously bought shares to halt the slide, but the panic resumed the next week.1,3 On Black Monday, October 28, the Dow fell nearly 13 percent, and on Black Tuesday, October 29, nearly 12 percent more.3,8 By mid-November the market had lost almost half its value.3 Shares of U.S. Steel fell from $262 to $22.1 Only about 2.5 percent of Americans had brokerage accounts, and the crash alone did not cause the Depression. But it frightened consumers, who stopped buying cars and other goods on credit, and companies like Ford cut production and laid off workers.1,3 The market kept sliding until July 1932, when the Dow closed at 41.22, 89 percent below its peak. It did not regain its 1929 level until November 1954.3

The bank panics. By the fall of 1930 the recession had lasted fifteen months, about as long as earlier downturns, and recovery seemed near.4 Then, in November 1930, the collapse of Caldwell and Company, the South’s largest financial holding company, set off a chain of failures among the banks that kept their reserves with it, and panic spread from town to town.4 On December 11 the Bank of United States, the fourth-largest bank in New York City, closed.4 More waves of panic followed in 1931 and 1932. About 1,350 banks failed in 1930 and more than 2,200 in 1931; between 1929 and 1933, some 5,000 collapsed, taking depositors’ savings with them.8,1 The Federal Reserve’s response varied by district. The Atlanta Fed rushed cash to troubled banks, and its region recovered sooner; the St. Louis Fed refused to help banks outside the system, and hundreds of them failed.4

Hard times. By 1933, 24.9 percent of the workforce, 12,830,000 people, were unemployed, and many who still had jobs worked only part time. Workers who kept their jobs saw their wages fall by 42.5 percent between 1929 and 1933.6,1 One in four farms went into foreclosure.8 Families exhausted their savings, borrowed from relatives, ran up credit at the corner store, and finally turned to charities, which were themselves running dry: by mid-1932 a quarter of New York’s private charities had closed, and relief agencies in Atlanta could give needy families only $1.30 a week.1 The homeless built shantytowns of packing crates and scrap metal, mockingly called “Hoovervilles,” and young people whose families could no longer feed them rode the rails in search of work.1,6 Employers were quicker to fire married women, on the assumption that they did not need to work.1 Black workers were generally the last hired and the first fired. In 1932, when national unemployment was about 25 percent, Black unemployment reached as high as 50 percent.1

The Dust Bowl. On the Great Plains, drought began in 1932 and lasted until at least 1936. Decades of plowing had stripped away the grass that held the soil, and winds whipped the exposed topsoil into dust storms that darkened the sky as far east as Washington, D.C., and the Atlantic.1 Ruined farmers from Oklahoma, Texas, Kansas, and Arkansas headed west to California; Oklahoma alone lost 440,000 people, 18.4 percent of its 1930 population.1 California, Florida, and Colorado set up “border blockades” in the winter of 1935–1936 to turn poor migrants away.1 Dorothea Lange’s 1936 photograph Migrant Mother, taken at a camp for farmworkers in Nipomo, California, became the era’s most famous image.1

Deportation. Fearing competition for scarce jobs, the Hoover administration tightened immigration rules in September 1930, and between 1930 and 1932 some 54,000 people were deported.1 Mexican immigrants and Mexican Americans were hit hardest. In Los Angeles, officials staged deportation raids and pressured Mexicans and Mexican Americans on relief to leave “voluntarily” for Mexico. From 1930 to 1940 the Mexican-born population of Arizona, California, New Mexico, and Texas fell from 616,998 to 377,433.1

Hoover’s response. Hoover was not idle, but he believed that voluntary cooperation, not federal relief, was the answer. “Any lack of confidence in the economic future and the basic strength of business in the United States is simply foolish,” he said in November 1929.1 He won pledges from business leaders not to cut wages, urged states and cities to expand public works, and created committees to coordinate private charity.8,1 “We cannot legislate ourselves out of a world economic depression,” he told Congress in 1931.1 As the crisis deepened he took steps that were radical for him: the Reconstruction Finance Corporation (1932) lent money to banks, railroads, and other businesses, and the Emergency Relief and Construction Act provided $2 billion for public works and $300 million for relief programs run by the states.8 Critics such as Congressman Fiorello La Guardia called the RFC a “millionaire’s dole.”1

The Bonus Army. In the summer of 1932, more than fifteen thousand unemployed World War I veterans and their families camped in Washington to demand early payment of a bonus that was not due until 1945. “While there were billions for bankers,” they complained, “there was nothing for the poor.”1 After the Senate voted the bonus down, most went home, but thousands stayed. Troops under General Douglas MacArthur drove the remaining veterans out with tear gas, bayonets, and tanks and burned their camp at Anacostia Flats.1,8 Accounts of the attack differ. The American Yawp writes that Hoover ordered the veterans out and that two marchers and a baby died. The Miller Center says that Hoover ordered a peaceful removal, that War Secretary Patrick Hurley and MacArthur exceeded their orders, and that one veteran was killed.1,8 Either way, the images helped seal Hoover’s defeat.1,8

Immediate consequences

In November 1932 voters turned Hoover out in a landslide and elected Franklin D. Roosevelt, who had promised “a new deal for the American people.”1,6 By his inauguration on March 4, 1933, the banking system had collapsed, and the Federal Reserve dates the bottom of the Depression to that month.6,2 “The only thing we have to fear is fear itself,” Roosevelt told the nation.9 He declared a national bank holiday, and in his first hundred days Congress passed a wave of laws that created the agencies of the New Deal.6,1 New banking laws created federal deposit insurance and reformed the financial system, and the United States left the gold standard in 1933.2,4,7

Recovery began in 1933 but was slow and incomplete. After the Federal Reserve doubled banks’ reserve requirements in 1936 and federal fiscal policy tightened, the economy fell in May 1937 into a sharp “recession within the Depression” that lasted until June 1938. Industrial production fell 32 percent, and unemployment rose again to 20 percent.5 Only when war orders and defense spending poured in did the economy return to full employment. Output grew by 49 percent from 1938 to 1942, and the Depression ended in 1941.5,6,2

Long-term significance

The Great Depression changed the relationship between Americans and their government. Before it, Washington played a small role in economic life; afterward, Americans expected the federal government to protect bank deposits, regulate the stock market, provide old-age pensions and unemployment insurance, and act to prevent mass unemployment.6,1,2 The reforms of the 1930s, and the lessons drawn from the Fed’s failures, turned the Federal Reserve into a modern central bank. In 2002 Bernanke, speaking at a conference honoring Milton Friedman, whose research with Anna Schwartz had laid much of the blame on the Fed, promised “we won’t do it again.”2

The Depression also reshaped American foreign policy. It pushed the United States further into isolation during the 1930s, as Japan, Italy, and Germany began their aggressions. In the long run, the State Department’s historians conclude, the lesson that turning inward had made the crisis worse helped lead the United States to take a leading role in the world economy after World War II.7 The Roosevelt Library calls it a test of “the fabric of American life” that caused Americans “to doubt their abilities and their values.”6
