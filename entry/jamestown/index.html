The Founding of Jamestown

The first permanent English settlement in North America, 1607

May 13, 1607 · Jamestown Island, Virginia

In May 1607, 104 English men and boys sent by the Virginia Company, a private company of investors, chose a marshy riverside site on the James River for a settlement they named for King James I.1,3 Jamestown nearly failed from disease, hunger, and conflict with the Powhatan peoples whose lands it occupied, and in the winter of 1609 to 1610 most of the settlers died.1,4 The colony was saved by gifts of food from Chief Powhatan, by new settlers and supplies, and, from 1612, by John Rolfe’s tobacco.1,2 In 1619 it held the first representative assembly in English North America, and within weeks the first documented Africans arrived. Its growth onto Powhatan land led to two major Powhatan attacks, in 1622 and 1644.8,9,1

Why it matters

Jamestown was where English America began, and where the contradictions of American history first took shape together: self-government alongside slavery and the dispossession of Native peoples.1,8,9

Historical background

The Chesapeake region was home to the Powhatan, whose chief Wahunsenacawh, called Powhatan by the English, inherited six communities and by the early 1600s ruled more than thirty Algonquian-speaking groups and some fifteen thousand people, who paid him tribute.7,4 The Powhatan’s own name for the region was Tsenacommacah. Historic Jamestowne gives its size as 8,000 square miles in one place and 10,000 in another.5,7 England’s first colonies in America had failed, including the “Lost Colony” at Roanoke.4 In April 1606 King James I granted a charter to the Virginia Company of London, a group of investors, to settle “that part of America commonly called Virginia” and any lands “not now actually possessed by any Christian Prince or People.” The charter said that the colonies would spread the Christian religion among people “in Darkness and miserable Ignorance,” and promised the colonists and their children “all Liberties, Franchises, and Immunities” of Englishmen.11 The Company’s instructions told the colonists to build a secure settlement, find gold, and look for a water route to the Pacific. They were also told to “have Great Care not to Offend the naturals,” meaning Native Americans.10,4

Causes and contributing factors

The settlers left London in December 1606 on three ships, the Susan Constant, the Godspeed, and the Discovery. The National Park Service dates the departure to December 6, and Historic Jamestowne’s biography of John Smith says the fleet sailed December 20.1,6 They reached the Chesapeake in April 1607. The Company had ordered that its council members’ names be kept in a sealed box. When it was opened, Edward Maria Wingfield was named president, and John Smith, who had been imprisoned on the voyage on a charge of mutiny, was released and made a member of the council.1,6

The colonists picked a site on the James River for reasons the Company had laid down. It was surrounded by water on three sides and far inland, which made it easy to defend against the Spanish. The water was deep enough to moor ships at the shore, and the site was not occupied by Native people.1 Its drawbacks showed up later: the settlers drank salty or slimy river water, and by 1609 a drought made food scarce, which the National Park Service links to the collapse of relations with the Powhatan.1 The National Park Service dates the choice of site to May 13, while Historic Jamestowne says the settlers landed on May 14.1,3

What happened

The fort. Within days of landing the colonists were attacked by Powhatan warriors, and Historic Jamestowne’s timeline records an attack by about two hundred, in which at least one settler was killed and eleven were wounded.3,4 By June 15 they had built a triangular fort with a bulwark at each corner for artillery.1 Captain Newport sailed for England on June 22, and soon the settlers began to die of disease, hunger, and war.1 Archaeology has recovered the site of the original fort, which many had thought lost to the river. Jamestown Rediscovery has dug there since 1994.5,10

Smith and Powhatan. Chief Powhatan sent gifts of food, and, in the National Park Service’s words, without the Powhatan’s help in the early years the settlement “would most likely have failed.”1 John Smith, a veteran soldier, was good at trading for corn with glass beads, copper, and iron tools.6,3 Captured by Powhatan’s men in December 1607, he later said that Pocahontas saved his life. Historic Jamestowne notes that historians doubt it, and its timeline says most historians now believe it was a mock execution, if it took place at all.6,4 As council president from September 1608 Smith enforced the rule that “he that will not worke, shall not eate,” and he strengthened the fort.6 Relations with the Powhatan went up and down. Newport brought Powhatan a crown, and Powhatan insisted that he was a king too and would not kneel. When Smith left after a gunpowder explosion in October 1609, he never returned to Virginia.4,6

The Starving Time (1609–1610). By late 1609 relations had soured because the English were demanding too much food in a drought.1 The Powhatan besieged the fort. Historic Jamestowne reports that about 300 settlers crowded in and that only 60 were still alive the next spring.3 Settlers who were afraid to leave the fort ate animals, shoe leather, and sometimes the bodies of the dead. Historic Jamestowne’s timeline cites the remains of a 14-year-old girl, nicknamed “Jane,” as evidence of survival cannibalism.1,4 The sources give different death rates: 80 to 90 percent according to William Strachey, quoted by the National Park Service, and more than 75 percent according to Historic Jamestowne.1,4 In May 1610 the survivors of the shipwrecked Sea Venture, who had spent ten months in Bermuda, finally reached Jamestown. Sir Thomas Gates found it in ruins and decided to abandon it. Less than a day after sailing, the colonists met a fleet bringing the new governor, Lord De La Warr, and returned.1,2

Tobacco and war. Sir Thomas Gates imposed martial law, and the English raided Powhatan towns. Historic Jamestowne’s timeline records that George Percy led an attack on the Paspahegh town, burning houses and cutting down cornfields, and that the Paspahegh queen and her children were kidnapped and later executed.4 John Rolfe, who had survived the Sea Venture wreck, experimented with a sweeter strain of tobacco in 1612. The colonists had tried silkmaking, glassmaking, lumber, sassafras, and soap ashes without profit.2 The sources disagree on where he got the seed: Ralph Hamor said it came from somewhere in the Caribbean, possibly Trinidad, and Historic Jamestowne’s timeline says probably Bermuda.2,4 In 1613 Captain Samuel Argall kidnapped Pocahontas, Powhatan’s favored daughter, and held her for ransom. She learned English, converted to Christianity, and took the name Rebecca. In April 1614 she married Rolfe, ending the First Anglo-Powhatan War.4,5,2 Tobacco exports rose from 20,000 pounds in 1617 to a million and a half pounds a dozen years later.2

Immediate consequences

Before the assembly met, the Virginia Company issued new instructions known as the “Great Charter,” ending martial law, authorizing an assembly, and setting up the headright system: fifty acres for anyone who paid his own passage, and fifty more for each person he brought. The Historic Jamestowne timeline says that system “lays the economic foundation” for legal slavery.4 On July 30, 1619, Governor George Yeardley opened the first General Assembly in the choir of the church at Jamestown. It had the governor, four councilors, and twenty-two burgesses elected by the free white men of each settlement. Its first law set a minimum price for tobacco.8,4 Historic Jamestowne calls it the first representative governing body to meet in the Americas, and the National Park Service the beginning of representative government in what is now the United States.8,1

In late August 1619 the White Lion, an English privateer, arrived at Point Comfort with “20. and odd Negroes,” as John Rolfe put it, who had been taken from a Portuguese slave ship bound for Mexico. They were from Angola, and they had been captured in wars between Portugal and the Kongo and Ndongo kingdoms.9 A second privateer, the Treasurer, may have sold seven to nine more, among them a woman called Angela.9 The Africans were sold into bondage although Virginia had no laws on slavery. It is contested whether they were considered indentured servants or enslaved people, but the National Park Service writes that the evidence points to treatment close to enslavement.9,1 The Company also sent about ninety women that year to marry settlers.4

Tobacco gave the colonists a reason to take more Powhatan land. In March 1622 Opechancanough, who had become the more powerful leader after Powhatan’s death in 1618 and was tired of English encroachment, planned a coordinated attack. A Powhatan boy living with the English warned Jamestown, which was spared, but 350 to 400 of the 1,200 settlers were killed according to the National Park Service. Historic Jamestowne says 347, about a quarter of the total, in one place and a third of the colonists in another.1,4,5 Ten years of war followed, until a shaky peace in 1632. In 1624 the king took away the bankrupt Company’s charter, and Virginia became a royal colony.1 In April 1644 Opechancanough attacked again and killed another 350 to 400 settlers of some 8,000. After he was captured and shot by a guard in 1646, the Powhatan chiefdom was reduced to tributary status and its new leader signed treaties making the Powhatan subjects of the English.1

Long-term significance

Jamestown set patterns that shaped the South and the nation. It showed that a private company could found a colony. It produced a plantation economy built on tobacco and a representative assembly open to the free white men of property. It also meant dispossession of Native peoples and a growing system of slavery.1,4 In 1676 Bacon’s Rebellion, in which about a thousand settlers followed Nathaniel Bacon against the Native peoples they blamed for frontier raids and against Governor Berkeley, burned much of the town. Afterwards a new treaty set up reservation lands and required yearly tribute of fish and game.1 Jamestown remained the capital until the statehouse burned in 1698 and the government moved to Williamsburg in 1699.1,10

The place itself nearly vanished. By 1893, when the Association for the Preservation of Virginia Antiquities (now Preservation Virginia) received part of the island, river erosion had led many to think that the 1607 fort lay under water. The National Park Service acquired the rest in 1934.10 Archaeologists have since uncovered the fort, thousands of Native artifacts, and, in 2010, the church where Pocahontas and Rolfe were married.5,10 Historic Jamestowne notes that the traditional story painted the settlers as ill-suited to the task, but twenty years of archaeology suggest that many of the artisans and laborers did all they could to build a working colony.10
