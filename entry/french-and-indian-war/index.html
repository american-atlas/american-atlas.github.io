The French and Indian War

The North American front of the Seven Years’ War, 1754–1763

1754 – February 10, 1763 · Ohio Valley, New York, Pennsylvania, Canada, and the Great Lakes

The French and Indian War was the North American part of a world war, known elsewhere as the Seven Years’ War, between Britain and France.1,3 It began in 1754 when a young George Washington led Virginia militia into the Ohio Valley, a region both empires claimed.2 France and its Native allies won the early battles, but British victories at Louisbourg, Quebec, and Montreal ended French rule in Canada by 1760.1 The Treaty of Paris of 1763 gave Britain most of eastern North America.1,6 Victory left Britain deep in debt, faced with Pontiac’s War, and determined to tighten control of its colonies, which set off the conflicts that led to the American Revolution.1,7

Why it matters

The French and Indian War made Britain the dominant power in eastern North America, but the costs of victory and the problems of governing a vast new empire pushed Britain and its colonies into the conflict that became the American Revolution. It also gave George Washington his first military experience, including his only surrender.1,2,3,7

Who fought

The war pitted Britain and its North American colonists against France, French colonists, and Native allies.1 The American Yawp stresses that these were not simply European wars: American militiamen fought for Britain against the French and their Native allies, and French victories early in the war “were often the result of alliances with Native Americans.”1 Native nations fought for their own reasons, above all trade and the defense of their lands, and the Six Nations of the Iroquois met Washington’s first mission in 1753, unimpressed by his tiny force.2 The name “French and Indian War” reflects the British colonists’ view of their enemies.1

Political background

Of the eighty-seven years between 1688 and 1775, the Yawp counts thirty-seven in which Britain was at war with France and its Native allies, with raids and captive-taking along the border of New England and New France.1 By the 1750s the two empires were feuding over their boundaries, especially the upper Ohio Valley, which Britain and Virginia land speculators claimed.1,2 In late 1753 Virginia’s lieutenant governor, Robert Dinwiddie, sent the young Major Washington with six frontiersmen to demand that the French leave. The French refused.2

Major phases

1754–1757: French victories. In 1754 Washington returned with about 140 men. On May 28 his Virginians and their Native allies attacked a French party, killing several soldiers and their commander, Joseph Jumonville.2 The sources describe the clash differently: the Yawp writes that Washington’s force “killed a French diplomat,” while Encyclopedia Virginia calls it a skirmish that the French “portrayed as an assassination.”1,2 Washington wrote, “I heard Bulletts whistle and believe me there is something charming in the sound.”2 On July 3–4 the French and their allies forced him to surrender at Fort Necessity, the only time he ever surrendered.3,2 In July 1755 General Edward Braddock’s column of about 1,700 regulars and militia was ambushed and routed near Fort Duquesne; Braddock was killed, and Washington, a volunteer aide, had two horses shot from under him.2,1 The French burned British outposts such as Fort William Henry in 1757 and repulsed a British attack on Fort Carillon (Ticonderoga) in 1758.1

1756: A world war. In Europe the war began in earnest in 1756, when Prussia, Britain’s ally, invaded Saxony. Britain paid subsidies to Prussia and smaller German states, while its navy defeated the French at sea and Robert Clive won at Plassey in India.1

1758–1760: British conquest. Control of the sea let Britain send more troops to America. Louisbourg fell in 1758, and in 1759 General James Wolfe defeated General Louis-Joseph de Montcalm on the Plains of Abraham outside Quebec. The British called 1759 their “year of miracles.” With the capture of Montreal in 1760, the war in North America ended.1 Britain then fought Spain, which entered the war in 1762, and took Cuba and the Philippines.1

Turning points

  • Sea power. British naval victories allowed reinforcements to reach North America and isolated French Canada.1
  • Louisbourg (1758) and Quebec (1759). The fall of France’s great Atlantic fortress and of Quebec broke French power in Canada.1
  • Colonial unity. At the Albany Congress of 1754 Benjamin Franklin proposed a plan of union to coordinate colonial defenses, and tens of thousands of colonists fought in the war, which the Yawp says “pushed the thirteen American colonies closer together politically and culturally than ever before.”1

Outcome

The Treaty of Paris, signed on February 10, 1763, ended French power on the North American mainland. Britain received much of Canada and North America from France, and Spain, to recover Cuba and the Philippines, gave up Florida.1,6,7 The Fort Necessity park notes that the war “ended in 1763 with the removal of French power from North America and India.”3

Consequences for American society

Pontiac’s War. The British replaced the French as the power in the Great Lakes and Ohio country, and the commander Jeffrey Amherst ended the French custom of diplomatic gift-giving and restricted sales of guns and ammunition to Native peoples, which many saw as preparation for war.1 Inspired in part by the prophet Neolin, the Ottawa leader Pontiac besieged Fort Detroit in May 1763 and Native warriors captured other British forts. The war lasted until 1766.1

The Proclamation of 1763. In October 1763 King George III reserved the lands west of the Appalachian divide for Native nations and forbade colonists “from making any Purchases or Settlements whatever” there.4,7 Colonists who had fought for that land protested.7

The road to revolution. The war doubled Britain’s national debt, and the Yawp writes that victory gave Britain “a larger empire than they could control.”1,7 To pay for it and govern it, Parliament turned to new taxes and enforcement, from the Sugar Act to the Stamp Act, provoking the protests that led to independence.7
