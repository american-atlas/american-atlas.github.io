The G.I. Bill

The Servicemen’s Readjustment Act of 1944

June 22, 1944

The Servicemen’s Readjustment Act, signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on June 22, 1944, just after D-Day, offered World War II veterans money for college and job training, a year of unemployment pay, and government-backed loans for homes, farms, and businesses.1,2 Designed by the American Legion and passed unanimously, the “G.I. Bill of Rights” helped some eight million veterans continue their education and millions more buy homes. It fueled the postwar boom and the growth of the suburbs.1,2,3 But its benefits were not shared equally: discrimination and segregation kept many Black veterans and women from using them fully.1,2,3

Why it matters

The G.I. Bill helped turn millions of veterans into college graduates and homeowners. The Yawp writes that it “established the hallmarks of American middle class life.” Because it was administered through a segregated society, it also widened the gap between white and Black Americans.2,1,3

Historical circumstances

While the war was still being fought, the Department of Labor estimated that 15 million men and women in uniform could come home to unemployment.1 Many feared a return of the Great Depression. The Yawp notes that the economy had collapsed after World War I.2 As early as 1942 the National Resources Planning Board, a White House agency, studied the needs of demobilized workers, and in June 1943 it recommended programs of education and training.1 The American Legion drafted the main features of a new law and pushed it through Congress. The Yawp calls it the brainchild of Henry Colmery, a former head of the Legion.1,2 The Legion publicist Jack Cejnar named it “the GI Bill of Rights.”1 It won support from progressives and conservatives alike and passed both houses unanimously in the spring of 1944. Roosevelt signed it on June 22, days after the Normandy landings.1,2

Principal ideas

The act declared the Veterans Administration “an essential war agency,” second only to the War and Navy Departments in priority for personnel and supplies. It authorized $500 million for new veterans’ hospitals.1 Its best-known titles gave veterans four kinds of help.1,2

  • Education and training. Tuition, books, supplies, equipment, counseling, and a living allowance for veterans who returned to school, college, or job training.1,2
  • Home, farm, and business loans. Government-guaranteed loans that did away with large down payments. The Yawp writes that veterans could buy homes for as little as $1 down.2
  • Unemployment pay. Up to a year of benefits for veterans who could not find work.2
  • Hospital care and job counseling through the Veterans Administration.1

Results

Within seven years about eight million veterans had received educational benefits. Some 2.3 million attended colleges and universities, 3.5 million went to other schools, and 3.4 million received on-the-job training.1 The number of degrees awarded more than doubled between 1940 and 1950.1 About half of all veterans, eight million, collected a total of $4 billion in unemployment pay.2 By 1955 the program had guaranteed 4.3 million home loans worth $33 billion, and veterans bought 20 percent of all new homes built after the war.1 The Yawp gives a slightly different figure, close to four million veterans buying homes, and it notes that the VA also helped nearly 200,000 veterans buy farms.2 Homeownership rose quickly. The Yawp’s World War II chapter says it climbed from about 40 percent of Americans in 1945 to 60 percent a decade later, and its chapter on the 1950s gives 44 percent in 1940 and almost 62 percent in 1960.2,3 By 1956 the education program had paid out $14.5 billion, but the Veterans Administration estimated that higher income taxes from better-educated veterans would repay the cost several times over.1 The law was later extended, and nearly 2.3 million veterans of the Korean War era and more than eight million of the Vietnam era used it.1

Who was left out

The benefits were federal, but veterans used them through private lenders and colleges in a segregated country.3,2 Black veterans were often unable to get mortgages in Black neighborhoods and were largely shut out of white suburbs.1 The VA, like the Federal Housing Administration, refused to back mortgages in “redlined” neighborhoods, and new suburbs such as Levittown sold only to white buyers.3 Segregation forced Black veterans into overcrowded historically Black colleges, which had to turn away close to twenty thousand applicants.2 Women veterans were fewer because the military limited their numbers, and colleges limited the number of women they admitted to make room for male veterans.2 The Yawp concludes that the same programs that lifted many white families into the middle class helped create and enforce racial segregation.3
