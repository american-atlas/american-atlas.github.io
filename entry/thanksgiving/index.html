Thanksgiving Day

How a New England harvest custom became a fixed national holiday, from Washington to Roosevelt

November 26, 1789 – December 26, 1941

Thanksgiving was built step by step. In 1621 the Plymouth colonists and about ninety Wampanoag men shared a three-day harvest celebration, which nineteenth-century writers came to call the “First Thanksgiving.”1,2 In 1789 the first Federal Congress asked George Washington to proclaim a national day of thanks, and he chose Thursday, November 26.3 After decades of varying dates, the editor Sarah Josepha Hale persuaded Abraham Lincoln to proclaim the last Thursday of November in 1863.4,5 When Franklin D. Roosevelt moved it a week earlier in 1939, the country briefly kept two Thanksgivings, until Congress fixed the fourth Thursday of November by law in 1941.3

Why it matters

Thanksgiving is one of the most widely shared American holidays. Its history shows how a regional custom became a symbol of national union, promoted by a magazine editor in the middle of a civil war and fixed in the calendar during the Depression.3,4,5

Historical background

Plymouth, 1621. The only eyewitness account of the harvest celebration at Plymouth is a letter by the colonist Edward Winslow, printed in 1622 in the pamphlet now known as Mourt’s Relation.2 Winslow wrote that after the harvest the governor sent four men out fowling, and that “many of the Indians” came, among them the Wampanoag leader Massasoit “with some 90 men, whom for three days we entertained and feasted”; the Wampanoag killed five deer and gave them to the colonists.2 About half of the 102 Mayflower passengers were still alive, so the English were outnumbered roughly two to one.2,1 Winslow never called the event a thanksgiving. In 1841 the antiquarian Alexander Young printed the passage with a footnote calling it “the first Thanksgiving,” because it resembled the New England holiday of his own day.2

A national day, 1789. On September 28, 1789, just before a recess, the first Federal Congress asked the president to recommend a day of thanksgiving. A few days later Washington proclaimed Thursday, November 26, 1789, a “Day of Publick Thanksgivin,” the first under the new Constitution. Later presidents issued their own proclamations, but the dates, and even the months, varied.3

Causes and contributing factors

  • Sarah Josepha Hale’s campaign. Born in New Hampshire in 1788, Hale became editor of Godey’s Lady’s Book, one of the most widely read magazines in the country, and used it to promote New England’s Thanksgiving customs, from roast turkey to pumpkin pie.4 She petitioned governors and presidents to adopt a single national day, and in one editorial she reported that the last Thursday of November had been kept in twenty-four of the twenty-nine states that held a Thanksgiving at all.4
  • Union in wartime. Hale argued that a fixed national festival would bind the states together, and she pressed the point during the Civil War. On September 28, 1863, she wrote to Lincoln asking for a proclamation making Thanksgiving “a National and fixed Union Festival.”5
  • The Depression calendar. In 1939 the last Thursday fell on November 30. Worried that a short Christmas shopping season would slow the recovery from the Great Depression, Roosevelt moved Thanksgiving to the second-to-last Thursday.3

What happened

Lincoln acted within days. His proclamation of October 3, 1863, written by Secretary of State William H. Seward, invited Americans at home, at sea and abroad to observe the last Thursday of November “as a day of thanksgiving and praise to our beneficent Father who dwelleth in the heavens,” and to remember the widows, orphans and mourners of the war.6,5 From then on presidents proclaimed the holiday every year.3,4

Roosevelt’s change in 1939 split the country. Thirty-two states followed him and sixteen kept the last Thursday, so for two years the nation celebrated two Thanksgivings a week apart.3 On October 6, 1941, the House passed a joint resolution making the last Thursday the legal holiday. The Senate changed it to the fourth Thursday, to allow for Novembers with five Thursdays, and the House agreed.3

Immediate consequences

Roosevelt signed the resolution on December 26, 1941, making the fourth Thursday of November the federal Thanksgiving holiday.3

Long-term significance

The holiday’s path, from a colonial harvest feast to a congressional request, to a magazine campaign, to a presidential proclamation and finally a federal law, shows a national tradition built by government as well as by custom.3,4 Its story has also changed. The Pilgrim Hall Museum stresses that Indigenous people outnumbered the colonists at the 1621 gathering, and that Wampanoag women and children, unmentioned in the English sources, may also have been there.1

Counting the people of 1621

Sources count the colonists slightly differently. The Pilgrim Hall Museum lists 53 Mayflower survivors: 22 men, 4 married women, 5 adolescent girls, 9 adolescent boys and 13 young children, noting that some ages are uncertain.1 Plimoth Patuxet Museums count the same 53 as 24 men, five women, and 24 children and teenagers.2 Both agree that about 90 Wampanoag men came with Massasoit, and Pilgrim Hall notes that the figure may undercount the Indigenous guests, since Winslow says “many of the Indians” came besides Massasoit’s party.1,2

How long did Hale campaign?

In her 1863 letter Hale told Lincoln she had promoted the idea in the Lady’s Book “for the last fifteen years.” Plimoth Patuxet Museums date her campaign to the 1830s.5,4 Her magazine editorials on a national day survive from at least 1848.4
