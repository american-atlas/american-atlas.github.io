Thanksgiving Day

How a day of thanks became a fixed national holiday, from Washington to Roosevelt

November 26, 1789 – December 26, 1941

Thanksgiving became a national custom by presidential proclamation. In 1789 the first Federal Congress asked George Washington to recommend a day of thanksgiving, and he named Thursday, November 26. Later presidents chose varying dates until Abraham Lincoln’s 1863 proclamation made the last Thursday of November the regular day. When Franklin D. Roosevelt moved it a week earlier in 1939, the country briefly celebrated two Thanksgivings until Congress fixed the holiday on the fourth Thursday of November in 1941.

Why it matters

Thanksgiving is one of the most widely shared American holidays; its history shows presidents and Congress shaping a national ritual, and even the economy of the Depression affecting the calendar.

Historical background

On September 28, 1789, just before a recess, the first Federal Congress passed a resolution asking the president to recommend a day of thanksgiving. A few days later George Washington proclaimed Thursday, November 26, 1789, a “Day of Publick Thanksgivin” — the first Thanksgiving celebrated under the new Constitution. Later presidents issued their own proclamations, but the dates and even the months varied.

Causes and contributing factors

Abraham Lincoln’s 1863 proclamation began the regular observance on the last Thursday of November. In 1939, however, the last Thursday fell on the last day of the month. Worried that a shortened Christmas shopping season might slow the recovery from the Great Depression, Franklin D. Roosevelt moved Thanksgiving to the second-to-last Thursday.

What happened

Thirty-two states followed Roosevelt’s proclamation; sixteen refused and kept the last Thursday. For two years the country celebrated two Thanksgivings, one week apart. To end the confusion, the House of Representatives passed a joint resolution on October 6, 1941, making the last Thursday of November the legal holiday. The Senate amended it to the fourth Thursday, to account for Novembers with five Thursdays; the House agreed.

Immediate consequences

President Roosevelt signed the resolution on December 26, 1941, establishing the fourth Thursday in November as the federal Thanksgiving holiday.

Long-term significance

The 1941 law settled the date that Americans still observe. The holiday’s path — from a congressional request in 1789, through presidential proclamations, to a fixed federal date — shows how a national tradition was built step by step by the government as well as by custom.
