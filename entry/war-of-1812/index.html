The War of 1812

The “second war of independence” against Britain, 1812–1815

June 18, 1812 – February 18, 1815

Caught between Britain and France in the Napoleonic Wars, the United States declared war on Britain on June 18, 1812, the first time it had formally declared war.2 Americans went to war over British seizures of their ships and sailors, and over western expansion and British support for Native resistance.1,2 American invasions of Canada failed, and British troops burned Washington, D.C. The Treaty of Ghent, signed on December 24, 1814, restored the prewar situation.2,1 Americans nonetheless remembered the war as a “Second War of Independence,” while for Native nations east of the Mississippi it was a decisive defeat.2

Why it matters

The War of 1812 confirmed American independence and launched Andrew Jackson’s career, but it was a disaster for the Native nations east of the Mississippi and opened their lands to rapid American expansion.2,4

Who fought

The United States fought Great Britain and its Canadian colonists.2 Many Native nations, above all the confederacy of the Shawnee leader Tecumseh, fought alongside the British in the hope of defending their lands.3,2 In the Southeast a civil war within the Creek Nation became part of the wider conflict.2 At New Orleans, Andrew Jackson’s army included frontiersmen, Louisiana militia, Free Men of Color, Choctaw warriors and the privateers of Jean Lafitte.4

Political background

Since 1793 Britain and France had been at war, and both tried to choke each other’s trade. In 1806 France banned neutral trade with Britain, and in 1807 Britain banned trade between France, its allies and the Americas.1 Britain also stopped American ships to search for deserters and “impressed” American sailors into the Royal Navy.1,2 Jefferson’s Embargo of 1807 closed American ports to foreign trade and plunged the country into depression without changing British policy.2 In the West, Americans blamed Britain for arming Native nations resisting settlement, and many who wanted war saw British Canada and Spanish Florida as possible prizes.2,1

President James Madison asked Congress for a declaration of war on June 1, 1812, and, pushed by the young “war hawks,” Congress declared it on June 18.1,2 Ironically, Britain was already repealing its trade restrictions; the news crossed the Atlantic too slowly to stop the fighting.2

Major phases

Failed invasions (1812–1813). The American plan was to seize Canada as a bargaining chip. By the end of 1812 American forces had been routed at Queenston Heights, turned back from Québec, and had surrendered Detroit.2 In 1813 Oliver Hazard Perry won control of Lake Erie in September, and in October Harrison’s army defeated the British and Tecumseh’s warriors at the Battle of the Thames, where Tecumseh was killed.2,3

The war at sea. The small U.S. Navy won early single-ship battles. When the Chesapeake was lost in 1813, its dying captain James Lawrence gave the rallying cry “Don’t give up the ship!”, and the Constitution earned the name “Old Ironsides” when cannonballs seemed to bounce off its hull.5

The Creek War (1813–1814). A war among the Creeks pitted the Red Sticks, influenced by Tecumseh, against those who sought accommodation. Andrew Jackson defeated the Red Sticks at Horseshoe Bend on March 27, 1814, and in the Treaty of Fort Jackson forced the whole Creek Nation, even his own allies, to cede nearly 23 million acres.2

Britain strikes back (1814–1815). Napoleon’s first exile in 1814 freed British troops for America. On August 24, 1814, they burned the public buildings of Washington, including the White House.2,5 Baltimore held in September: militia stopped the land attack at North Point, and Fort McHenry withstood the British fleet, inspiring Francis Scott Key’s “Star-Spangled Banner.”2,5 An American victory on Lake Champlain stopped another invasion from Canada.5 On January 8, 1815, Jackson’s army crushed a British assault below New Orleans; about 2,000 of 3,000 attacking British soldiers became casualties in less than thirty minutes.4

Turning points

  • Lake Erie and the Thames (1813): secured the Northwest for the United States and destroyed Tecumseh’s confederacy.2,3
  • Horseshoe Bend (1814): broke Red Stick power and forced huge Creek land cessions.2
  • Baltimore and Lake Champlain (1814): stopped Britain’s major offensives; by 1814, the State Department’s historians write, “neither side could claim a clear victory.”1,5

Outcome

Under the mediation of the Russian tsar, negotiators met at Ghent in the summer of 1814 and signed a treaty on Christmas Eve. It restored the prewar boundaries, set up a commission for border disputes, and provided for peace with the Native nations.1 It said nothing about impressment; Britain had already stopped its trade restrictions.2 The State Department concludes that “the real causes of the war of 1812, were not merely commerce and neutral rights, but also western expansion, relations with American Indians, and territorial control of North America.”1

Consequences for American society

Americans emerged with a surge of nationalism and new heroes, above all Andrew Jackson.4 The war was followed by the “Era of Good Feelings,” and the Federalist Party, which had opposed the war and met at the Hartford Convention in 1814, collapsed.2,5 The Battlefield Trust calls Native Americans “the worst losers of the war”: Britain abandoned their hope for a recognized Native nation in the peace talks, and without British arms they lost the ability to defend their lands.2 Canada gained a proud military heritage, and relations between Britain and the United States generally improved.2

Was New Orleans pointless?

The Battle of New Orleans was fought two weeks after the Treaty of Ghent was signed, because the news had not yet crossed the Atlantic. The Battlefield Trust’s overview calls it “a stunning but ultimately pointless victory.” The Trust’s own page on the battle disagrees: the treaty said fighting would stop only when both governments ratified it, so the battle “was, indeed, justified.”2,4

Small disagreements in the sources

The Battlefield Trust’s overview says Madison ratified the treaty on February 18, 1815, while its New Orleans page says it was ratified by Congress on February 16.2,4 The overview describes a 25-hour bombardment of Fort McHenry; the American Yawp says twenty-seven hours.2,5 A Yawp caption even dates the burning of Washington to 1813, though its own text gives August 24, 1814.5
