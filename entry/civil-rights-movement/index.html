The Civil Rights Movement

The mass struggle against segregation and disfranchisement, 1950s–1960s

1954–1968

The civil rights movement was a mass campaign by Black Americans and their allies to end legal segregation and win the right to vote in the South.1,2 Building on decades of NAACP lawsuits, it won the Supreme Court’s Brown v. Board of Education decision in 1954 and then turned to nonviolent direct action: the Montgomery bus boycott, the sit-ins, the Freedom Rides, the Birmingham campaign, the March on Washington, Freedom Summer, and the Selma marches.1,2,3 Its victories, the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, dismantled Jim Crow in law and transformed American democracy.15,16

Key facts

Date
1954–1968
Dating note
Often dated from Brown v. Board of Education (1954) to the death of Martin Luther King Jr. (1968); its roots reach back to the NAACP’s legal campaigns of the 1930s and the wartime Double V campaign.
Landmark ruling
Brown v. Board of Education, May 17, 1954, decided 9–0
Key organizations
NAACP (1909), CORE (1942), SCLC (1957), SNCC (1960), and local groups such as Montgomery’s Women’s Political Council
Largest protest
More than 200,000 people at the March on Washington, August 28, 1963
Key laws
Civil Rights Act of 1964 (Senate vote 73–27); Voting Rights Act of 1965
Leaders
Martin Luther King Jr., Rosa Parks, Ella Baker, John Lewis, Diane Nash, Fannie Lou Hamer, Bayard Rustin, Thurgood Marshall, and many local organizers
Method
Litigation, nonviolent direct action, boycotts, and voter registration

Why it matters

The civil rights movement ended legal segregation in the United States, brought about the greatest change in federal protection of voting since Reconstruction, and showed how ordinary people using nonviolent mass action could change the law of a nation.1,16,2

Goals

The movement set out to destroy Jim Crow, the system of laws and customs that segregated Black Southerners in schools, buses, restaurants, parks, and jobs and kept most of them from voting.1,2 After World War II, the “Double V” campaign for victory over fascism abroad and racism at home, and the postwar boom, raised expectations that persistent segregation then disappointed.1 The demands of the 1963 March on Washington summed up the movement’s program: a comprehensive civil rights bill ending segregated public accommodations, “protection of the right to vote,” “desegregation of all public schools,” a federal jobs program, and a law “barring discrimination in all employment.”8 King described the ultimate end as “the creation of the beloved community,” “a society at peace with itself, a society that can live with its conscience”; by the end of his life he argued that full citizenship also required economic security.10,14,17

Strategies

In the courts. Beginning in the early 1930s, NAACP lawyers including Charles Hamilton Houston and Thurgood Marshall chose cases on education, voting, and juries to wear away the legal foundations of segregation, first arguing that Southern states failed to provide even “equal” facilities and then, by the late 1940s, that separation itself was unconstitutional.1 Brown v. Board of Education combined five cases from South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware, Washington, D.C., and Kansas. In Clarendon County, South Carolina, the district spent $179 on each white student in 1950 and $43 on each Black student.1 On May 17, 1954, Chief Justice Earl Warren announced a unanimous decision that “separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.”1,18 But the Court’s 1955 order to desegregate “with all deliberate speed” was so vague that it left the work in the hands of segregation’s defenders.1

Montgomery. On December 1, 1955, Rosa Parks, a member of the NAACP, was arrested for refusing to give up her seat on a Montgomery bus.9,3 Jo Ann Robinson and the Women’s Political Council, who had pressed the city about bus segregation since 1954, called a one-day boycott and printed tens of thousands of leaflets overnight.3,1 On December 5, 90 percent of Montgomery’s Black citizens stayed off the buses, and that day the new Montgomery Improvement Association chose a twenty-six-year-old minister, Martin Luther King Jr., as its president.3,1 For thirteen months, Black residents walked or rode in a carpool of about 300 cars, while city officials indicted more than eighty leaders and segregationists bombed King’s and E. D. Nixon’s homes.3 The protest ended in victory on December 20, 1956, after the Supreme Court struck down bus segregation.3 Women such as Robinson, Johnnie Carr, and the “nameless cooks and maids who walked endless miles” sustained the boycott, and its success led King and other ministers to found the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC) in 1957.3,5

Students take the lead. On February 1, 1960, four Black students from North Carolina A&T sat down at the whites-only Woolworth lunch counter in Greensboro and refused to leave.4 By the end of the month sit-ins had spread to more than thirty places in seven states, and by the end of April more than 50,000 students had taken part. In Nashville, James Lawson trained students such as John Lewis, Diane Nash, and Marion Barry in Gandhian nonviolence.4 That April, SCLC’s Ella Baker called the student leaders to a conference at Shaw University, where they founded the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC). Baker urged them to stay independent and to build “group-centered leaders” rather than follow a single charismatic figure.4,19 In May 1961 the Congress of Racial Equality (CORE) sent interracial Freedom Riders south to test a Supreme Court ruling against segregated bus terminals. At Anniston, Alabama, a mob firebombed one of their buses, and riders were beaten in Birmingham and Montgomery. When CORE halted the rides, Nash and the Nashville students insisted on continuing: “We can’t let them stop us with violence. If we do, the movement is dead.”6 Students from across the country then crowded into the jails of Jackson, Mississippi, and on November 1, 1961, a federal ban on segregation in interstate bus travel took effect.6

Birmingham and Washington. In April 1963 SCLC joined Fred Shuttlesworth’s local movement in Birmingham with sit-ins, marches, and a boycott of downtown stores.7 Arrested on Good Friday, King wrote his “Letter from Birmingham Jail” in reply to eight white clergymen who had called the protests “unwise and untimely”: “For years now, I have heard the word ‘Wait!’ … This ‘Wait’ has almost always meant ‘Never.’”11 In May, more than a thousand Black schoolchildren marched, and the city’s public safety commissioner, Eugene “Bull” Connor, turned fire hoses and police dogs on them. The images caused international outrage and forced a settlement that removed “Whites Only” signs and began the desegregation of lunch counters.7 President John F. Kennedy went on national television in June to call for a civil rights bill.2,15 On August 28, 1963, more than 200,000 people joined the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom, organized by A. Philip Randolph and Bayard Rustin, where King delivered his “I Have a Dream” speech.8

Mississippi and Selma. In Mississippi, where violence made mass street protests nearly impossible, SNCC’s Bob Moses built a grassroots voter registration campaign.12,2 In the summer of 1964 about 1,000 volunteers, most of them white Northern college students, joined Freedom Summer. About 17,000 Black Mississippians tried to register that summer, but registrars accepted only 1,600 applications, and 41 Freedom Schools taught more than 3,000 young people.12 The movement’s Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party challenged the state’s all-white delegation at the Democratic National Convention, where Fannie Lou Hamer, a former sharecropper who had been fired for trying to register and savagely beaten in a Mississippi jail, testified on national television.13 In Selma, Alabama, where only two percent of Black residents were on the voting rolls, SNCC and SCLC launched a voting rights campaign in January 1965. On March 7, “Bloody Sunday,” state troopers clubbed and tear-gassed marchers led by John Lewis and Hosea Williams on the Edmund Pettus Bridge.14 Two weeks later, under federal protection, marchers walked the fifty-four miles to Montgomery, their numbers swelling to 25,000 by the final day.14

Opposition

White Southerners fought the movement with defiance, mass arrests, economic reprisals, and terror. In August 1955, fourteen-year-old Emmett Till of Chicago was abducted and murdered in Mississippi for supposedly whistling at a white woman; an all-white jury acquitted his killers, who months later described the crime in Look magazine. His mother’s insistence on an open-casket funeral made his death national news.9,1 Desegregation met fierce resistance: in 1957 soldiers of the 101st Airborne Division had to escort nine Black students into Little Rock’s Central High School, and in 1962 two people died in rioting when James Meredith enrolled at the University of Mississippi.1,2 Alabama governor George Wallace promised “segregation now, segregation tomorrow, segregation forever” in 1963 and stood in the door of the University of Alabama to block its integration.2

The violence was relentless. The night after the Birmingham agreement, bombs exploded near King’s motel and at his brother’s home; on September 15, 1963, Ku Klux Klan members bombed Birmingham’s Sixteenth Street Baptist Church, killing four young girls.7 The civil rights leader Medgar Evers was assassinated at his home in June 1963.2 In June 1964 three civil rights workers, James Chaney, a Black Mississippian, and two white Northerners, Michael Schwerner and Andrew Goodman, were murdered near Philadelphia, Mississippi, by Klan members and local law officers.12,2 In 1965 a state trooper shot Jimmie Lee Jackson during a march in Marion, Alabama; local white men attacked and fatally injured the Unitarian minister James Reeb in Selma; and Klansmen shot Viola Liuzzo, a volunteer from Michigan, after the march to Montgomery.14

The federal government was often a reluctant ally. The Kennedy administration intervened only after mobs had attacked the Freedom Riders, and President Lyndon B. Johnson, fearing the loss of white Southern votes, backed a compromise at the 1964 convention that offered the Mississippi Freedom Democrats just two at-large seats. “We didn’t come all this way for no two seats,” Hamer replied.6,13 The FBI later made SNCC a target of its COINTELPRO program against Black militancy.10

Achievements

The movement destroyed legal segregation. The Civil Rights Act of 1964, which Kennedy proposed and Johnson pushed through after Kennedy’s assassination, outlawed segregation in hotels, restaurants, theaters, and other public places and banned discrimination in employment. Southern senators tried to talk it to death in a filibuster before the Senate passed it 73 to 27, and Johnson signed it on July 2, 1964.15 The Voting Rights Act, which Johnson introduced after Selma with the words “we shall overcome,” outlawed literacy tests, sent federal examiners to register voters, and required states with a history of discrimination to clear changes to their voting rules with Washington. Johnson signed it on August 6, 1965.14,16 By the end of 1965 a quarter of a million new Black voters had been registered, one-third of them by federal examiners.16 Enforcement of Brown came slowly but dramatically: in 1968, about 80 percent of Black schoolchildren in the South still attended schools that were 90 to 100 percent nonwhite, but by 1972 only 25 percent did, and Southern schools became by many measures the most integrated in the nation.1

The movement also changed how Americans protested. Its tactics, from the boycott and the sit-in to the mass march, and its moral language, rooted in Black churches, helped inspire new movements of Native Americans, Chicanos, and women in the 1960s.2 In King’s own summary, “Montgomery led to the Civil Rights Act of 1957 and 1960; Birmingham inspired the Civil Rights Act of 1964; and Selma produced the voting rights legislation of 1965.”14

Unresolved questions

The laws of 1964 and 1965 attacked segregation by law, but not the poverty, housing discrimination, and police brutality that shaped Black life in Northern cities as well as Southern ones.2 Federal housing policy had itself helped create segregated neighborhoods: from the 1930s, maps “redlined” Black neighborhoods as risky, and the Federal Housing Administration and the Veterans Administration refused to back mortgages there.1 Days after the Voting Rights Act was signed, rioting broke out in the Watts neighborhood of Los Angeles, and riots followed in other cities every summer, most destructively in Newark and Detroit in 1967.2

The movement itself divided. After the 1964 convention, many SNCC activists lost faith in interracial cooperation and nonviolence. In June 1966 Stokely Carmichael, SNCC’s new chairman, began calling for “Black Power,” and SNCC and CORE soon excluded white members.10,2 Malcolm X, a Nation of Islam minister who was killed in 1965, had offered a more militant alternative to King’s approach.2 King, meanwhile, turned to poverty in the North and to opposition to the Vietnam War, and was planning a Poor People’s Campaign for jobs and income when he was assassinated on April 4, 1968.5,9,17 The Supreme Court in 2013 struck down a key provision of the Voting Rights Act requiring federal oversight of voting rules in nine states.16
