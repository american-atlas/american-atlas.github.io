30:T426,Khrushchev called the quarantine an “act of aggression,” but on October 24 and 25 some Soviet ships turned back from the quarantine line. Meanwhile the missile sites neared readiness, and American forces went to DEFCON 2, meaning war involving the Strategic Air Command was imminent. On October 26 a Soviet agent approached ABC News reporter John Scali with an offer: the missiles would go if the United States promised not to invade. That night came a long, emotional letter from Khrushchev with a similar proposal, urging both sides to “untie that knot.” Hope faded the next day, October 27: a second Soviet message demanded the removal of U.S. Jupiter missiles from Turkey, and an American U-2 was shot down over Cuba. Kennedy chose to answer the first letter and ignore the second, while his brother, Attorney General Robert Kennedy, told the Soviet ambassador privately that the Turkish missiles would be removed soon, but could not be part of any public deal.
