Manifest Destiny

The belief that the United States was destined to spread across the continent

1845

“Manifest destiny” was a phrase coined in 1845 by the editor John L. O’Sullivan, who wrote of America’s “manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions.”1,2 It named a belief much older than the phrase: that the United States had a God-given mission to spread its democracy, and that the lands to the west were meant for American settlement.2 The idea drove the annexation of Texas, the acquisition of Oregon, and the Mexican-American War.2,3 It also rested on assumptions of white racial superiority, and it was disastrous for the Native peoples and Mexicans who stood in its way.2 Critics from Emerson to Lincoln mocked it, and the expansion it justified deepened the conflict over slavery.2

Why it matters

Manifest Destiny gave a confident, providential name to the conquest of the continent. The American Yawp concludes that westward expansion “exacerbated the slavery question, pushed Americans toward civil war, and, ultimately, threatened the very mission of American democracy it was designed to aid.”2

Definition

The American Yawp describes Manifest Destiny as a widely held but vaguely defined belief made of three claims. The first was that the strength of American values and institutions justified a moral claim to leadership in the hemisphere. The second was that the lands west of the Mississippi, and later into the Caribbean, were destined for American political and agricultural “improvement.” The third was that God and the Constitution had ordained an irrepressible destiny to redeem and democratize the world.2 Underneath was a vision of the United States as a democratic, agrarian republic that would save the world.2

O’Sullivan’s phrase came in an essay about Texas. Other nations, he wrote, had intruded “in a spirit of hostile interference against us, for the avowed object of thwarting our policy and hampering our power, limiting our greatness and checking the fulfillment of our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions.”1,2 He blamed “England, our old rival and enemy” and France.1

Historical context

The belief dated back to the founding, even if the name did not.2 The State Department’s historians note that by the mid-1800s, the Monroe Doctrine together with ideas of Manifest Destiny “provided precedent and support for U.S. expansion on the American continent.”4 Its champions in the 1840s included the Young America movement, strongest among Democrats, which emphasized American exceptionalism, territorial expansion, and democratic participation and played down divisions over slavery.2 In 1841 the publisher Horace Greeley urged Americans to “Go west, before you are fitted for no life but that of the factory.”2

The idea was bound up with race. The Yawp writes that the dispossession of Native Americans, whether by official policy or by settlers, “depended on the belief in manifest destiny,” and that expansion always rested on the belief that white Americans could best use new lands.2 O’Sullivan himself described the “Anglo-Saxon foot” already on the borders of California and called Mexico “imbecile and distracted.”1

Not all Americans agreed. Emerson praised the generous spirit of the “Young American” but opposed aggressive expansion, and many Whigs, and later Republicans, argued that the United States should lead by example, not conquest.2 In 1859 Abraham Lincoln mocked the “Young American,” who “owns a large part of the world, by right of possessing it; and all the rest by right of wanting it, and intending to have it,” and called him “the unquestioned inventor of ‘Manifest Destiny.’”2

Examples

  • Florida. Southern slaveholders pressed for the conquest of Spanish Florida. In 1816 U.S. troops destroyed the Negro Fort, killing about 270 people, and in 1817 Andrew Jackson invaded Florida.2
  • Indian Removal. More than 60,000 Native Americans were forced west before the Civil War, including the Cherokees on the Trail of Tears.2
  • Texas and Oregon. James K. Polk won the presidency in 1844 on promises of expansion toward Texas, Oregon, and California. Texas was annexed in 1845, and the Oregon Treaty with Britain in 1846 brought the Pacific Northwest under U.S. control.2,3
  • The Mexican-American War (1846–1848). The war gave the United States California and the Southwest.3,2
  • Overland migration. By 1848 about twenty thousand Americans lived west of the Rockies, three-quarters of them in Oregon.2 The Yawp notes that disease, starvation, bad trails, and weather were far greater dangers to migrants than attacks by Native Americans, which were often dramatized.2
  • Beyond the continent. Filibusters such as William Walker tried to seize territory in Mexico and Central America, and Americans looked to Cuba. The Yawp notes that in Cuba manifest destiny for the first time sought territory off the continent.2
