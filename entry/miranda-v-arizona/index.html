Miranda v. Arizona

The ruling that requires police to warn suspects of their rights

1966 · Washington, D.C.; Phoenix, Arizona

In Miranda v. Arizona (1966) the Supreme Court ruled 5 to 4 that the Fifth Amendment’s privilege against self-incrimination applies when police question a suspect in custody.1,3 Writing for the Court, Chief Justice Earl Warren held that before questioning, a person must be warned “that he has a right to remain silent, that any statement he does make may be used as evidence against him, and that he has a right to the presence of an attorney, either retained or appointed.”1 Statements taken without these safeguards could not be used in court.1 The “Miranda warnings” became a routine part of American policing, and in 2000 the Court declined to overrule the decision.4

Why it matters

Miranda v. Arizona turned the privilege against self-incrimination into a concrete routine that applies at the moment a person is most exposed to the power of the state. It remains one of the best known decisions of the Warren Court and a lasting point of debate about how to balance individual rights against effective law enforcement.1,2,4

Historical context

For decades the Court had judged confessions by asking whether they were voluntary, looking at the “totality of circumstances” in each case.1,2 In Escobedo v. Illinois (1964) it had reversed a conviction where police questioned a man at a station without effectively advising him of his right to remain silent or to consult his lawyer, and Miranda was meant to give “concrete constitutional guidelines for law enforcement agencies and courts to follow.”1 The case was part of what the National Constitution Center calls the Warren Court’s “revolution in criminal procedure,” along with Gideon v. Wainwright and Mapp v. Ohio.2

The Court decided four cases together. In each, a suspect was questioned in a room “cut off from the outside world” and was not given “a full and effective warning of his rights at the outset.”1 On March 13, 1963, Ernesto Miranda was arrested at his home and taken to a Phoenix police station, where the complaining witness identified him.1 Two officers took him to “Interrogation Room No. 2” of the detective bureau. They admitted at trial that they had not told him he had a right to have a lawyer present.1 Two hours later they came out with a written confession. A typed paragraph at the top said it was made “with full knowledge of my legal rights.”1 Miranda was convicted of kidnapping and rape and sentenced to 20 to 30 years on each count. The Arizona Supreme Court affirmed, stressing that he had not specifically asked for a lawyer.1

The legal question

Does the Fifth Amendment’s privilege against self-incrimination apply when police question a suspect in custody, and if so, what must police do to protect it?1,3

The ruling

The Court held that the prosecution “may not use statements, whether exculpatory or inculpatory, stemming from custodial interrogation of the defendant unless it demonstrates the use of procedural safeguards effective to secure the privilege against self-incrimination.”1 By custodial interrogation it meant “questioning initiated by law enforcement officers after a person has been taken into custody or otherwise deprived of his freedom of action in any significant way.”1

  • Before questioning, the person must be told of the right to remain silent, that any statement may be used as evidence, and of the right to a lawyer, “either retained or appointed.”1
  • A suspect may waive these rights, but only “voluntarily, knowingly and intelligently,” and a “heavy burden” rests on the government to prove it.1
  • If the suspect “indicates in any manner and at any stage” that he wants a lawyer, “there can be no questioning.”1

The convictions of Miranda, Vignera, and Westover were reversed. In the fourth case the Court affirmed the California Supreme Court, which had already overturned Stewart’s conviction.1

The Court’s reasoning

The interrogation room. Warren drew on police training manuals to show that modern questioning was “psychologically rather than physically oriented.” The manuals advised keeping the suspect “off balance” and even conceding the right to silence as a tactic to win his trust.1 Even without violence, he wrote, “the very fact of custodial interrogation exacts a heavy toll on individual liberty and trades on the weakness of individuals.”1 The records in the four cases showed no “overt physical coercion,” but none of the suspects had been given safeguards to make sure their statements “were truly the product of free choice.”1

A simple, uniform rule. The privilege was “so fundamental” and a warning “so simple” that the Court would not ask in each case whether the defendant already knew his rights.1 Warren noted that the FBI already warned suspects that they could say nothing and could have a lawyer, and he cited its “exemplary record of effective law enforcement.”1 He wrote that the decision was “not intended to hamper the traditional function of police officers,” and that general questioning at a crime scene was not affected.1,2

The dissents. Justice Clark dissented in three of the cases and concurred in the result in the fourth. He wrote that the majority went “too far on too little” and would have kept a case-by-case test of voluntariness.1 Justice Harlan, joined by Stewart and White, called the decision “poor constitutional law” with “harmful consequences for the country at large.”1 Justice White, joined by Harlan and Stewart, found “no significant support” for the rule in the history of the privilege. He warned that in some cases it would “return a killer, a rapist or other criminal to the streets.”1

Consequences

Warnings modeled on the decision became standard in police stations across the country. Their wording is so familiar that the Court later called the warnings “part of our national culture.”4 Critics kept arguing that the rule let guilty people go free. Soon after the decision, Congress passed a law, 18 U.S.C. § 3501, that made the admissibility of a confession in federal court depend only on whether it was voluntary.4

Later interpretation

How later courts, scholars, and the public have judged the case. This is not part of the ruling itself.

The conflict came to a head in Dickerson v. United States. A federal appeals court held that Miranda was not a constitutional rule and that the statute controlled.4 On June 26, 2000, the Supreme Court reversed by 7 to 2. Chief Justice William Rehnquist wrote that “Miranda, being a constitutional decision of this Court, may not be in effect overruled by an Act of Congress, and we decline to overrule Miranda ourselves.”4 He added that Miranda “has become embedded in routine police practice to the point where the warnings have become part of our national culture,” and that later cases had “reduced Miranda’s impact on legitimate law enforcement while reaffirming the decision’s core ruling.”4 Justice Antonin Scalia, joined by Justice Clarence Thomas, dissented.4
