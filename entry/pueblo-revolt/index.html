The Pueblo Revolt

Pueblo nations unite to drive Spanish colonizers out of New Mexico, 1680

August 10 – September 21, 1680 · Santa Fe and the Pueblos of the upper Rio Grande, New Mexico

In August 1680, the Pueblo peoples of New Mexico, who lived in dozens of independent towns and spoke several languages, rose together against eight decades of Spanish rule.5,1 Planned by a religious leader from San Juan Pueblo known as Popé or Po’pay, the revolt killed hundreds of settlers and missionaries, burned the missions, and forced the Spanish out of Santa Fe.4,5 They stayed away for twelve years, and the Penn Museum calls it arguably the most successful Indigenous uprising against a European colonial power in the New World.4 When the Spanish returned in 1692, they did not restore everything they had lost: the forced labor and aggressive suppression of Pueblo religion did not come back.1

Why it matters

The Pueblo Revolt shows that Native peoples did not simply endure colonization. They organized across linguistic and political lines to resist it, and for twelve years they won.4,5

Historical background

Spanish conquistadors first reached the Pueblo country in 1540 under Francisco Vázquez de Coronado, looking for gold.1 In 1598 Juan de Oñate led about four hundred settlers, soldiers, and missionaries from Mexico into New Mexico.6 The riches never appeared, but the Spanish crown kept paying for the colony because of its Franciscan missions.1 Spanish Santa Fe was founded in 1607 and made the capital in 1610, according to the Library of Congress. The American Yawp dates the founding to 1610.1,6 Few Spaniards ever came. By 1680 only about three thousand colonists lived in the province.6

The colony rested on Native labor. Under the encomienda, Pueblo families handed over part of their crops every year. Under the repartimiento, they were forced to work in Spanish households for nothing.1,5 Violence was part of the system. In 1599 Oñate’s soldiers attacked Acoma Pueblo, where twelve Spanish soldiers had been killed. The sources describe the punishment differently: the Library of Congress says over 600 Acoma people were captured and many mutilated, and the American Yawp says nearly half of the roughly 1,500 inhabitants were killed and that Oñate ordered one foot cut off every surviving male over fifteen. Another account says he sentenced males over twelve to twenty years of servitude and men over twenty-five to lose a foot, a punishment that was not carried out, or not fully.1,6,2 Oñate was removed from office and recalled for his cruelty.2

The Pueblos were not one people. EBSCO notes that three language families were spoken in the Rio Grande region alone, and that the villages were autonomous and “rarely acted in unison.”5

Causes and contributing factors

Franciscans, according to EBSCO, “recognized no belief system except their own” and saved their harshest punishments for those who kept the old religion. In 1655, it says, Father Salvador de Guerra had a man at Oraibi whipped, doused with turpentine, and burned to death, and missing Mass could bring a flogging.5 The attack on Pueblo religion was, in EBSCO’s words, “the single greatest cause of the Pueblo Revolt.” The Pueblos believed that harmony with the community and the natural world depended on ceremonies of the kachina spirits in their kivas, and it seemed no coincidence that things went wrong when the priests stopped them.5 Spanish priests also sexually abused Pueblo women.2

Physical suffering made things worse. Droughts, famine, Apache raids, and European diseases cut the Pueblo population from about 50,000 or 60,000 around 1600 to about 17,000 by the 1670s. Measles alone killed 3,000 in 1640, and between 1667 and 1672 people were reduced to boiling leather for food.5,6 Some Pueblos were shipped south as slaves to the silver mines of Mexico.5

The spark came in 1675. Spanish authorities arrested forty-seven Pueblo religious leaders for “sorcery.” The sources disagree on their fate: one says four were sentenced to death and the rest whipped and imprisoned, another that three were hanged and one killed himself.2,5 The Pueblos forced the release of the prisoners, and one of them, Popé of San Juan Pueblo, began to organize a coordinated uprising.2 The Architect of the Capitol adds that Po’pay was among those flogged.3

What happened

Planning. In hiding at Taos Pueblo, Popé built a coalition across languages. He recruited Luis Tupatú, a Tiwa speaker and leader at Picuris, Antonio Malacate, a Keresan spokesman from the southern pueblos, and Francisco El Ollita, a Tewa war leader from San Ildefonso, among others.5 Pedro Naranjo, a San Felipe man who was captured and questioned by the Spanish in 1681, testified that Popé had a cord of knotted maguey fiber passed from pueblo to pueblo. Each town that agreed untied one knot, and the remaining knots counted the days until the rising. Swift young runners carried the cord, on pain of death if they betrayed the secret.2 The Architect of the Capitol says the legend speaks of deerskin strips, and the sources give different days for the planned start: August 11, August 12, or August 13.1,3,5 Naranjo said the Spanish governor imprisoned two accomplices two days before the date, and the Library of Congress says the Spanish learned of the plot on August 9.2,1

The rising. Popé moved the date forward, and on August 10, 1680, the Pueblos attacked. The people of Tesuque killed Father Juan Pío early that morning, and the revolt spread across the country.5,1 They burned churches and killed priests, settlers, women, and children.2,4 The Penn Museum counts 401 settlers and 21 friars dead, and EBSCO counts 400 settlers and 21 of the 33 priests.4,5 A lieutenant governor, Alonso García, gathered the survivors in the south and led them toward El Paso on August 14. On August 15 five hundred Pueblos besieged Governor Antonio de Otermín in Santa Fe and demanded that he free any Native slaves and leave. Otermín attacked, but as more than two thousand warriors gathered and his water supply was cut, he abandoned the capital.5,3 The Library of Congress dates the end of the siege to September 21, while EBSCO says Otermín left with more than a thousand settlers on August 21 and reached El Paso on September 29.1,5

Immediate consequences

For twelve years New Mexico was free of Spanish rule. The Pueblos set about undoing Christianity. According to Naranjo’s testimony, Popé ordered the images of Christ and the saints, the crosses, and the temples destroyed and the bells broken. People washed themselves in the root amole to remove their baptismal names, kivas were rebuilt, and the masked kachina dances resumed.2 Anyone who still kept faith with the priests and the Spaniards was punished, and some were killed.2 A new kachina entered the Pueblo pantheon, known among the Hopi as Yo-we, the “Priest-killer.”5 Naranjo’s testimony, the editor notes, is “filtered through Spanish eyes,” and it calls the Pueblo gods devils, so it has to be read with care.2 EBSCO adds that Popé “had become something of a tyrant himself.”5

The unity did not last. Drought, disease, and Apache raids continued, and the coalition unraveled.5 Popé died in 1692.5 Meanwhile the Spanish launched punitive expeditions from the south, including raids on Santa Ana in 1687 and Zia in 1689, though they could not retake the province.1

Long-term significance

In 1692 a new governor, Diego de Vargas, marched north from El Paso, surrounded Santa Fe with his army and two cannons, and entered it on September 13 after negotiations.1,5 Many historians have called this a “bloodless reconquest.” The Penn Museum calls that inaccurate: Vargas offered pardon to the pueblos if they became vassals of the crown, but many Pueblo lives were lost in later campaigns. In 1694 as many as two thousand people from seven Tewa villages held the mesa-top refuge at Tunyo for nine months against Vargas.4 Revolts in 1694 and 1696 were crushed. But the friars who came back with Vargas could not bring back the forced labor and the harsh suppression of Native religion, and in the 1700s Spaniards and Pueblos began to live together more peacefully.1 In 1820 the Pueblos were granted equal citizenship in the province.1

The revolt also changed the Southwest well beyond the Pueblos. EBSCO reports that Spanish sheep reached the Navajo and became the core of a herding way of life, and that horses, now freed by the hundreds, were widely traded. Within a century they had spread to the Plains nations.5 Pueblo people today see the revolt as a key reason they survived as distinct peoples.5 Others who had settled with Otermín at El Paso lost much of their language, arts, and customs.5 The tricentennial in 1980 was marked by events across the country celebrating the “First American Revolution.”5 In 1997 New Mexico chose Po’pay as its second statue in the National Statuary Hall in the U.S. Capitol. He is the first person in the collection to have been born on what became American soil, and no image or description of him is known.3
