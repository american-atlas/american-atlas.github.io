The Constitution of the United States

The framework of the federal government, written in 1787 and amended twenty-seven times

September 17, 1787 · Philadelphia, Pennsylvania

The Constitution, drafted in secret at a convention in Philadelphia in the summer of 1787 and signed on September 17, replaced the weak Articles of Confederation with a strong national government of three branches, each able to check the others.1,2 It was a document of compromises: between large and small states, between North and South, and between those who wanted a powerful central government and those who feared one.1,2,5 Ratified in 1788 after a fierce public debate, and soon amended by the Bill of Rights, it has governed the United States ever since.2,6

Why it matters

The Constitution created a national government strong enough to tax, regulate commerce, and act directly on citizens, while dividing power to guard against tyranny.2,5 Its compromises over slavery helped hold the convention together, but the National Archives calls the slave-trade bargain “a crippling blow to those working to abolish slavery,” and later antislavery leaders blamed the three-fifths clause for giving slaveholders outsized power in Washington.2,7

Historical circumstances

Under the Articles of Confederation, in force since 1781, the United States was a “league of friendship” among thirteen sovereign states. Congress could not tax, could not effectively regulate commerce, and had little power to settle quarrels between the states.2 The burden of repaying the Revolutionary War debt fell on the states, which raised taxes; paper money flooded the country, and farmers who could not pay were jailed for debt or lost their land.2,5 In 1786 and early 1787, armed farmers in western Massachusetts led by Daniel Shays, a veteran of the Continental Army, closed the courts to stop foreclosures until the state militia dispersed them.5,2 To nationalists like James Madison, Shays’s Rebellion proved that the country needed a stronger government. “Liberty,” Madison warned, “may be endangered by the abuses of liberty as well as the abuses of power.”5

A meeting at Annapolis in September 1786, called to discuss commerce, produced a report by Madison and Alexander Hamilton urging a general convention to revise the Articles, and Congress issued the call.2 Seventy-four delegates were appointed and fifty-five attended. Rhode Island refused to take part, and Patrick Henry stayed home, saying he “smelt a rat.”2 Jefferson and John Adams were abroad on diplomatic missions. Those who came included Washington, the eighty-one-year-old Benjamin Franklin, Hamilton, George Mason, James Wilson, and Gouverneur Morris. Their average age was forty-two.2 The convention opened when a quorum arrived on May 25, 1787, and unanimously elected Washington to preside.1,2 It met in secret, with guards at the doors. Jefferson, from Paris, called the rule of secrecy “so abominable a precedent.”2 Most of what we know about the debates comes from Madison, who sat at the front of the room and took notes every day.2

Principal ideas

A national government. On May 29 Edmund Randolph presented the Virginia Plan, drafted largely by Madison: a government of three branches, legislative, executive, and judicial, “each branch structured to check the other,” with power to veto state laws.2 By mid-June it was clear that the convention would write an entirely new frame of government rather than amend the Articles.1 Small states rallied behind William Paterson’s New Jersey Plan, which would only have strengthened the existing Congress, but the convention rejected it.2 Hamilton’s proposal, modeled on the British government, with an executive and senate serving for life, found no following.2

The Great Compromise. Large states wanted representation by population; small states insisted on the equal vote they had under the Articles. The quarrel grew so bitter that one delegate thought the convention “on the verge of dissolution,” and Franklin proposed daily prayers.2 The solution, proposed by Roger Sherman of Connecticut, gave the House of Representatives members according to population and gave each state two senators.5,2

Slavery. The word “slavery” does not appear in the original Constitution, which speaks only of “Persons,” but the institution runs through it.3,7 On July 12 Oliver Ellsworth proposed counting three-fifths of “all other persons,” a euphemism for enslaved people, for representation and direct taxes. The compromise increased the power of slaveholding states in Congress.2,7 In August, delegates from South Carolina and Georgia struck what George Mason called “a bargain” with New England: Congress could not prohibit the “Migration or Importation” of enslaved people before 1808, and in return the South accepted that navigation laws needed only a simple majority.2,3 A fugitive clause required that a person “held to Service or Labour” who escaped to another state “shall be delivered up.”3 The abolitionist minister Samuel Hopkins called the Constitution a document fit for the flames.2

Checks and balances. The Constitution divides power among Congress, a president, and a Supreme Court, and between the national government and the states. After weighing election by the people, by state legislatures, and by Congress, the delegates settled on the Electoral College, a compromise that gave large states proportional strength and left the choice to the House when no candidate won a majority.2 Article VI made the Constitution, federal laws, and treaties “the supreme Law of the Land,” binding on the judges in every state.3 Article VII provided that ratification by conventions in nine states would be enough to establish it. That choice allowed the nationalists to go around the state legislatures, where many politicians stood to lose power.3,2

The final draft. A Committee of Detail produced a first draft on August 6. Gouverneur Morris, the chief architect of the Committee of Style’s final text, later boasted that the Constitution “was written by the Fingers which wrote this letter.” Its preamble begins: “We the People of the United States, in Order to form a more perfect Union.”2,3 On September 17 Franklin, in a speech read by James Wilson, appealed for unity, and thirty-nine delegates signed. Mason, Randolph, and Gerry refused to sign; Mason had protested that the Constitution lacked a bill of rights.2

Ratification and the Bill of Rights

The fight over ratification was fought in newspapers, taverns, and state conventions.2 Federalists, who supported the Constitution, faced Anti-Federalists, who feared that a distant, powerful government would be dominated by the wealthy and would swallow the states. The Anti-Federalist “Centinel” warned that “the United States are to be melted down” into a despotic empire.2 In New York, Hamilton, Madison, and John Jay answered with the eighty-five essays of the Federalist Papers, most of them written by Hamilton. Madison argued there that a large republic would be safer than a small one, because no single faction could easily dominate it.2

Pennsylvania ratified on December 12, 1787, by 46 to 23, and by January 1788 five states had approved. Massachusetts followed on February 6 by 187 to 168, after Federalists agreed to recommend amendments.2 Rhode Island rejected the Constitution in a popular referendum by ten to one. In the summer of 1788 New Hampshire became the ninth state to ratify, and Virginia and New York followed by narrow margins.2 Hamilton believed that most New Yorkers opposed the Constitution, and the National Archives judges it probable that a majority of Americans did. Only the promise of amendments secured victory.2

The call for a bill of rights had been the Anti-Federalists’ strongest weapon; even Jefferson wrote that a bill of rights was “what the people are entitled to against every government on earth.”2 In the First Congress, Madison guided seventeen amendments through the House; the Senate cut them to twelve, and Congress proposed those to the states on September 25, 1789.2,6 Ten were ratified by December 15, 1791, and became the Bill of Rights. One of the others, on congressional pay, was finally ratified in 1992 as the Twenty-seventh Amendment.6

The parchment

The four-page signed Constitution has survived in far better condition than the Declaration of Independence. It was kept by the State Department, hidden in Leesburg, Virginia, when the British burned Washington in 1814, moved to the Library of Congress in 1921 in a Model T truck, sent to Fort Knox during World War II, and transferred to the National Archives in an armored procession on December 13, 1952. It is displayed there today.2,1
