Thomas Jefferson

Author of the Declaration of Independence and third president

1743–1826

Thomas Jefferson wrote the Declaration of Independence, served as the first secretary of state and the third president, doubled the size of the nation with the Louisiana Purchase, and founded the University of Virginia.1,2,9 He was the leading voice of a republic of independent farmers, limited government, and religious freedom.1,4 He was also a lifelong slaveholder who owned more than 600 people over his lifetime, freed only a handful, and most likely fathered six children with Sally Hemings, an enslaved woman in his household.1,7 As Encyclopedia Virginia puts it, each generation has claimed him “on both sides of almost every important question.”1

Why it matters

Jefferson wrote the creed of the American nation, that “all men are created equal,” and later Americans have used it against the very inequalities he lived with.4 As president he cut the federal government, doubled the nation’s territory, and built the first opposition party to win power peacefully. As a slaveholder and a theorist of Black inferiority, he embodies the contradiction between liberty and slavery at the heart of the early republic.1,4,2

Early life and context

Jefferson was born on April 13, 1743 (April 2 by the Old Style calendar), at Shadwell, on the Rivanna River in what became Albemarle County, Virginia.1,2 His father, Peter Jefferson, was a surveyor and planter who helped make the famous Fry-Jefferson map of Virginia; his mother, Jane Randolph, came from one of the colony’s most powerful families.1 When his father died in 1757, the fourteen-year-old inherited more than 5,000 acres and about twenty enslaved people.1 At the College of William and Mary he studied mathematics and science with the Scottish professor William Small and law with George Wythe. He learned Latin, Greek, French, Italian, and Spanish and became perhaps the best-read lawyer in Virginia.1,2

In 1769 he began building Monticello, his house on a hilltop overlooking Shadwell. He would remodel it for four decades, and his enslaved craftsmen built much of its fine furniture.1,2 On January 1, 1772, he married Martha Wayles Skelton, a young widow. When her father, John Wayles, died in 1773, Jefferson inherited more than 11,000 acres, 135 enslaved people, including the Hemings family, and a large debt.1 Of their six children only two daughters, Martha and Mary, reached adulthood. Martha Jefferson died in 1782 after a difficult childbirth, on September 6 according to Encyclopedia Virginia (the Miller Center gives November 6), and Jefferson reportedly promised her never to remarry.1,7,2

Major actions and achievements

Revolutionary. A member of the House of Burgesses from 1769, Jefferson won fame in 1774 with A Summary View of the Rights of British America, which argued that Parliament had no authority over the colonies at all.1,2 In the Second Continental Congress he was named to the committee to draft a declaration of independence. The other members deferred to him; John Adams admired his writing, and Franklin said he would never write anything for others to edit.2 Jefferson’s draft, revised by Congress, which struck out his attack on the king for the slave trade, was adopted on July 4, 1776.1,2 His authorship did not become widely known until the 1790s.1

Virginia reformer. Back in Virginia, Jefferson led a revision of the state’s laws. His bills abolished entail and primogeniture, which had kept great estates intact, and his plan for public schools failed.1,2 His Statute for Religious Freedom, carried through the legislature by James Madison, passed on January 16, 1786, and separated church and state in Virginia.1,2

Wartime governor. Elected governor in 1779, Jefferson had little power under the state constitution, and his term was a series of disasters. In January 1781, partly because Jefferson was slow to call out the militia, Benedict Arnold’s British force occupied Richmond.1 That June British cavalry raided Charlottesville and Jefferson barely escaped capture at Monticello. Critics accused him of cowardice, charges Encyclopedia Virginia calls “almost certainly unfounded,” but the legislature ordered an inquiry, which later thanked him.1,2

Writer and diplomat. In Notes on the State of Virginia, written after his governorship and published in 1785–1787, Jefferson praised farmers as “the chosen people of God,” defended American nature against European claims of inferiority, and denounced slavery as an evil.1 In the same book he wrote that Black people were “inferior to the whites in the endowments both of body and mind.”2,1 (The Miller Center calls Notes his only book; Encyclopedia Virginia calls that description inaccurate.)2,1 In Congress in 1784 he drafted a plan for governing the western territories that would admit them as equal states and ban slavery there after 1800. Congress dropped the slavery ban by one vote, but kept the principle of admitting new states as equals.1,2 From 1785 to 1789 he served as minister to France, where he became Europe’s leading interpreter of America, designed the Virginia State Capitol on a Roman temple, and urged Madison to add a bill of rights to the new Constitution.1,2

Secretary of state and party leader. As Washington’s first secretary of state (1790–1793), Jefferson clashed with Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton. He helped broker the compromise that traded federal assumption of state debts for a capital on the Potomac, but he came to believe that Hamilton was a secret monarchist.1 With Madison he organized the Republican Party, which favored states’ rights, a strict reading of the Constitution, and sympathy for revolutionary France.1 After finishing second to Adams in 1796 and becoming vice president, he secretly wrote the Kentucky Resolutions of 1798, which claimed a state’s right to nullify the Alien and Sedition Acts.1,3

President. In 1800 Jefferson and his running mate, Aaron Burr, tied with 73 electoral votes each, and the Federalist House elected Jefferson only on the thirty-sixth ballot, on February 17, 1801.1,3 It was a peaceful transfer of power from one party to another. In his inaugural address he declared, “We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists.”1,8 He walked to his inauguration, dressed simply, and sent his messages to Congress in writing.1 His administration cut the army and navy, repealed internal taxes, pardoned the last prisoners under the Sedition Act, and reduced the national debt from $80 million to $57 million, even after paying for Louisiana.4,1 He never vetoed a bill.1

In 1803 Napoleon offered to sell all of Louisiana. Jefferson’s envoys agreed to pay $15 million for 828,000 square miles, doubling the size of the nation. Jefferson had doubts that the Constitution allowed the purchase, but he set them aside.1,9 He sent Meriwether Lewis and William Clark to explore the new lands, and their expedition crossed to the Pacific and back between 1804 and 1806.1,9 He also sent the navy against Tripoli in the Barbary War rather than pay higher tribute.1,9 Reelected easily in 1804, he struggled in his second term as Britain and France seized American ships. After a British warship fired on the USS Chesapeake in 1807, Jefferson chose economic pressure over war: the Embargo Act of December 1807 banned American foreign trade. It failed. Exports fell from $108 million to $22 million, smuggling flourished, and Congress replaced it as Jefferson left office.1,9 Following Washington’s example, he refused a third term.1

Beliefs and ideas

Jefferson believed in a republic of independent landowning farmers, a “wise and frugal Government, which shall restrain men from injuring one another” but otherwise leave them free.8,4 He favored strict limits on federal power, freedom of religion, public education, and the spread of property.2,1 A deist, he believed in a creator who governed the world through natural laws, and he opposed any official religion.2

On slavery he wrote eloquently against the institution while holding hundreds of people in bondage. He believed that Black and white Americans could not live together as equals in freedom and that emancipation had to be combined with deportation.1 In 1820 he called the Missouri crisis “a fire bell in the night” and wrote of slavery, “we have the wolf by the ear, and we can neither hold him, nor safely let him go.”1

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Jefferson’s slaveholding is the central controversy of his reputation. Over his lifetime he owned more than 600 people, mostly through inheritance and natural increase. He sold people to raise money, sold others out of state as punishment, sanctioned whippings, and pursued those who escaped.1 Beginning in Paris, by the Miller Center’s account, or in the late 1780s or early 1790s, by Encyclopedia Virginia’s, he had a long sexual relationship with Sally Hemings, the enslaved half-sister of his late wife, who had come to Paris at fourteen as a companion to his daughter.2,1,7 In 2000 the Thomas Jefferson Foundation concluded, based on documents, the oral histories of Monticello’s Black families, and DNA studies, that Jefferson “most likely was the father of all six of Sally Hemings’s children.” He freed or allowed to leave all four who survived.1 Federalists exposed the relationship as a national scandal during his presidency; Jefferson kept a public silence.3

His presidency had its critics too. Federalists called the Louisiana Purchase unconstitutional.3 He led attempts to remove Federalist judges by impeachment, succeeding against John Pickering but failing against Samuel Chase.4 He pressed treason charges against Aaron Burr, who was acquitted in 1807, and his embargo devastated the economy.1,9 His Kentucky Resolutions were later invoked in the nullification crisis of the 1830s and by opponents of civil rights enforcement in the 1960s.1

Legacy

In retirement at Monticello, Jefferson sold his library of roughly 6,700 volumes to Congress for $23,950 in 1815 to replace the books burned by the British. It became the core of the modern Library of Congress.1 His last great project was the University of Virginia, chartered in 1819 and opened in 1825. He designed its buildings, chose its faculty and books, and left out a chapel.1,5 In 1812 he and John Adams reconciled and began one of the great correspondences in American letters.1

Jefferson lived beyond his means all his life, and debts left his estate insolvent. At his death he owed $107,273.63, and in 1827 more than 100 enslaved people were sold at auction to pay his creditors.1,5 He died at Monticello on July 4, 1826, fifty years to the day after the Declaration was adopted, a few hours before John Adams.1 He chose his own epitaph, naming only the Declaration, the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom, and the University of Virginia.1 “If Jefferson was wrong, America is wrong,” the biographer James Parton wrote in 1874. “If America is right, Jefferson was right.”1
