The Columbian Exchange

The transfer of plants, animals, people, and diseases between the hemispheres after 1492

1492 – c. 1600s

The “Columbian Exchange,” a term coined by the historian Alfred W. Crosby, describes the enormous transfer of crops, livestock, people, and microbes between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres that followed 1492. Maize and potatoes transformed diets in Europe, Africa, and Asia; horses, cattle, and wheat transformed the Americas. Most devastating of all, Old World diseases such as smallpox and measles killed a large share of the Native peoples of the Americas.

Key facts

Date
1492 – c. 1600s
Dating note
The exchange began with Columbus’s voyages and continued for centuries.
From the Americas
Maize, white and sweet potatoes, manioc, peanuts, tomatoes, squash, pineapples, avocados
From Europe, Africa, and Asia
Wheat, rice, barley, sugar, horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, goats
Diseases to the Americas
Smallpox, measles, malaria, yellow fever, typhoid, and others
Term coined by
Alfred W. Crosby, in his 1972 book The Columbian Exchange

Why it matters

The Columbian Exchange reshaped ecosystems, diets, and populations on every inhabited continent. For the Americas it meant both the arrival of new livestock and crops and the greatest population collapse in human history, a central reason why European colonization succeeded.

Definition

The Columbian Exchange is the movement of living things (plants, animals, and pathogens) and of people between the Old World and the New after Columbus’s voyages reconnected hemispheres that had developed separately for thousands of years. It was not a fair trade: some exchanges brought abundance, others catastrophe, and their effects fell very unevenly.

Historical context

Before 1492, the Americas and the Eastern Hemisphere had very different inventories of life. The Americas had few domesticated animals (dogs, llamas, guinea pigs, and a few fowl) and no wild horses or cattle to tame. The Old World had many herd animals, which, as Crosby explains, were among humanity’s richest sources of disease microorganisms. Because people in the Old World had long lived in dense populations alongside livestock, they carried diseases (smallpox, measles, malaria, yellow fever, typhoid, cholera, plague) to which Native Americans had no acquired immunity.

The result was a demographic disaster. Epidemics often spread ahead of European settlers themselves, as in coastal New England, where diseases spread by earlier English visitors devastated the Wampanoag before the Pilgrims landed at Plymouth. Population collapse made conquest and colonization far easier and helped drive Europeans to import enslaved Africans as laborers.

Examples

  • New foods for the world. American crops (maize, potatoes, manioc, peanuts) became staples in Europe, Africa, and Asia, supporting population growth across the globe.
  • Sugar and slavery. Sugar cane, brought to the Caribbean and Brazil, created plantation economies built on the labor of enslaved Africans.
  • Tobacco. An American plant became the cash crop of colonial Virginia after 1612 and a global commodity.
