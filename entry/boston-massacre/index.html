The Boston Massacre

British soldiers fire into a Boston crowd, killing five, in March 1770

March 5, 1770 · King Street, Boston, Massachusetts

On the evening of March 5, 1770, a crowd confronted a small group of British soldiers outside the Custom House on King Street in Boston. Taunted and pelted with snowballs, ice, and sticks, the soldiers fired, killing five men, among them Crispus Attucks, a sailor of African and Indigenous ancestry, and wounding six.1,3 Patriots called it a massacre of civilians; loyalists called it self-defense against a mob.1 John Adams defended the soldiers in court and won acquittals for most of them, but the killings became one of the most powerful stories of the coming Revolution.6,8

Why it matters

The Boston Massacre was the first time British soldiers shed colonists’ blood in the quarrel that became the American Revolution.1 Patriots turned it into propaganda and an annual day of remembrance, while the trials that followed showed that even hated soldiers could get a fair hearing in a Massachusetts court, a principle later written into the Sixth Amendment.6,8

Historical background

The quarrel that ended in bloodshed on King Street began with money. Britain emerged from the French and Indian War victorious but deeply in debt, and Parliament decided that the colonies should help pay for the empire that protected them.1 Beginning with the Sugar Act in 1764 and continuing with the Stamp Act and the Townshend Acts, it laid duties on sugar, paper, lead, glass, and tea, goods that had become part of colonists’ sense of a comfortable, genteel life.9 Colonists who had long taxed themselves through their own assemblies saw these measures as a threat to their liberty, and Boston became home to some of the loudest opposition and the largest protests.1

In 1767 Parliament passed the Townshend Acts, and to restore order in rebellious Boston the government sent the army.1 On October 1, 1768, a British fleet arrived and hundreds of soldiers marched into the town.1 Rather than calming Boston, the troops inflamed it. Townspeople felt they were living under the occupation of a foreign army; soldiers were insulted and taunted on patrol, and verbal abuse soon became fistfights in the streets.1 Middling families worried that they might be forced to lodge soldiers in their homes, and men who made their living from the sea had particular grievances: sailors feared being seized for the Royal Navy, and off-duty soldiers competed with dockworkers and ropemakers for scarce jobs.4

Causes and contributing factors

By the winter of 1769–1770 the confrontations had become personal and violent. For months soldiers and maritime workers traded insults and punches along the wharves.4 In February 1770 an eleven-year-old boy, Christopher Seider, was killed while protesting with a group outside the home of a loyalist, and thousands of Bostonians turned out for his funeral.1 Tension and distrust between civilians and soldiers grew sharper still.1

The Custom House on King Street, where the hated duties were collected, had become a symbol of British taxation.1 Behind the street confrontations stood deeper divisions as well. Wealthy merchants had been the first to suffer from and protest the new taxes, and when the Sons of Liberty organized a boycott of British goods, small shopkeepers, mechanics, and artisans were drawn in too.4 By 1770, resentment of the army was shared across Boston society, even if different groups resented it for different reasons.4

What happened

On the evening of March 5, 1770, just eleven days after Seider’s death, Private Hugh White of the 29th Regiment of Foot stood sentry outside the Custom House.1 When a young wigmaker’s apprentice, Edward Garrick, shouted that a British officer had not paid his bill, White reprimanded him, the two argued, and White struck the young man on the side of the head with his musket.1

Word spread through the streets, and a crowd began to gather around the lone sentry. White called for help. Captain Thomas Preston marched seven soldiers of the 29th to the Custom House with fixed bayonets.1 Church bells rang, people poured out of their homes, and the crowd may have grown to three or four hundred.1 Witnesses later placed Crispus Attucks, a tall sailor of African and Indigenous descent, at the head of a group of sailors carrying clubs.3 People in the crowd shouted insults, threw snowballs, ice, and sticks, and dared the soldiers to fire; the soldiers loaded their muskets in full view.1,3

At some point a club or stick struck one of the soldiers and knocked him down. He got up and fired into the crowd, and seconds later the others fired too.1 Eleven men were hit. Attucks, struck by two musket balls in the chest, died instantly; Samuel Gray and James Caldwell also died that night, seventeen-year-old Samuel Maverick died the next morning, and Patrick Carr died of his wounds nearly two weeks later.3,6 When the smoke cleared, Preston ordered his men to cease fire and called out more soldiers to guard the Custom House.1 The acting governor, Thomas Hutchinson, came to the scene, calmed the crowd, and promised justice.1

Immediate consequences

The next day Preston and the eight soldiers were arrested and jailed.1,2 To prevent further violence, Hutchinson moved the troops out of the town to Castle William, a fort on an island in Boston Harbor.2 Both sides began at once to fight over the meaning of the night. Newspapers, depositions, broadsides, letters, and diaries told the story in competing ways, as a “Horrid Massacre” or as an “Unhappy Disturbance.”2 Paul Revere sold an engraving, copied from a drawing by Henry Pelham, under the provocative title “The Bloody Massacre,” showing a line of soldiers firing in unison on a peaceful crowd; broadsides decorated with black coffins appeared in newspapers.1 Because Attucks and Caldwell had no family in Boston, their bodies lay in state at Faneuil Hall, and the funerals of the victims became huge public demonstrations.1,3

Finding lawyers for the accused was hard. The day after the shooting, a loyalist merchant, James Forrest, approached John Adams on Preston’s behalf, explaining that the captain wished for counsel and could get none; two other lawyers, Robert Auchmuty and Josiah Quincy Jr., said they would serve only if Adams joined them.6 Adams agreed, risking his reputation, because he believed that every accused person in a free country deserved a lawyer and a fair trial.6

The trials were delayed for months, partly by court schedules and partly by Hutchinson, who hoped tempers would cool.7 Preston was tried first, beginning on October 24, 1770. The prosecution, Robert Treat Paine and Samuel Quincy, had no physical evidence and had to rely on eyewitnesses, whose stories contradicted each other; some could not even agree on what Preston had worn.6 Several witnesses swore that the captain had stood in front of his men when they fired, which a commander giving an order to shoot would hardly do. One of them, a free Black man named Newton Prince, testified that he “heard no orders given to fire.”6 On October 30 the jury found Preston not guilty.7

The trial of the eight soldiers began on November 27, with Adams now leading the defense.6 More than forty witnesses testified. The most striking evidence came from a dead man: the surgeon John Jeffries repeated the dying words of Patrick Carr, who had told him that he had often seen soldiers fire on crowds in his native Ireland but had never seen them bear half so much before they fired.6 Samuel Quincy argued for the prosecution that the soldiers could have retreated instead of killing; Adams answered that if the soldiers were attacked and their lives endangered, the law allowed them to defend themselves, and that facts, not passions, must decide the case.6 On December 5, nine months to the day after the shooting, a jury with no Boston residents acquitted six soldiers and found Hugh Montgomery and Matthew Kilroy guilty of manslaughter.7 Both escaped hanging by claiming “benefit of clergy,” an old rule that spared first offenders, and were branded with an “M” on the hand where the thumb meets the palm.7

Long-term significance

Bostonians did not let the night be forgotten. From the first anniversary on, they marked each March 5 with public orations, poems, and pictures; speakers such as James Lovell and, in 1775, Joseph Warren used the anniversary to rally opposition to Britain.8 These commemorations kept anger at the army alive until fighting broke out at Lexington and Concord in 1775.1 Long afterward, John Adams wrote that no battle of the war had been more important than “the battle of King Street,” and in 1786 he remarked that the massacre had laid the foundation of American independence.1,6

The trials left a legacy of their own. They showed Britain that a colonial court could give unpopular defendants a fair hearing, yet in 1774, after the Boston Tea Party, Parliament passed the Administration of Justice Act, which took certain trials out of the colonists’ own hands and deepened their mistrust of British rule.6 The right Adams had defended, the accused’s right to counsel and a fair trial, was later written into the Sixth Amendment of the Bill of Rights.6,11

Crispus Attucks became the best-remembered victim, although his own life is barely documented. Early reports called him Michael Johnson, perhaps an alias; a 1750 Boston newspaper advertisement sought a runaway enslaved man named “Crispas” from Framingham, but contemporary sources do not identify Attucks as enslaved, and how he gained his freedom is unknown.3 In the 1850s Black abolitionists in Boston, led by William Cooper Nell, honored him as the first martyr of the Revolution, and activists later persuaded the city to recognize March 5 as Crispus Attucks Day.3 The site of the shooting, beneath the balcony of the Old State House, is marked today by a circle of granite pavers on the Freedom Trail.5
