The Missouri Compromise

The 1820 bargain that drew a line across the West between slavery and freedom

February 1819 – 1821 · Washington, D.C.

When Missouri asked to join the Union as a slave state in 1819, it set off the first great national crisis over slavery.1 The Missouri Compromise of 1820 admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state, keeping the balance between them in the Senate, and banned slavery in the rest of the Louisiana Purchase north of latitude 36°30′.2,3 The line held for thirty-four years.2 But the crisis, as the American Yawp puts it, “made the sectional nature of American politics impossible to ignore,” and the aging Thomas Jefferson heard in it “a fire bell in the night.”1,4

Why it matters

The Missouri Compromise was the first great sectional bargain over slavery. It preserved the Union for a time by dividing the West between slavery and freedom, but it also made slavery’s expansion the central question of national politics.1,2

Historical background

Since 1789 Virginia had wielded more influence in the federal government than any other state; four of the first five presidents were Virginians.1 As the northern states gradually ended slavery while the southern states came to depend on it even more, northerners grew wary of a southern voting bloc united by the defense of slavery.1 The Louisiana Purchase had added an immense territory with no settled rule on slavery.2

Missouri, a territory carved out of the Purchase, applied for statehood in 1819. It already held more than ten thousand enslaved people and was poised to join the southern bloc in Congress.1

Causes and contributing factors

  • The Tallmadge Amendment. In February 1819 Representative James Tallmadge of New York proposed admitting Missouri only if no more enslaved people were brought in and the children of those already there were freed at twenty-five. Tallmadge argued that slavery mocked the Declaration of Independence.1,3
  • A sectional vote. Nearly all northern congressmen, who had a majority in the House, voted for the amendment and it passed; the Senate quickly defeated it.1,3
  • Morals and power. Opponents of slavery in Missouri, the Yawp notes, acted from moral conviction but also wanted to keep a sectional balance of power.1

What happened

When Congress reconvened, Senator Jesse Thomas of Illinois proposed a bargain, and Henry Clay of Kentucky helped push it through, earning the nickname “the Great Compromiser.”1 Maine, long part of Massachusetts, was seeking statehood, so the two admissions were paired: Missouri as a slave state, Maine as a free state.1,3 Slavery was banned in the rest of the Louisiana Purchase north of 36°30′, Missouri’s southern border, while the land to the south, organized as Arkansas Territory, stayed open to slavery.1,3 A conference committee reported the compromise on March 1, 1820.2

A second dispute broke out when Missouri submitted a constitution that barred free Black people from living in the state. Northern congressmen said this violated the Constitution’s guarantee that citizens of each state enjoy the rights of citizens in all states. Congress resolved it by requiring Missouri to promise that no law would be passed under that clause in violation of those rights, and Missouri’s legislature agreed.3

Immediate consequences

The crisis ended peacefully, but it split the Democratic-Republican Party along sectional lines.1 Many Americans were alarmed at how readily politicians spoke of disunion and even civil war.1 Jefferson warned that “a geographical line, coinciding with a marked principle, moral and political,” would never be erased, and that every new irritation “will mark it deeper and deeper.”4

Long-term significance

For a generation the 36°30′ line kept an uneasy peace. Congress repealed it in the Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854, and in 1857 the Supreme Court’s Dred Scott decision declared the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional.2 The question it had postponed, whether slavery would spread west, would, as the Yawp says, “cause worse trouble in years ahead,” leading to the Civil War.1

Jefferson’s letter

In April 1820 Jefferson wrote to John Holmes, thanking him for a letter to his constituents on the Missouri question. He called the Missouri question “the knell of the Union” and said it was “hushed indeed for the moment. but this is a reprieve only, not a final sentence.” On slavery itself he wrote, “we have the wolf by the ear, and we can neither hold him, nor safely let him go. justice is in one scale, and self-preservation in the other.”4 In the same letter he argued that spreading slavery over a wider area would make it easier to end.4

What exactly did Tallmadge propose?

Sources describe the amendment slightly differently. The American Yawp says it would have freed children born to enslaved people in Missouri at age twenty-five; a Vanderbilt University course summary says it provided for “the gradual emancipation of those already there.”1,3 Both agree it would have barred bringing more enslaved people into the state.
