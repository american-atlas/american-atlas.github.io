The Constitutional Convention

Fifty-five delegates meet in secret to redesign the American government

May 14 – September 17, 1787 · Pennsylvania State House (Independence Hall), Philadelphia

In the summer of 1787, delegates from twelve of the thirteen states met in secret in Philadelphia, officially to revise the Articles of Confederation.4,2 Instead, they wrote a new Constitution. They argued bitterly over how to represent large and small states, how much power to give the central government, and how to count enslaved people, and they settled each dispute by compromise.1,2 Only 55 of the 74 delegates appointed ever attended.2 Three of those who stayed to the end refused to sign. The document the others signed on September 17 became the framework of the United States government.2,5

Why it matters

In four months the convention replaced a failing confederation with a national government designed to be strong enough to govern but divided enough to restrain itself.1,2,4

Historical background

By 1786 many leaders believed the union was failing. Under the Articles of Confederation, in force since 1781 as a “league of friendship” among thirteen sovereign states, the central government could not tax, could not effectively regulate commerce, and had little power to settle quarrels between the states.2,3 Congress faced an empty treasury and a flood of paper money. Many farmers were jailed for debt or lost their farms for unpaid taxes.2 The burden of paying Revolutionary War debts fell on the states, which owed money to the lenders who held their war bonds.4

In 1786 farmers in western Massachusetts, led by the veteran Daniel Shays, blocked courts to stop foreclosures. They saw their cause as an extension of the “Spirit of 1776.” The state militia put them down in January 1787.4,2 The rebellion frightened wealthy Americans. George Washington wrote to Madison from Mount Vernon: “Wisdom and good examples are necessary at this time to rescue the political machine from the impending storm.”2 For Madison, the lesson was that “liberty may be endangered by the abuses of liberty as well as the abuses of power.”4 A meeting at Annapolis in September 1786 had called for a broader convention. Madison and Alexander Hamilton wrote its report, which called on Congress to summon delegates from all the states to revise the Articles, and Congress issued the call even though many saw the report as a usurpation of its authority.2

Causes and contributing factors

James Madison had spent years studying the history of confederacies and republics and was convinced that the Articles had to be replaced.2,4 The research gave him a bold idea. Classical learning said that a republic could survive only in a small, uniform state, but Madison believed that an “extended republic” of diverse people could work.4 Other leaders wanted a government that could raise revenue, pay debts, and regulate trade. Some delegates came mainly to protect their states’ interests.2

Some of the era’s major figures were missing. Thomas Jefferson and John Adams were abroad, John Jay was at the Foreign Office in New York, and Patrick Henry, who said he “smelt a rat,” refused to attend. He rightly suspected that Madison wanted a powerful central government that would weaken the state legislatures.2 Rhode Island, whose leaders saw a conspiracy against the existing government, sent no one.2 Washington, who was suffering from rheumatism and doubted the convention would accomplish much, agonized for months before agreeing to attend.2 The delegates averaged 42 years of age. Benjamin Franklin, at 81, was the oldest, and Jonathan Dayton, at 27, the youngest.2

What happened

Opening and secrecy. The convention formally got under way on May 25, 1787, when a quorum of seven states was finally present. Dirt was spread on the street outside the State House to muffle the noise of carriages, and guards kept the curious away. On the motion of Robert Morris, Washington was unanimously elected president of the convention.2,1 No reporters or visitors were allowed. Jefferson, writing from Paris, thought it “so abominable a precedent” to tie up the delegates’ tongues.2 Madison sat in front of the presiding officer and recorded the debates, missing no day and no major speech, and he said later that sitting in the stifling hall almost killed him.2

The Virginia Plan (May 29). Edmund Randolph opened the debate with a plan the Virginians had drawn up. It proposed a government with a legislature, an executive, and a judiciary, each checking the others, and a national veto over state laws. The Yawp adds that the legislature would have two houses, with states represented according to population or tax base.2,4 Randolph admitted that it “meant a strong consolidated union in which the idea of states should be nearly annihilated.”2 By mid-June, as the National Archives puts it, “rather than amend the existing Articles, the Convention would draft an entirely new frame of government.”1 The delegates also agreed that the new Constitution should be ratified by conventions of the people, not by the state legislatures, where officeholders had power to lose.2

The New Jersey Plan and Hamilton (June). On June 13 delegates from the smaller states rallied around William Paterson’s plan, which would only have revised the Articles to give Congress the power to raise revenue and regulate commerce. After three days of debate the convention voted it down.2 On June 18 Hamilton presented his own plan, with an executive and senators who would serve for life or during good behavior. He called the British government “the best in the world.” It won no following.2

The great deadlock. By late June the large and small states were arguing over representation in the legislature, and the debate descended into threats and accusations. Franklin urged the delegates to begin each day with prayer, but the convention, Hugh Williamson noted, had no money to pay a preacher.2 On June 29 the convention voted that the House of Representatives would be based on population. A motion for equal state representation in the Senate ended in a tie, and one member said the convention “was on the verge of dissolution, scarce held together by the strength of an hair.”2 On July 10 Washington complained that he regretted having had “any agency” in the proceedings.2

The compromises on representation and slavery. On July 12 Oliver Ellsworth proposed that House seats be based on the free population plus three-fifths of “all other persons.” In the following week the members agreed that direct taxes would follow representation, and then approved equal representation in the Senate.2 The Yawp credits Roger Sherman with the compromise and notes that the final version gave each state two senators who voted independently.4 A Committee of Detail wrote a first draft, which the convention took up on August 6.2 The delegates then fought over commerce. Charles Pinckney warned that if Congress controlled trade, the southern states would be “nothing more than overseers for the Northern States.” On August 21 the dispute became bound up with slavery when Luther Martin proposed a tax on imported slaves. John Rutledge of South Carolina insisted that “Interest alone is the governing principle with nations.”2 Mason opposed unlimited importation but also wanted federal protection for existing slave property. He later described what happened as “a bargain”: South Carolina and Georgia accepted a simple majority for navigation laws, and in exchange New England delegates supported allowing the slave trade to continue for twenty years.2 The Constitution says that Congress may not prohibit the importation of “such Persons as any of the States now existing shall think proper to admit” before 1808. It counts “three fifths of all other Persons” for representation and taxes and requires that a person “held to Service or Labour” who escapes be “delivered up” on claim.6

The executive and the final draft. The delegates considered electing the executive by the people, by state legislatures, by governors, and by Congress. The result, the National Archives says, was the Electoral College, “a master stroke of compromise.” Mason predicted that the House would choose the president 19 times out of 20.2 On the question of a single executive, the Yawp notes that James Wilson’s proposal on June 1 was contentious because it conjured up images of an elected monarchy.4 On August 31 Mason said he “would sooner chop off his right hand than put it to the Constitution as it now stands.” He wanted a bill of rights and a second convention, and the motion was voted down.2 A Committee of Style and Arrangement, with Gouverneur Morris as its chief architect, produced the final text, which went before the convention on September 12.2

Signing (September 15 to 17). On September 15 Randolph, Mason, and Gerry proposed that state conventions make amendments to be considered by a second general convention. They were soundly rebuffed, and every state delegation voted aye.2 On September 17 James Wilson read a speech by Franklin. “I confess that there are several parts of this constitution which I do not at present approve,” Franklin wrote, but he agreed to it “because I expect no better, and because I am not sure, that it is not the best.” He urged every member who still had objections to “doubt a little of his own infallibility.”5 Before the vote, Nathaniel Gorham proposed changing the ratio of representatives from one for every 40,000 people to one for every 30,000. Washington, who as presiding officer had until then kept his views on questions before the House to himself, said that he wished the change to be adopted.5 Randolph said that he refused to sign because he feared that nine states would fail to ratify the plan. Mason and Gerry also withheld their signatures. The other delegates present signed and the convention adjourned at 4 p.m., then dined at City Tavern.5,2

Immediate consequences

The Constitution was sent to Congress, then sitting in New York, with a cover letter from Washington, and to the states for ratification by special conventions.4 Nine states had to approve it. The debate that followed was fierce. Federalists, who supported the Constitution, and Anti-Federalists, who feared a distant government of the wealthy, wrote in newspapers across the country.2 The Anti-Federalist essays signed “Centinel” warned that the states would be “melted down” into a despotism of the “well-born.”2 Hamilton, Madison, and John Jay answered with the 85 essays of the Federalist Papers.2 The absence of a bill of rights became a rallying point for opponents, because the convention had voted down Mason’s proposal.4 Delaware, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Georgia, and Connecticut had ratified by January 9, 1788. Massachusetts followed on February 6 by 187 to 168, after Federalists agreed to recommend amendments, and Rhode Island’s voters rejected the Constitution ten to one in a referendum in March.2 New Hampshire became the ninth state to ratify, and Congress learned of it on July 2, 1788. Virginia and New York ratified within two months, by very close margins. Hamilton thought that most New Yorkers actually opposed it, and the National Archives says only the promise of amendments secured Federalist victory.2

Long-term significance

The convention created a durable framework of government built on compromise between large and small states, between nation and states, and between North and South.1,2 The National Archives calls the Constitution “a model of cooperative statesmanship and the art of compromise.”1 The bargains over slavery also wrote protections for the institution into the Constitution, and the National Archives article quotes a contemporary critic, the minister Samuel Hopkins, who called the document a sellout and fit for the flames.2 Because the debates were secret, the record that survives depends on Madison’s notes, which remain central to arguments over what the Constitution means.2 Opponents’ demand for a bill of rights was met in the first amendments added after ratification.2
