The National Mall

“America’s Front Yard” in Washington, D.C.

Washington, D.C., from the U.S. Capitol to the Potomac River

The National Mall is the long open space at the heart of Washington, D.C., running from the U.S. Capitol to the Potomac River.1 Its open spaces go back to Pierre L’Enfant’s plan for the capital, commissioned by George Washington, and it was reshaped in the early twentieth century to frame the Washington Monument and the Lincoln Memorial.1,2,3 Lined with monuments and memorials, it has also been the nation’s stage for inaugurations, celebrations, and protests, most famously the 1963 March on Washington, where Martin Luther King Jr. gave his “I Have a Dream” speech.1,5

Why it matters

The National Mall is the premier civic and symbolic space of the United States. Its monuments show how Americans have chosen to remember their past, and its lawns have been where citizens come to celebrate, mourn, and demand that the country live up to its founding promises.1,3,5

About the place

The Mall is a long swath of lawn and elm trees with the Capitol at one end, the Washington Monument near the middle, and the Lincoln Memorial and its Reflecting Pool at the western end.1,3 The National Park Service unit that cares for it manages more than 1,000 acres, including the Washington Monument; the Jefferson, Lincoln, Franklin Delano Roosevelt, World War I, World War II, Korean War Veterans, Vietnam Veterans, and Martin Luther King Jr. memorials; Constitution Gardens; and Pennsylvania Avenue from the Capitol to the White House.1 About 2,000 American elms line the Mall, and 3,000 Japanese cherry trees grow around the nearby Tidal Basin.1

History

L’Enfant’s plan. The Park Service traces the Mall’s origins to the 1791 plan for the Federal City drawn by Pierre L’Enfant at George Washington’s request. Its open spaces created “an ideal stage” for national remembrance, celebration, and the exercise of First Amendment rights.1 The plan reserved a prominent place for a monument to Washington where a line running south from the White House crossed one running west from the Capitol.2

The Washington Monument. In 1833 a private group, the Washington National Monument Society, set out to build a monument “unparalleled in the world.” It chose Robert Mills’s design for a 600-foot obelisk ringed by columns.2 The cornerstone was laid on July 4, 1848, before a crowd of upwards of 20,000. By 1854 the shaft stood 156 feet high, but a faction aligned with the controversial Know-Nothing Party took over the society, donors fled, and the money ran out.2 For more than twenty years the stump stood unfinished, “doing more to embarrass the nation than to honor” Washington.2 Congress took over in 1876, and the Army Corps of Engineers under Thomas Lincoln Casey strengthened the foundation, simplified the design, and finished it at 555 feet. The stone came from different quarries, and the change in color one-third of the way up can still be seen.2 When the capstone was set on December 6, 1884, it was the tallest building in the world.2

The McMillan Plan and the Lincoln Memorial. In 1901 Senator James McMillan formed the Senate Park Commission, which included the architects Daniel Burnham and Charles McKim, the landscape architect Frederick Law Olmsted Jr., and the sculptor Augustus Saint-Gaudens.3 In 1902 it proposed a Lincoln Memorial at the edge of the Potomac as the western end of the Mall, with a bridge to Arlington National Cemetery linking the former Union and Confederate sides.3 Construction began on February 12, 1914, on filled land that needed 122 concrete piers sunk to bedrock.3 Henry Bacon modeled the building on the Parthenon and used stone from many states, from Massachusetts granite to Tennessee pink marble, to symbolize the reunited Union.4 Daniel Chester French’s nineteen-foot statue was carved from 28 blocks of Georgia marble.3,4 The memorial was dedicated on May 30, 1922, and the Reflecting Pool was finished soon after.3

A stage for protest. The Mall has been the setting for presidential inaugurations and for demonstrations.1 Civil rights activists gathered at the Lincoln Memorial for a Prayer Pilgrimage for Freedom in May 1957 and a Youth March for Integrated Schools in October 1958.5 On August 28, 1963, more than 200,000 people joined the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom, which called for a comprehensive civil rights bill, protection of the right to vote, and fair employment.5 Speakers and performers included A. Philip Randolph, John Lewis, Daisy Bates, Marian Anderson, Joan Baez, and Bob Dylan.5 Toward the end, King spoke from the steps of the Lincoln Memorial. The American Yawp says his speech put “unprecedented pressure on politicians to pass meaningful civil rights legislation.”6 The King Institute notes that the march was not universally embraced; Malcolm X called it “the Farce on Washington.”5

More memorials. Memorials to later presidents, wars, and leaders, among them the Martin Luther King Jr. Memorial, now stand in the park, and the Park Service writes that the park “continues to evolve as Americans seek new ways to recognize our heritage.”1,7
