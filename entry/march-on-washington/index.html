March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom

The 1963 march where King delivered “I Have a Dream”

August 28, 1963 · Lincoln Memorial, Washington, D.C.

On August 28, 1963, a crowd that the King Institute puts at more than 200,000 and the National Archives at more than 250,000 gathered in Washington, D.C., for the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom.1,3 Conceived by the labor leader A. Philip Randolph and organized by Bayard Rustin in less than two months, it united the major civil rights organizations, labor unions, and churches. They demanded a strong civil rights law, voting rights, school desegregation, jobs, and fair employment.1,3,4 It closed at the Lincoln Memorial with Martin Luther King Jr.’s “I Have a Dream” speech. The march helped build pressure for the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, whose provisions reflected its demands.1,5

Why it matters

The March on Washington joined the moral appeal of the civil rights movement to its economic demands. It showed the nation the movement’s size, discipline, and breadth, and it gave King’s dream of racial equality its most lasting expression.1,3,6

Historical background

The idea had a long history. In 1941 A. Philip Randolph, head of the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, threatened to bring 100,000 Black marchers to Washington to protest their exclusion from defense jobs. President Franklin Roosevelt responded with Executive Order 8802, which created the Fair Employment Practices Commission, and Randolph called off the march.1 Civil rights supporters gathered at the Lincoln Memorial for the Prayer Pilgrimage for Freedom in May 1957 and for a Youth March for Integrated Schools in October 1958.1,4

By 1963, the centennial of the Emancipation Proclamation, Black Americans still faced high unemployment, low wages, disfranchisement, and segregation.1 Randolph began planning in 1962. The King Institute dates his first permit letter to May 24, 1962, while the National Archives says he first proposed the march in late 1962 and got little response.1,3 In May 1963, at the height of the Birmingham campaign, King joined Randolph, James Farmer of CORE, and Charles McDew of SNCC in calling for a march, declaring, “Let the black laboring masses speak!” The next month President Kennedy asked the nation for civil rights legislation.1,5

Causes and contributing factors

The march’s official goals, as listed by the King Institute and the American Yawp, were a comprehensive civil rights bill ending segregation in public accommodations, protection of the right to vote, desegregation of all public schools in 1963, a federal works program to train and place unemployed workers, a federal fair employment law, and a higher minimum wage.1,6 Its full name, the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom, reflected the economic demands at its heart, which came from Randolph’s labor background.1,3

Success depended on cooperation among the “Big Six”: Randolph, Roy Wilkins of the NAACP, Whitney Young of the National Urban League, King of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, James Farmer of the Congress of Racial Equality, and John Lewis of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee. The National Archives notes that these leaders competed for funding and press coverage.3 Bayard Rustin, Randolph’s trusted associate, whom Randolph called “Mr. March-on-Washington,” was appointed deputy director despite the concerns of many civil rights leaders. Working from headquarters in Harlem with a small office in Washington and a core staff of 200 volunteers, Rustin put the event together in less than two months.3,4 Sponsors grew to include the NAACP, the National Urban League, the National Council of the Churches of Christ, the National Catholic Conference for Interracial Justice, and the United Auto Workers.1

What happened

The day began with a rally at the Washington Monument featuring celebrities and musicians. Marchers then walked the mile-long National Mall to the Lincoln Memorial for a three-hour program.3 Performers and speakers included Marian Anderson, Odetta, Joan Baez, Bob Dylan, Daisy Bates, Ossie Davis and Ruby Dee, Rabbi Joachim Prinz, Walter Reuther of the UAW, Roy Wilkins, Whitney Young, and John Lewis.1

Lewis’s prepared speech caused a crisis. It called Kennedy’s civil rights bill “too little and too late” and threatened to “march through the South, through the heart of Dixie, the way Sherman did.” Reuther, Justice Department official Burke Marshall, and Archbishop Patrick O’Boyle of Washington objected. In a caucus with King, Randolph, and SNCC’s James Forman, Lewis agreed to cut those passages, though he believed the final version was “still a strong speech, very strong.”1

King spoke near the end. Drawing on themes he had used in speeches for years, including an address to 150,000 people in Detroit two months earlier, he used a metaphor suggested by his adviser Clarence Jones for the unfulfilled promise of constitutional rights: “America has defaulted on this promissory note insofar as her citizens of color are concerned.”2 He had finished the text only at 3:30 that morning. Partway through, he set the manuscript aside and turned to a refrain he had used before: “I have a dream.”2 He later recalled, “I just turned aside from the manuscript altogether. I didn’t come back to it.”2 He closed by imagining the day when all of God’s children could sing, “Free at last! Free at last! Thank God Almighty, we are free at last!”2 The New York Times columnist James Reston wrote that King “touched all the themes of the day, only better than anybody else.”2

Immediate consequences

That afternoon the march leaders met President Kennedy and Vice President Lyndon Johnson at the White House to discuss the need for bipartisan support of civil rights legislation.1,3 Not everyone was moved. Malcolm X, who attended, mocked it as “the Farce on Washington,” the Nation of Islam condemned it, and the AFL-CIO’s executive board stayed neutral, though many member unions marched.1

Long-term significance

The National Archives calls the march the largest demonstration for human rights in American history and “a rare display of unity” among civil rights organizations.3 The American Yawp writes that King’s speech raised the movement’s profile “to new heights and put unprecedented pressure on politicians to pass meaningful civil rights legislation.”6 After Kennedy’s death, Congress passed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, whose provisions, the King Institute notes, reflected the march’s demands.1,5 “I Have a Dream” became one of the best-known speeches in American history.6
