Rosa Parks

Activist whose arrest sparked the Montgomery bus boycott

1913–2005

Rosa Parks was the seamstress and veteran NAACP activist whose arrest on December 1, 1955, for refusing to give up her seat to a white passenger on a Montgomery, Alabama, bus sparked the Montgomery bus boycott.1,3 The thirteen-month protest ended with bus segregation struck down and made Martin Luther King Jr. a national leader.1,2 Long remembered as a tired woman who simply wanted to sit down, Parks was in fact a seasoned organizer who had fought Jim Crow for more than a decade. “The only tired I was,” she wrote, “was tired of giving in.”1,4

Why it matters

Parks’s refusal became the most famous single act of the civil rights movement, but its power came from years of organizing behind it. Her arrest gave Montgomery’s Black community the rallying point it had been looking for, and the boycott that followed showed that nonviolent mass protest could defeat segregation.1,2,3

Early life and context

Rosa Louise McCauley was born on February 4, 1913, in Tuskegee, Alabama, and spent her first years on a small farm with her mother, grandparents, and brother.1,5 As a child she watched the Ku Klux Klan ride at night and listened in fear to news of lynchings near her home.5 She grew up in Montgomery, attended an industrial school for girls and the laboratory school of Alabama State College, and later enrolled at Alabama State Teachers College, but had to withdraw when her grandmother fell ill.1,4

In 1932, at nineteen, she married Raymond Parks, a barber and NAACP member who was then active in the Scottsboro case.1,4 In 1943 Rosa Parks joined the Montgomery branch of the NAACP and was elected its secretary, a post she held until 1956; she worked closely with the branch president, E. D. Nixon.1,5,3 Two years later, after being turned away twice, she succeeded in registering to vote.1 As secretary of the Alabama State Conference of the NAACP, she traveled the state interviewing victims of discrimination and witnesses to lynchings.5 By 1949 she was adviser to the local NAACP Youth Council, whose members challenged Jim Crow by checking books out of the whites-only library.1 She had also defied the bus rules before: once a driver put her off a bus for refusing to obey them.5 In the summer of 1955, with the help of Virginia Durr, she attended a workshop on carrying out the Supreme Court’s school desegregation decision at the Highlander Folk School in Tennessee, where she was encouraged by a fellow participant, Septima Clark.1

Major actions and achievements

On the evening of December 1, 1955, Parks, then forty-two, boarded a city bus on her way home from the Montgomery Fair department store, where she worked as a seamstress.3 Montgomery reserved the front ten seats of each bus for white passengers, and Parks sat in the first row behind them. When the bus filled, the driver, James Blake, ordered her and the three other Black passengers in her row to give up their seats. The other three moved; Parks stayed where she was. Blake believed he had the right to move the color line, though the law was unclear on that point, and he called the police.3 She was arrested, fingerprinted, and charged with “refusing to obey orders of bus driver.” When she called home from jail, her mother’s first question was “Did they beat you?”3

Parks was not the first. Nine months earlier, fifteen-year-old Claudette Colvin had been arrested for the same offense, and in October eighteen-year-old Mary Louise Smith was arrested too.1,2 Neither case moved the city’s Black leaders to act. Parks was different: as King later wrote, she was “one of the most respected people in the Negro community.”2 E. D. Nixon, with Clifford and Virginia Durr, bailed her out. Jo Ann Robinson and the Women’s Political Council called a one-day boycott for December 5.2 That day, 90 percent of Montgomery’s Black citizens stayed off the buses, and the boycott grew into a campaign that lasted more than a year.2 About 17,000 Black citizens took part.5 Parks was convicted, and her lawyer appealed.3

During the boycott Parks worked as a dispatcher for the carpool that replaced the buses, and she spoke in churches across the country, including in the North, to raise money for the Montgomery Improvement Association.1 She was indicted with King and more than eighty others under a 1921 law against conspiracies that interfered with lawful business.1,2 Her own appeal was still tied up in the state courts when a three-judge federal court ruled in another case, Browder v. Gayle, that bus segregation was unconstitutional. It did so in June 1956 (on June 4, by the National Archives’ account; on June 5, by the King Institute’s), and the Supreme Court upheld the ruling on November 13, 1956.3,2 Copies of Parks’s police report and fingerprint card were entered as evidence in that case.3 On December 20, 1956, the boycott ended. It had lasted 381 days by the National Archives’ count, or 382 by the Nobel Committee’s.2,3,6

Beliefs and ideas

Parks always rejected the story that she had stayed seated simply because her feet hurt. “I was not tired physically, or no more tired than I usually was at the end of a working day,” she wrote in her autobiography. “I was not old, although some people have an image of me as being old then. I was forty-two. No, the only tired I was, was tired of giving in.”1,4 Her protest grew out of a lifetime of resistance that began with her childhood memories of Klan terror.5 She believed in organized, persistent action: she spent years documenting violence for the NAACP, training young activists, and registering voters before the moment that made her famous.1,5 She remained modest about her place in history: “I had no idea when I refused to give up my seat on that Montgomery bus that my small action would help put an end to the segregation laws in the South.”1

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Fame brought Parks hardship rather than reward. She lost her job at the department store and received death threats, and harassment continued after the boycott ended.5,1,4 In 1957 she and her husband left Montgomery, first for Hampton, Virginia, and then for Detroit.4,5 Shortly before she left, the Montgomery Improvement Association declared August 5, 1957, “Rosa Parks Day” and presented her with $800.1 Even later in life money was scarce. By 1980, after years of giving her time and money to the movement, the widowed Parks suffered from financial and health troubles, and when she nearly lost her home, neighbors and churches in Detroit came to her aid.4

Her story has also often been told in a misleading way. News accounts at the time, and many retellings since, presented her as a quiet seamstress who acted on impulse. The National Women’s History Museum argues that this image diminished her role as an established organizer who helped plan the boycott itself.4,1

Legacy

In Detroit, Parks stayed active in the NAACP and in campaigns against inequality in the city.4,5 In 1964 she supported John Conyers, a Black lawyer, in his campaign for Congress. After he won, he hired her for his Detroit office, where she worked, among other tasks, helping homeless people find housing, until she retired in 1988.1,5 In 1987 she founded the Rosa and Raymond Parks Institute for Self-Development to offer job training and leadership programs for young people.1,5 In 1999 Congress awarded her its Gold Medal, and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference gives an annual Rosa Parks Freedom Award.5

Parks died on October 24, 2005, at the age of ninety-two.4 On October 30 and 31, 2005, she lay in honor in the U.S. Capitol.7
