Populism and the People’s Party

The farmers’ revolt of the 1890s

1890–1896

Populism was a mass political movement of farmers in the South and West who believed that railroads, banks, and the two major parties served “the moneyed power” at the expense of ordinary producers. Organized first through the Farmers’ Alliance, they formed the People’s Party, which won congressional seats in 1890 and issued the sweeping Omaha Platform in 1892. In 1896 the Populists endorsed the Democrat William Jennings Bryan and soon faded, but many of their proposals later became law.

Key facts

Date
1890–1896
Dating note
The Populists won their first victories in the 1890 midterm elections, adopted the Omaha Platform in 1892, and merged with the Democrats behind William Jennings Bryan in 1896.
First breakthrough
1890 midterms: nine congressional seats and state and local victories
Omaha Platform
Adopted July 4, 1892, at the party’s first national convention
Key demands
Unlimited coinage of silver, a graduated income tax, government ownership of railroads and telegraphs, direct election of senators
1896
Endorsed Democrat William Jennings Bryan

Why it matters

The Populist revolt was the largest third-party challenge of the Gilded Age, and it put the regulation of corporations and the expansion of democracy on the national agenda.

Goals

Populists believed national policies favored industry, banks, and the Northeast over farmers, who faced falling crop prices, debt, and high railroad rates. They sought to expand the money supply, especially through the free coinage of silver, to ease debts; to regulate or nationalize railroads and telegraphs; to institute a graduated income tax; and to make government more directly answerable to voters.

Strategies

Farmers organized through cooperative Farmers’ Alliances and then turned to independent politics. Meeting in Omaha on the 116th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence, July 4, 1892, the People’s Party declared that the nation had been “brought to the verge of moral, political, and material ruin” and that both major parties had permitted “grievous wrongs” against “a plundered people.”

Opposition

Republicans were attacked by the Populists for serving the “moneyed” power of the Northeast, and Southern Democrats were put on notice as small farmers broke away. Both major parties, business interests, and much of the press in turn portrayed the Populists as dangerous radicals.

Achievements

Though their presidential bids failed, the Populists set the stage for William Jennings Bryan’s Democratic campaign of 1896. Several of their demands were later written into the Constitution: a federal income tax (Sixteenth Amendment, 1913) and the direct election of senators (Seventeenth Amendment, 1913).

Unresolved questions

Historians debate Populism’s character: a democratic movement of producers against concentrated power, or one that also carried nativist and racial prejudices. The word “populism” is still used to describe movements that claim to speak for ordinary people against elites.
