Common Sense

Thomas Paine’s 1776 pamphlet that made the case for independence

January 10, 1776 · Philadelphia, Pennsylvania

Common Sense was a pamphlet of about fifty pages, published in Philadelphia on January 10, 1776, by Thomas Paine, an Englishman who had arrived in America little more than a year before.1,2 At a time when most colonial leaders still hoped to reconcile with Britain, Paine attacked not just British policy but monarchy itself and called for an independent American republic.1,2 Written in plain language full of biblical references, it became, according to the American Battlefield Trust, one of the best-selling works in America after the Bible.1,2 Six months later Congress declared independence.1

Why it matters

Common Sense turned independence from a whispered possibility into a public cause. Its plain style reached ordinary readers in taverns and town meetings, and its attack on monarchy gave the Revolution a democratic, republican goal. It remains one of the most influential political pamphlets ever written.1,3

Historical circumstances

Thomas Paine was born in Norfolk, England, in 1737. He worked as a staymaker’s apprentice, customs officer, privateer, and schoolteacher before becoming an advocate for his fellow excise officers.1 In London he met Benjamin Franklin, who in 1774 encouraged him to go to Pennsylvania. Paine nearly died of typhoid on the crossing, then found work as a writer and editor for the Pennsylvania Magazine.1

By late 1775 the colonies had been at war with Britain for months, yet many Patriot leaders still argued only that Parliament had violated their rights as British subjects, and the Continental Congress was still petitioning the king.1,3 The American Yawp notes that in the opening months of 1776 independence, “for the first time, became part of the popular debate,” and that it took seven more months before Congress acted.3 Paine wrote to push that debate forward.1

Principal ideas

  • Government is a necessary evil. “Society in every state is a blessing, but government even in its best state is but a necessary evil; in its worst state an intolerable one.”4
  • Monarchy and hereditary rule are absurd. Paine attacked the idea of kingship itself, not just King George III’s policies, and called for a republic, a government that is a “public affair.”1,3
  • Geography argues for independence. “There is something very absurd, in supposing a continent to be perpetually governed by an island.”4,3
  • The time has come. “The blood of the slain, the weeping voice of nature cries, ’Tis time to part.”4
  • America’s cause is universal. “The cause of America is in a great measure the cause of all mankind,” he wrote, and he urged Americans to “prepare in time an asylum for mankind.”4

Reception

The American Yawp writes that Paine’s “combination of easy language, biblical references, and fiery rhetoric proved potent,” and that the pamphlet was quickly reprinted and spread; arguments over its ideas “filled taverns throughout the colonies.”3 A Connecticut reader told a Philadelphia newspaper: “We were blind, but on reading these enlightening words the scales have fallen from our eyes.”1 Town meetings began passing resolutions for independence, and Congress declared it in July 1776.3,1 Not everyone agreed: a reply called Plain Truth, by a writer signing himself “Candidus,” was published in answer to it.5

The American Crisis

Paine kept writing as the war went on. In December 1776, after Washington’s army had been driven from New York, he began The American Crisis: “These are the times that try men’s souls.” The Battlefield Trust writes that Washington had it read to his troops on December 23, shortly before the surprise attack at Trenton.1

Paine’s later life

Paine later defended the French Revolution in Rights of Man (1791–92), was imprisoned in France, and wrote The Age of Reason, a work of deism. When he returned to America in 1802 his attacks on Washington and Adams and his religious views had made him unpopular, and he died in New York in 1809 with few at his funeral.1 A line the Battlefield Trust attributes to John Adams sums up his importance: “Without the pen of the author of ‘Common Sense’, the sword of Washington would have been raised in vain.”1
