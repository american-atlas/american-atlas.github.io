The Labor Movement

Workers organize for wages, hours, and a voice on the job

1877–1937

As industrial capitalism remade the United States after the Civil War, millions of wage workers, many of them immigrants, organized to win higher pay, shorter hours, safer conditions, and a say over their work.1 For half a century their biggest strikes were crushed. The Great Railroad Strike of 1877, the eight-hour campaign that ended at Haymarket, the Homestead strike, and the Pullman boycott were all broken by police, militias, private guards, court injunctions, or federal troops.1,2 Workers built new organizations anyway: the Knights of Labor, the American Federation of Labor, the Industrial Workers of the World, and garment unions built by young immigrant women.1,6 Only in 1935 did the Wagner Act guarantee most private-sector workers the right to organize and bargain collectively. Union membership then surged.4,5

Why it matters

The labor movement turned the problems of industrial work into public questions. Its defeats in the Gilded Age and its victory in the New Deal shaped the rights that American workers have today.1,4

Goals

Industrial workers toiled long hours for little pay. A typical industrial laborer, the American Yawp notes, worked sixty hours a week, could expect to be out of work one month a year, and might still earn less than a poverty-line income. Wives and children were pushed into paid work to make up the difference.1 Mechanization threw skilled workers into unskilled jobs, and as companies grew larger, individual workers felt smaller and more powerless.1

Unions sought higher wages, shorter hours, safer workplaces, and recognition. The eight-hour day was the demand that most united American workers.1 Some organizations wanted much more. The Knights of Labor imagined a cooperative society that rewarded labor rather than capital.1 Socialists such as Eugene Debs called for “the overthrow of the capitalist system and the emancipation of the working class from wage slavery.”1 Others, like the American Federation of Labor, focused on practical gains.1 Working women also asked for dignity. The garment unionist Rose Schneiderman said in 1912: “The worker must have bread, but she must have roses, too.”7

Strategies

The Great Upheaval of 1877. In 1877, in the stagnant economy that followed the collapse of the railroad bubble in 1873, rail lines slashed wages. Workers struck from Baltimore to St. Louis and shut down rail traffic across the country.1 Militias fired into crowds in Baltimore, Pittsburgh, and Reading, and in St. Louis a general strike demanded the eight-hour day and an end to child labor. Federal troops finally broke the strike after six weeks. Nearly 100 people died.1 Its failure convinced workers they needed lasting unions.1

The Knights of Labor. The Knights tried to unite skilled and unskilled workers and welcomed women. They barred only lawyers, bankers, and liquor dealers. By 1886 they had more than 700,000 members.1 That spring nearly 200,000 workers struck Jay Gould’s southwestern railroads, but Pinkertons, strikebreakers, and state militias broke the strike.1

The eight-hour day. On May 1, 1886, between 300,000 and 500,000 workers struck nationwide for the eight-hour day.1 After Chicago police broke up a strike at the McCormick reaper works, killing one man according to the Illinois library and several according to the Yawp, radicals called a protest meeting at Haymarket Square on May 4. As police moved to break it up, someone threw a bomb.1,3 Seven police officers died, along with at least one civilian, according to the University of Illinois library. The American Yawp writes that police fire killed four people in the crowd.3,1 Eight anarchists were tried on little evidence linking them to the bomb. The Yawp says they were found guilty of conspiracy, while the Illinois library says they were charged with murder. Four were hanged.1,3 Many Americans came to link unions with radicalism, and the Knights and the eight-hour movement collapsed.1

Craft unions. The American Federation of Labor, an alliance of unions of skilled workers, rejected the Knights’ broad vision. It pursued “pure and simple trade unionism”: higher wages, fewer hours, and safer conditions, won if possible without strikes.1

Industrial unions and boycotts. Eugene Debs’s American Railway Union, formed in 1893, enrolled every kind of railroad worker and grew to 150,000 members.2 In 1894 it refused to handle Pullman cars anywhere in the country in support of striking Pullman factory workers, and national rail traffic ground to a halt.2,1

Radical unionism and politics. In 1905 William D. “Big Bill” Haywood and other radicals founded the Industrial Workers of the World, the “Wobblies,” which welcomed all workers regardless of race or gender.1 In 1912 it led textile strikers in Lawrence, Massachusetts.1 Debs ran for president as a Socialist and won almost a million votes, about 6 percent, in 1912.1

Women’s strikes. In 1909 thousands of New York garment workers answered the young Clara Lemlich’s call for a general strike, with help from the Women’s Trade Union League. A cloakmakers’ strike in 1910 won a grievance system for the industry.6 After the Triangle fire of 1911, unions and reformers pushed New York to pass a new code of factory safety laws.8

The New Deal. In 1933 and 1934 a great wave of strikes, including citywide general strikes and factory takeovers, swept the country.4 The Wagner Act of 1935 protected workers’ “right to self-organization” and created the National Labor Relations Board.4 That year John L. Lewis of the United Mine Workers broke with the AFL to found the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO), which organized whole industries. In 1937 auto workers held a sit-down strike in General Motors’ plant in Flint, Michigan, until GM recognized the United Automobile Workers.5

Opposition

Employers had money, the law, and often the government on their side. Gould used strikebreakers and the Pinkerton Detective Agency, a private security firm, to break the 1886 rail strike.1 At Carnegie’s Homestead steel works in 1892, Henry Clay Frick sent hundreds of Pinkertons against striking steelworkers. The workers fought them off, but the governor sent the state militia, broke the strike, and reopened the mill. The union was all but destroyed.1 During the 1877 strike the head of the Pennsylvania Railroad, Thomas Scott, suggested that unhappy workers be given “a rifle diet for a few days.”1

The courts became a powerful weapon. In the Pullman strike, federal lawyers won an injunction against the boycott under the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890, a law meant to combat monopolies.2 Going over the head of Illinois governor John Peter Altgeld, who sympathized with the workers, President Grover Cleveland sent thousands of soldiers and marshals. Dozens of people were killed, and Debs was jailed for contempt.2,1 Jail convinced Debs that political and judicial leaders were tools of capital.1 Socialists and other radicals faced government repression and censorship, especially during and after World War I.1 Even in the 1930s, employers spied on, fired, and blacklisted union members, and Southern mill owners brought in thousands of strikebreakers during the textile strike of 1934.4,5

Achievements

Labor’s long pressure won gains even before the 1930s. Progressive reformers embraced many socialist policies, the American Yawp notes.1 After the Triangle fire, New York passed more than twenty new factory safety and health laws.1,8 The Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914 protected labor’s right to bargain collectively, picket, and boycott.7

The great breakthrough came with the New Deal. The Supreme Court upheld the Wagner Act in 1937, and it brought a dramatic surge in union membership. By the end of the 1930s more than 800,000 women belonged to unions, three times as many as in 1929.4 In 1938 the Fair Labor Standards Act created the modern minimum wage.5 Union members came to see Roosevelt as their protector. One union leader called him an “agent of God.”5

Unresolved questions

The labor movement was often divided by race, sex, and skill. The American Railway Union admitted only white railroad workers.2 The AFL organized mainly skilled craftsmen, and in 1935 the new CIO broke with the craft-oriented federation.1,5 In the South, the Colored Farmers’ Alliance, which once had as many as 250,000 members, declined rapidly after 1891, when white violence crushed the cotton pickers’ strikes it sponsored.1 The Wagner Act itself did not cover farm workers, railroads, airlines, or government employees.4 Congress later rewrote its provisions in the Taft-Hartley Act of 1947 and the Landrum-Griffin Act of 1959.4 The balance of power between workers and employers, and the government’s role in protecting the right to organize, remained central questions in American politics.
