Civil Rights Act of 1964

The law that outlawed segregation in public places and job discrimination

July 2, 1964

The Civil Rights Act of 1964, signed by President Lyndon B. Johnson on July 2, 1964, outlawed segregation in hotels, restaurants, theaters, and other public accommodations. It banned discrimination in employment, authorized federal action to desegregate schools and public facilities, and created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission.1,2 The National Archives calls it “the most sweeping civil rights legislation since Reconstruction.”1 Proposed by John F. Kennedy after the protests of 1963, it was pushed through Congress after his assassination by Johnson and a bipartisan coalition, overcoming a Southern filibuster of 75 days.1,2,4 Together with the Voting Rights Act a year later, the American Yawp concludes, it “effectively ended Jim Crow.”3

Why it matters

The Civil Rights Act ended legal segregation in public life across the South and gave the federal government new power to fight discrimination in schools and workplaces.1,3 Politically, the Yawp notes, it and the Voting Rights Act helped drive the rise of Republicans in the South and West.3

Historical circumstances

Segregation had survived the Brown decision of 1954. The Civil Rights Act of 1957, the first civil rights law since Reconstruction, created the Civil Rights Division of the Justice Department and the U.S. Civil Rights Commission, but Congress stripped out a provision that would have let the Justice Department enforce Brown.2 Civil rights leaders such as A. Philip Randolph kept pressing Presidents Eisenhower and Kennedy to outlaw segregation.2

The protests of 1963 forced the issue. In April and May the Southern Christian Leadership Conference waged its Birmingham campaign of business boycotts, sit-ins, and marches to topple segregation in Alabama’s largest city.3 On June 11, 1963, Kennedy went on national television and radio to call civil rights “a moral issue … as old as the scriptures and … as clear as the American Constitution,” declaring that “this Nation, for all its hopes and all its boasts, will not be fully free until all its citizens are free.”5,2 (The National Archives dates the address to June 6; the King Institute and the Miller Center give June 11.)1,2,5 On June 19 he sent Congress a bill covering voting rights, public accommodations, school desegregation, and nondiscrimination in federally funded programs.2,1 At the March on Washington in August, King demanded the bill’s passage, writing afterward that “everything, not some things, in the President’s civil rights bill is part of NOW.”2

Kennedy was assassinated in November 1963. Johnson, a Southerner who had grown up under Jim Crow, pushed the bill through as a way to honor Kennedy’s memory and preserve his legacy.4 The House Rules Committee bottled it up until supporters threatened to bring it to the floor without committee approval, and the House passed it in mid-February 1964.1,2 In the Senate, Southern Democrats filibustered for 75 days. Senator Hubert Humphrey led the floor fight, and Republican leader Everett Dirksen of Illinois brought enough Republicans to break the filibuster. The bipartisan coalition of Northern and border-state Democrats and moderate Republicans outvoted Southern Democrats and conservative Republicans.1,2,4 The Senate passed the compromise bill 73 to 27, to applause from the galleries.1 Johnson signed it a few hours after final passage on July 2, 1964, with King and other civil rights leaders present.1,2

Principal ideas

The act is divided into titles. The most important are these.

Title I, voting. It required that registration standards be applied equally, forbade rejecting applicants for minor errors, and restricted literacy tests in federal elections.1,2

Title II, public accommodations. “All persons shall be entitled to the full and equal enjoyment of the goods, services, facilities, and privileges … of any place of public accommodation … without discrimination or segregation on the ground of race, color, religion, or national origin.” It covered hotels, restaurants, lunch counters, theaters, and sports arenas whose operations affected interstate commerce. The Miller Center notes that in practice this reached all but the most local businesses.1,4

Titles III, IV, and VI, public facilities, schools, and federal funds. The attorney general could sue to desegregate public facilities and schools, and federally funded programs could not discriminate.1,2

Title VII, employment. It outlawed discrimination in hiring, firing, pay, and other conditions of work and created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) to address race and sex discrimination in employment.1,2 The Yawp notes that the act outlawed discrimination based on race, ethnicity, gender, and national or religious origin.3

Title X created a Community Relations Service to help communities settle racial disputes.2,1

“Equal before God … equal in the polling booths”

At the signing, Johnson told the nation that the law did not restrict the freedom of any American so long as he respected the rights of others. “It does say,” he explained, “that those who are equal before God shall now also be equal in the polling booths, in the classrooms, in the factories, and in hotels, restaurants, movie theaters, and other places that provide service to the public.”6 King had predicted that the act would “bring practical relief to the Negro in the South” and give Northern Black Americans “a psychological boost.”2

Enforcement and resistance

Passing the law did not end violence. In the summer of 1964, during the Freedom Summer voter registration drive in Mississippi, three civil rights workers disappeared and were later found murdered.3 That year the Republican Party nominated the conservative senator Barry Goldwater of Arizona for president, and the Miller Center notes that the War on Poverty and the civil rights laws provoked lasting antagonism from conservatives.3,4 Between 1964 and 1968, riots also broke out in many American cities.4
