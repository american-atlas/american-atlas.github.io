The Cold War

The global rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union, 1947–1991

1947 – December 25, 1991 · Worldwide: Europe, East Asia, Southeast Asia, Latin America, Africa, the Middle East, and the arms and space races

The Cold War was a global political and ideological rivalry between the capitalist countries led by the United States and the communist countries led by the Soviet Union.1 It grew out of the failure of the World War II allies to agree on the postwar world and lasted from the late 1940s until the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991.1,9 The two superpowers never fought each other directly. Instead they built rival alliances, raced to build nuclear weapons and to reach space, and backed opposing sides in wars from Korea to Vietnam.1,6 The closest they came to nuclear war was the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962.8

Why it matters

For more than four decades the Cold War defined world politics. It divided Europe, spread conflict and competition to every continent, and produced the nuclear arms race and the space race. Its peaceful end in 1989–1991 was one of the great surprises of the twentieth century.1,9

Who fought

The United States led an alliance of Western democracies and market economies, formalized in the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) in 1949. Its original members were the United States, Canada, Britain, France, Belgium, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Italy, Portugal, Norway, Denmark, and Iceland.1,5 The Soviet Union led the socialist states of Eastern Europe, which formed the Warsaw Pact in 1955 after West Germany joined NATO: Albania, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Poland, and East Germany.1,5 The People’s Republic of China, founded in 1949, was at first a Soviet ally and later a rival.1,10 As European empires collapsed, both superpowers competed for influence in the newly independent nations of Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America.1

Political background

The Soviet Union and the United States had been allies against Germany, but the alliance rested on necessity. Suspicion ran deep on both sides: the United States had sent troops against the Red Army during the Russian Civil War and did not recognize the Soviet Union for years after its founding in 1922.1 At Yalta and Potsdam in 1945, the Big Three failed to reach a lasting settlement over Germany and Eastern Europe.1 Stalin regarded the territory his armies had won from Germany in Eastern Europe as a Soviet sphere of influence, while American leaders feared that communism would spread through a ruined Europe.1 When Truman told Stalin at Potsdam about a “new weapon of unusual destructive force,” the Soviet leader simply said he hoped the Americans would make “good use” of it.1

Relations worsened in 1946. Disputes over Soviet troops in northern Iran, Soviet pressure on Turkey over the Straits, and the failure of plans for international control of atomic energy ended hopes for a durable postwar partnership.3,1 In February 1946 George Kennan, a U.S. diplomat in Moscow, sent his “Long Telegram” urging that Soviet power be “contained.” In March, Winston Churchill declared in Missouri that an “iron curtain” had descended across Europe.1 In 1947 Kennan’s anonymous “X” article called for “long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containment” of Soviet expansion. That idea became the basic American strategy for the next four decades.2

Major phases

The division of Europe (1947–1949). On March 12, 1947, President Harry S. Truman asked Congress for $400 million for Greece and Turkey and pledged to support “free peoples” resisting outside pressure: the Truman Doctrine.3,1 The State Department’s historians note that Stalin had in fact deliberately refrained from aiding the Greek communists.3 In June 1947 Secretary of State George Marshall proposed a program to rebuild Europe, and the Marshall Plan eventually provided over $12 billion (the American Yawp puts the figure at $13 billion from 1948 to 1952).4,1 The Soviet Union declined to take part and kept the countries of Eastern Europe out, seeing the plan as an instrument of American economic domination, and offered its own program of aid to the East.4,1 In June 1948, after the Western powers introduced a new currency in their zones of Germany, Soviet forces blockaded land access to West Berlin. American and British planes supplied the city by air, at the peak landing one every forty-five seconds, until the blockade ended in May 1949.6,1 That year the North Atlantic Treaty created NATO, and Germany was divided into two states.5,6

Globalization and the arms race (1949–1962). In August 1949 the Soviet Union tested its first atomic bomb, years earlier than American officials had expected, and in October the Chinese Communist Party took power in China.1 A secret 1950 report, NSC-68, called for a massive American military buildup and expanded containment to the whole world: “a defeat of free institutions anywhere is a defeat everywhere.”7,2 In June 1950 war broke out in Korea, where more than 30,000 Americans and millions of Koreans died before an armistice in July 1953.1 Both superpowers built hydrogen bombs, the United States in 1952 and the Soviet Union in 1953, and a strategy of deterrence took hold. J. Robert Oppenheimer compared it to “two scorpions in a bottle.”1 At home, fear of communism fed the Red Scare of Senator Joseph McCarthy.1

The space race. The Soviet Union took the lead in space. On October 4, 1957, it launched Sputnik 1, the world’s first artificial satellite. In 1959 its Luna 2 became the first human-made object to reach the Moon, and on April 12, 1961, Yuri Gagarin became the first human in orbit.1 The United States created NASA in 1958, and President John F. Kennedy used the Soviet lead to win funding for a Moon landing.1

The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962). After the failed U.S.-backed Bay of Pigs invasion of Cuba, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev agreed with Fidel Castro in July 1962 to place Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba to deter another invasion.8 On October 14 an American U-2 spy plane photographed the missile sites. Rejecting advice to bomb and invade, Kennedy ordered a naval “quarantine” of Cuba on October 22.8 After days of tense messages, and the downing of a U-2 over Cuba, the two leaders made a deal. On October 28 Khrushchev announced that the missiles would be removed; Kennedy promised not to invade Cuba and secretly agreed to remove American Jupiter missiles from Turkey, which was done in April 1963.8 The crisis led to a direct “Hotline” between Washington and Moscow and to the Limited Test Ban Treaty of 1963.8,10

Détente (1969–1979). By the late 1960s the arms race had become enormously expensive for both nations. The Soviet Union had split with China, and the United States was mired in Vietnam.10 The superpowers entered a period of relaxed tensions known as détente. They signed the Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty (1968), the first Strategic Arms Limitation Talks agreements and the Antiballistic Missile Treaty (1972), and the Helsinki Final Act (1975), which recognized Europe’s borders and promoted trade, cultural exchange, and human rights.10 Détente faded in the late 1970s, and after Soviet troops entered Afghanistan in 1979 arms talks stalled.10

The last decade (1980–1991). President Ronald Reagan greatly increased military spending in his first term. Yet, the American Yawp notes, he “helped bring the Cold War to an end through negotiation,” a tactic he had once scorned.11 Arms control talks resumed when Mikhail Gorbachev came to power in the Soviet Union.10 Gorbachev’s reforms loosened Soviet control over Eastern Europe. The Berlin Wall fell in November 1989, communist governments across Eastern Europe gave way, and Gorbachev accepted the reunification of Germany.9

Turning points

  • The Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan (1947–1948): committed the United States to containing Soviet influence in Europe.3,4
  • 1949: the end of the Berlin blockade, the founding of NATO, the first Soviet atomic bomb, and the communist victory in China.6,5,1
  • The Korean War (1950–1953): turned containment into a global military commitment.1,7
  • Sputnik (1957): opened the space race and shocked Americans into investing in science and rockets.1
  • The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962): the moment the superpowers came closest to nuclear war, followed by the first arms control agreements.8
  • The fall of the Berlin Wall (1989) and the end of the Soviet Union (1991).9

Outcome

The Cold War ended with the collapse of communist rule in Eastern Europe in 1989 and the breakup of the Soviet Union. Gorbachev’s introduction of multiparty elections and a Soviet presidency began a democratization that, the State Department’s historians write, “eventually destabilized Communist control.”9 An unsuccessful coup by hard-line Communists in August 1991 weakened Gorbachev and brought Boris Yeltsin to the forefront of Russian politics.9 In December 1991 Yeltsin and the leaders of two other Soviet republics formed the Commonwealth of Independent States. On December 25, 1991, Gorbachev resigned, and the Soviet flag was lowered over the Kremlin for the last time and replaced by the Russian tricolor.9 The United States then worked with the new governments to secure the former Soviet nuclear arsenal.9

Consequences for American society

The Cold War reshaped American life. Instead of demobilizing after World War II, the United States built a permanent defense establishment, which President Dwight Eisenhower in 1961 called the “military-industrial complex.” Defense spending transformed regions from Southern California to the South.1 The arms race produced arsenals capable of destroying civilization, and fear of nuclear war became part of everyday culture.1 The space race created NASA and pushed both nations to remarkable achievements in science and engineering.1

At home, anticommunism led to loyalty investigations of federal employees, congressional hearings, blacklists, and the career of Senator McCarthy, who claimed without evidence to hold lists of communists in the government. The Senate condemned him in 1954.1 Abroad, the logic of containment and the “domino theory” drew the United States into interventions across Asia, Latin America, and Africa, most painfully in Vietnam, where more than 500,000 American troops eventually served and nearly 60,000 died.1 The Soviet Union, for its part, pointed to racial injustice in the United States in its propaganda, an embarrassment for a nation claiming to lead the “Free World.”1
