The Siege of Yorktown

The 1781 American and French victory that effectively won the Revolutionary War

September 28 – October 19, 1781 · Yorktown, Virginia

In the fall of 1781, an American army under George Washington and a French army under the Comte de Rochambeau trapped the British general Charles Cornwallis at Yorktown, Virginia, while a French fleet under the Comte de Grasse kept the Royal Navy from rescuing him.1,2 After a three-week siege, including a night assault on two British redoubts led in part by Alexander Hamilton, Cornwallis surrendered on October 19, 1781.1 It was the last major land battle of the Revolution. Britain’s will to fight collapsed, and the war formally ended with the Treaty of Paris in 1783.1,3

Why it matters

The siege of Yorktown was the decisive engagement of the American Revolution. It ended Britain’s attempt to defeat the rebellion by force and led directly to the peace that recognized the United States as an independent nation.1,2

Historical background

By 1781 both sides were exhausted after six years of war. Britain held only a few coastal areas, was also fighting France and Spain, and faced growing opposition to the war at home.1,3 The Americans, deep in debt and short of food, needed a decisive victory.1

French help had become essential. After the American victory at Saratoga, France signed a treaty with the United States in 1778, and in July 1780 Rochambeau’s expeditionary force of 450 officers and 5,300 men landed in Rhode Island.3,1 Meanwhile Britain shifted its attention to the South, where it expected more loyalist support. Its armies took major cities but lacked the men to hold the countryside, and the fighting between patriots and loyalists became, in the American Yawp’s words, “truly a civil war.”3

Causes and contributing factors

In 1780 and early 1781 Cornwallis led his army through the Carolinas, suffering defeats at Kings Mountain and Cowpens and a costly victory at Guilford Courthouse in March 1781.2 Convinced that the southern colonies could not be held “until Virginia was reduced,” he marched into Virginia, a decision his commander, Sir Henry Clinton in New York, never approved.2 The young Marquis de Lafayette, with a much smaller American force, shadowed him all summer, avoiding a pitched battle.2 Under orders from Clinton to take a defensive post at Williamsburg or Yorktown and send part of his army to New York, Cornwallis fortified Yorktown, on the York River, to refit and wait for supplies and reinforcements by sea.2,1

Washington and Rochambeau had to choose between attacking New York City and moving south. When de Grasse promised his fleet for operations in the Chesapeake, they chose Virginia.1 Some 8,000 French and American troops left New York, while fewer than 4,000 stayed behind to guard the Hudson; the Park Service handbook dates the start of the march to August 19, 1781.4 The Yawp says Washington marched south “in October,” but by then the siege had already begun.3,1 The Battlefield Trust calls it “the largest troop movement of the American Revolution.”1

What happened

  • The Battle of the Capes. On September 5, while the allied armies were still marching, the French fleet met a British fleet at the mouth of the Chesapeake Bay and drove it off, leaving Cornwallis cut off by sea.1
  • The siege lines. The allies arrived near Yorktown on September 28. Guided by French engineers, soldiers dug parallel trenches toward the British works, where Washington and Rochambeau planned to join and lead about 12,000 more militia, French, and Continental troops.1
  • The bombardment. On October 9 the allied guns opened fire, with Washington touching off the first American cannon. For nearly a week the shelling was ceaseless. On October 11 the allies began a second line of trenches 400 yards closer.1
  • Redoubts 9 and 10. Two British strongpoints blocked the new line. On the moonless night of October 14, French troops stormed Redoubt 9 while Alexander Hamilton led 400 light infantry against Redoubt 10 with bayonets fixed and muskets unloaded, taking it with only 34 American casualties.1
  • Surrender. A British counterattack on October 15 failed. On October 17 a drummer beat a parley and an officer waved a white handkerchief. On October 19 the British and their Hessian allies marched out between lines of American and French troops, their flags cased.1 Washington refused them the honors of war, as British commanders had refused them to Americans at Charleston earlier in the war.1 The Articles of Capitulation were drawn up at the Moore House outside town.5

Immediate consequences

When news reached London on November 25, Prime Minister Lord North reportedly exclaimed, “Oh God. It is all over.” On March 5, 1782, Parliament authorized the government to make peace, and North resigned fifteen days later.1 The Yawp writes that the capture of another army “left the British without a new strategy and without public support to continue the war.”3 Peace talks took place in France, and the war formally ended with the Treaty of Paris on September 3, 1783.3,2

Long-term significance

Yorktown secured American independence and showed how much the Revolution depended on France: a French fleet sealed the trap, and French soldiers and engineers fought beside the Americans.1,2 It became a central scene in the nation’s founding story, and the U.S. government commissioned John Trumbull to paint the surrender.3 One legend holds that the British band played “The World Turned Upside Down” at the surrender. The Battlefield Trust traces the story to an account published in 1828 and notes that historians doubt it: no widely known tune by that name existed then, and the surrender terms called for the British to march out to “a British or German march.”1
