Siege of Yorktown

The 1781 American and French victory that effectively won the Revolutionary War

September 28 – October 19, 1781 · Yorktown, Virginia

In the fall of 1781 George Washington’s Continental Army and a French army under the Comte de Rochambeau marched hundreds of miles south to trap the British army of Lord Cornwallis at Yorktown, Virginia, while a French fleet under the Comte de Grasse blocked any rescue by sea. After a three-week siege, including a daring night attack in which Alexander Hamilton’s men stormed a British fort with unloaded muskets and fixed bayonets, Cornwallis surrendered on October 19. The British prime minister reacted, “Oh God. It is all over.” Fighting wound down, and the Treaty of Paris recognized American independence in 1783.

Why it matters

Yorktown was the decisive and last major land battle of the American Revolution. It broke Britain’s will to keep fighting and showed how essential the French alliance — an army, a navy, and money — had been to American independence.

Historical background

By 1781, after six years of war, both sides were exhausted. Britain held only a few places on the American coast and was also fighting France and Spain around the world; the Americans faced enormous debts, food shortages, and low morale. In the South, Cornwallis had marched through the Carolinas, but his costly victory at Guilford Courthouse in March 1781 left his army “crippled beyond measure.” Deciding on his own that Virginia had to be conquered first, he moved into Virginia, where a small American force under the Marquis de Lafayette harassed him for weeks. Ordered to hold a defensive base on the coast, Cornwallis fortified Yorktown on the York River, expecting supplies and reinforcements from the Royal Navy.

Causes and contributing factors

Washington and Rochambeau, camped north of New York City, had planned to attack the British stronghold there. When they learned that de Grasse’s fleet could sail to the Chesapeake, they turned south instead and marched their armies hundreds of miles to Virginia — the largest troop movement of the war. On September 5 de Grasse’s ships met a British fleet off the Virginia Capes and forced it to withdraw, leaving Cornwallis cut off by sea.

What happened

The allied armies arrived on September 28 and, guided by French engineers, dug a line of trenches, or parallel, close to the British defenses. On October 9 the bombardment began, with Washington himself touching off the first American gun, and continued almost without pause for a week. On October 11 the allies began a second parallel just 400 yards from the British lines, but two British forts, Redoubts 9 and 10, blocked it from reaching the river.

On the moonless night of October 14 the allies stormed both. The password was “Rochambeau,” which the Americans translated as “Rush on boys!” Lieutenant Colonel Alexander Hamilton led some 400 light infantry against Redoubt 10, their muskets unloaded to keep the attack silent; they hacked through the obstacles with axes, climbed the walls, and overwhelmed the defenders in close fighting, while French troops took Redoubt 9. With allied guns now firing on him from three sides, Cornwallis tried a counterattack on October 15 that failed. On the morning of October 17 a British drummer beat a call for a parley, and an officer waving a white handkerchief came out to discuss terms.

Immediate consequences

On October 19, 1781, the British and their German (Hessian) allies marched out between lines of American and French soldiers and laid down their arms, their flags cased. Washington refused them the honors of war, because the British had denied the same courtesy to the Americans who had surrendered at Charleston, South Carolina, earlier in the war. About 8,600 British and German soldiers had been killed, wounded, or captured. The famous story that the British band played “The World Turned Upside Down” during the surrender is folklore; it first appeared in print decades after the war.

Long-term significance

When the news reached London on November 25, Prime Minister Lord North exclaimed, “Oh God. It is all over.” In March 1782 Parliament authorized peace negotiations, and North resigned. Two more years of diplomacy followed before the Treaty of Paris, signed on September 3, 1783, formally ended the war and recognized the independence of the United States. Yorktown Battlefield is preserved as part of Colonial National Historical Park.
