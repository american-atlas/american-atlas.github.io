Indian Removal

The forced expulsion of Native nations from the East, 1830–1850

May 28, 1830 – 1850 · The Southeast and Old Northwest to Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma)

Indian removal was the federal policy of forcing Native nations east of the Mississippi River to give up their homelands and move to “Indian Territory” in what is now Oklahoma.1,2 President Andrew Jackson made it a central goal, and Congress passed the Indian Removal Act on May 28, 1830.1,4 Through coerced treaties, bribery, and the army, the government relocated about 100,000 people between 1830 and 1850, among them the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Muscogee Creek, and Seminole nations.2,3 The best-known removal, the Cherokee Trail of Tears of 1838–1839, killed thousands.2,3 Removal opened millions of acres of rich land east of the Mississippi to white settlers.3

Why it matters

Indian removal stripped the southeastern nations of their homelands and defied the Supreme Court to do it, and the Trail of Tears became the most painful symbol of the policy.3,2,6

Historical background

From the earliest years of the republic, white settlers pressed onto Native land in the backcountry of the South. Presidents Thomas Jefferson and James Monroe suggested that southeastern nations trade their lands for territory west of the Mississippi, but they did not force the issue.3,2 The first great transfer came through war. In 1814 Andrew Jackson defeated the Red Stick Creeks at Horseshoe Bend and imposed a treaty that took more than twenty million acres, about half of present-day Alabama and a fifth of Georgia.3 Over the next decade Jackson helped negotiate nine of the eleven major treaties that pushed Native nations off their land. Between 1816 and 1840, the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Creek, and Seminole nations signed more than forty treaties ceding territory.3,2

Many of these nations adopted features of white American society in the hope of keeping what remained. The Cherokee had turned to farming, created a written language through Sequoyah’s syllabary, published a newspaper, and adopted a written constitution. Some Cherokee, like some of their white neighbors, held enslaved people.5 None of this protected them. State and federal governments kept pressing them to sign away more land.5

Causes and contributing factors

Settlers wanted the land, and the states wanted Native nations gone.3 When gold was found on Cherokee land in 1830, Georgia held lotteries to hand Cherokee land and gold rights to white settlers, and it passed laws forbidding Cherokees to conduct tribal business, testify in court against whites, or mine gold.2

Jackson, who had made his name fighting Native nations, held that the federal government had no right to protect the Cherokees against Georgia.4 He urged Congress to pass a removal law, and on May 28, 1830, he signed the Indian Removal Act. It authorized the president to exchange land west of the Mississippi for the homelands of nations that agreed to leave, to pay for their move, and to guarantee them their new land “forever.”1,3 It was the only major law passed at Jackson’s request in his eight years as president.4 In December 1830 he told Congress that “the benevolent policy of the Government, steadily pursued for nearly thirty years,” was “approaching to a happy consummation.” “What good man,” he asked, “would prefer a country covered with forests and ranged by a few thousand savages to our extensive Republic, studded with cities, towns, and prosperous farms?”1

What happened

With the act in place, the State Department’s historians write, Jackson and his followers were free to “persuade, bribe, and threaten” nations into signing removal treaties.3 The Choctaws and Chickasaws submitted, and the Creeks agreed under duress.4 An estimated 3,500 Creeks died in Alabama and on the journey west, some of them transported in chains.2 In the north, a short campaign in 1832 crushed resistance by Black Hawk’s band of Sauk and Fox.4 In Florida the Seminoles, joined by free Black people and people who had escaped slavery, fought the Second Seminole War from 1835 to 1842. An American commander called it “a negro, not an Indian War.”5,3 By the end of Jackson’s presidency almost seventy removal treaties had moved nearly 50,000 eastern Native people west.3 The National Park Service counts about 100,000 people removed between 1830 and 1850.2

The Cherokee fought in the courts. In Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831), Chief Justice John Marshall called the tribes “domestic dependent nations” but ruled that the Court lacked jurisdiction.3,5 The next year, in Worcester v. Georgia, the Court held that Georgia’s laws did not apply within Cherokee territory.5,3 Jackson refused to enforce the ruling. According to a much-repeated story, he said that Marshall “has made his decision; let him enforce it now if he can.” The National Park Service reports the words as something he “reportedly said.”2,3

In December 1835 federal agents met a minority faction at New Echota, Georgia. Only 300 to 500 Cherokees attended, and none were elected officials of the nation. Twenty men signed a treaty giving up all Cherokee land east of the Mississippi for $5 million and new land in Indian Territory.2 More than 15,000 Cherokees protested, but on May 23, 1836, the Senate ratified it by a single vote.2 (The State Department’s summary says Congress ratified the treaty in 1835. The National Park Service gives the Senate’s vote of May 1836.)3,2

Immediate consequences

Most Cherokees, including Principal Chief John Ross, did not believe they would really be forced out.2 In May 1838, under Jackson’s successor Martin Van Buren, federal troops and state militia began rounding up more than 16,000 Cherokees in Tennessee, Alabama, North Carolina, and Georgia.6,4 Families were separated, the elderly and sick forced out at gunpoint, and white looters ransacked homes as their owners were led away.2 Three groups sent west by water that summer suffered badly in drought. Ross then won permission from General Winfield Scott to lead the rest of the removal himself.2,6 Detachments of about a thousand people each set out on the 800-mile overland route that autumn. The National Park Service counts twelve such overland groups on one page and fourteen detachments in all on another.2,6 Heavy rains, mud, ice on the Ohio and Mississippi rivers, and short rations made the winter crossing deadly. “People feel bad when they leave Old Nation,” one survivor remembered. “Womens cry and make sad wails. Children cry and many men cry.”2 The last survivors reached Indian Territory in March 1839.2

No one knows how many died. The sources give different figures:

  • The missionary doctor Elizur Butler, who traveled with the Cherokees, estimated more than 4,000, nearly a fifth of the nation.2
  • The National Archives says about 4,000 of 16,000.1
  • The State Department’s historians give 3,000 to 4,000 of 15,000 to 16,000.3
  • Another National Park Service page counts more than a thousand deaths on the march, with perhaps several thousand more from its consequences.6
  • The American Yawp says that of 16,000 who set out, only 10,000 completed the journey.5

Long-term significance

By the 1840s, apart from a few Seminoles still resisting in Florida, no Native nation remained in the American South between the Atlantic and the Mississippi.3 The removed nations rebuilt in Indian Territory. In August 1839 the Cherokee elected John Ross principal chief of the reunited nation, with its capital at Tahlequah, which remains the Cherokee Nation’s headquarters today. About a thousand Cherokees who escaped the roundup in the mountains became the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians in North Carolina.2 The Cherokee, Choctaw, and Chickasaw built their own school systems in the West.5 The State Department’s historians conclude that through “coerced treaties and the contravention of treaties and judicial determination,” the government cleared the way for westward expansion.3
