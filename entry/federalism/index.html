Federalism

How power is divided between the national government and the states

Federalism is the division of governing power between a national government and the states. The Constitution lists the powers of the federal government, makes federal law “the supreme Law of the Land,” and, through the Tenth Amendment, reserves the rest “to the States respectively, or to the people.”1,2 James Madison called the result “neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of both.”3 Where to draw the line between the two levels has been fought over ever since, in disputes over banking, tariffs, slavery, civil rights, and much else.6,7,8

Key facts

Constitutional basis
Enumerated powers (Article I), the supremacy clause (Article VI), and the Tenth Amendment
Before the Constitution
Under the Articles of Confederation each state kept its “sovereignty, freedom and independence”
Madison’s view
Federal powers “few and defined”; state powers “numerous and indefinite”
Key turning points
McCulloch v. Maryland (1819); the Nullification Crisis; the Civil War; federal enforcement of civil rights

Why it matters

Federalism shapes almost every American political fight, because the question of which government should decide is often as contested as the decision itself. Appeals to states’ rights have defended local self-government, and they have also been used to defend slavery and segregation; federal power has been used both to override and to protect individual rights.6,7,8,11

Definition

In a federal system, two levels of government each have real authority over the same people. The Constitution lists the powers of Congress, among them to tax, regulate commerce, coin money, and declare war, and to make all laws “necessary and proper” for carrying them out. It declares the Constitution and federal laws made under it “the supreme Law of the Land.”1 The Tenth Amendment adds: “The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people.”2

Madison described the balance in Federalist No. 45: “The powers delegated by the proposed Constitution to the federal government are few and defined. Those which are to remain in the State governments are numerous and indefinite.”4

Historical context

The framers designed federalism as a middle path. Under the Articles of Confederation the states were bound only in “a firm league of friendship,” and each kept its “sovereignty, freedom and independence.” The Confederation Congress could not tax and was nearly powerless over trade.9 Classical theory held that a republic had to be small; Madison argued instead for “an extended republic,” and his Virginia Plan proposed a strong national government with power over national concerns.10

In Federalist No. 51 Madison argued that dividing power gave liberty extra protection: “In the compound republic of America, the power surrendered by the people is first divided between two distinct governments, and then the portion allotted to each subdivided among distinct and separate departments. Hence a double security arises to the rights of the people.”5

Examples

  • The national bank. In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) Chief Justice John Marshall upheld Congress’s power to charter a bank and struck down a Maryland tax on it, warning that “the power to tax involves the power to destroy.”6
  • Nullification. In 1828 John C. Calhoun argued that the Union was a compact among sovereign states, so a state could nullify a federal law it considered unconstitutional and, if necessary, leave the Union. South Carolina later put the doctrine into practice against a federal tariff. The American Yawp writes that the crisis “united the ideas of secession and states’ rights,” and that the underlying fear was for slavery.7
  • Slavery and fugitives. Before the Civil War, northern states appealed to states’ rights to refuse to help capture people escaping slavery, while white southerners demanded a national commitment to slavery.8
  • Civil rights. In 1957 President Eisenhower sent federal troops to protect Black students at Little Rock’s Central High School against the opposition of Arkansas’s governor. The National Archives dates his executive order to September 23, 1957; the Eisenhower Library lists it under September 24.11,12
