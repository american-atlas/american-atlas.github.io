Martin Luther King Jr.

Minister and leader of the nonviolent civil rights movement

1929–1968

Martin Luther King Jr., a Baptist minister from Atlanta, became the most prominent leader of the civil rights movement.1 Chosen to lead the Montgomery bus boycott soon after he arrived in the city as a young pastor, he joined the ideals of Christianity to Gandhi’s methods of nonviolent resistance.1,2,9 He led campaigns in Birmingham and Selma, delivered “I Have a Dream” at the March on Washington, and helped win the Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act.4,5,6,7 At thirty-five he became the youngest man to receive the Nobel Peace Prize. He was assassinated in Memphis in 1968, while supporting a strike of sanitation workers.1,12

Why it matters

King gave the civil rights movement its moral voice and showed how nonviolent mass protest could change American law and conscience.1,9 In his last years he pressed the nation to confront poverty and war as well as segregation.10,11,13

Early life and context

King was born in Atlanta on January 15, 1929, as Michael Luther King Jr.; his name was later changed to Martin.1 Preaching ran in the family. His grandfather led Atlanta’s Ebenezer Baptist Church from 1914 to 1931, and his father, Martin Luther King Sr., led it after him; the younger King would become co-pastor there in 1960.1 His father also served on the executive committee of Atlanta’s NAACP branch, and in 1944 the teenage King chaired the youth membership committee of its youth council.15 He attended segregated public schools, finished high school at fifteen, and in 1948 graduated from Morehouse College, as his father and grandfather had.1

At Crozer Theological Seminary in Pennsylvania, he was elected president of a mostly white senior class and first studied the ideas of Mohandas Gandhi, whom he later called “the greatest Christian of the modern world.”1,9 In 1950 he heard Mordecai Johnson, the president of Howard University, describe Gandhi’s techniques of nonviolent resistance after a trip to India.9 He went on to Boston University, where he earned his doctorate in 1955.1 In Boston he met Coretta Scott, a student at the New England Conservatory of Music who had joined the NAACP in college. They married on June 18, 1953, at her family’s home near Marion, Alabama, and had four children.14 In 1954 he became pastor of the Dexter Avenue Baptist Church in Montgomery, Alabama.1

Major actions and achievements

King was still new to Montgomery when Rosa Parks was arrested on December 1, 1955. On December 5, Black leaders formed the Montgomery Improvement Association and elected him president. Parks later explained that he was “so new to Montgomery” that he had not had time “to make any strong friends or enemies.”2 That night he told a crowd of several thousand at Holt Street Baptist Church, “If we are wrong, the Supreme Court of this nation is wrong. If we are wrong, the Constitution of the United States is wrong.”2 During the boycott, which lasted 382 days, his home was bombed and he was convicted of conspiring to interfere with lawful business. When a crowd gathered outside his bombed house, he calmed it: “Be calm as I and my family are.”1,2 On December 21, 1956, after the Supreme Court struck down bus segregation, Black and white passengers rode Montgomery’s buses as equals.1

In 1957 King became president of the new Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC), which coordinated local protest movements across the South through Black churches.3 In 1959 he and Coretta spent five weeks in India, where they met Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and Gandhi’s family.9 Not every campaign succeeded. In Albany, Georgia, in 1961 and 1962, Police Chief Laurie Pritchett jailed hundreds of protesters while avoiding public brutality, and the press called the result “one of the most stunning defeats” of King’s career. King later admitted that he had protested “against segregation generally rather than against a single and distinct facet of it.”16

He applied that lesson in Birmingham in 1963. Jailed on Good Friday for defying a court order against protest, he wrote his “Letter from Birmingham Jail” in the margins of a newspaper and on scraps of paper, answering eight white clergymen who had called the campaign “unwise and untimely.”4,17 When police turned fire hoses and dogs on marching children, the images drew international outrage and forced the city’s business leaders to an agreement.4 On August 28, 1963, he closed the March on Washington with his “I Have a Dream” speech, telling a crowd that the King Institute counts at more than 200,000 (the Nobel Committee’s biography says 250,000) that America had “defaulted on this promissory note insofar as her citizens of color are concerned.”5,1,7 Time named him its Man of the Year for 1963.1 In October 1964 he learned that he had won the Nobel Peace Prize, the youngest man ever to receive it, and he gave the prize money of $54,123 to the civil rights movement.1,8 In Selma in 1965, he joined SNCC and local activists in the voting rights campaign that led to the Voting Rights Act, which President Lyndon B. Johnson signed in King’s presence on August 6, 1965.6 King himself summed up the record: “Montgomery led to the Civil Rights Act of 1957 and 1960; Birmingham inspired the Civil Rights Act of 1964; and Selma produced the voting rights legislation of 1965.”6

Between 1957 and 1968 King traveled more than six million miles, spoke more than 2,500 times, wrote five books, and was arrested upwards of twenty times.1 In 1966 he took the movement north, moving his family into a Chicago slum to campaign against housing discrimination. During a march through an all-white neighborhood, he was struck by a rock: “I have never seen anything so hostile and so hateful as I’ve seen here today,” he said. He won an open-housing agreement from Mayor Richard Daley, but by 1967 he complained that the city’s agencies had reneged on it.10

Beliefs and ideas

King took the ideals of his movement from Christianity and its methods from Gandhi. “Christ showed us the way,” he said, “and Gandhi in India showed it could work.”1,9 He called nonviolence “the only morally and practically sound method open to oppressed people in their struggle for freedom.”9 The purpose of direct action, he explained in the Birmingham letter, was to create a crisis that would force negotiation, and he defended breaking unjust laws by pointing to the biblical Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego and to the Boston Tea Party.17 He spoke of the movement’s goal as a “beloved community,” and at Selma he described it as “a society at peace with itself, a society that can live with its conscience.”18,6

In his last years King insisted that civil rights were incomplete without economic justice. In 1967 he planned a Poor People’s Campaign to bring poor Americans of all races to Washington to demand jobs, a fair minimum wage, unemployment insurance, and education.11 He also came to see racism, poverty, and war as connected. In a speech at New York’s Riverside Church on April 4, 1967, he condemned the Vietnam War and called for “a radical revolution of values.”13

Controversies, limits, and criticism

King was attacked from many sides. Segregationists jailed him, bombed his home, and denounced him; Birmingham’s Eugene “Bull” Connor called his Nobel Prize “scraping the bottom of the barrel.”8,2 Younger activists in SNCC criticized his leadership style. During the Freedom Rides some asked why he offered only moral and financial support instead of riding himself, and in Albany they worried that his fame would make local people wait for a savior rather than act on their own.19,16 At Selma, his decision to turn the marchers back at the Edmund Pettus Bridge on March 9, 1965, angered many of them.6 As calls for “Black Power” grew after 1966, he was often criticized as too moderate and too dependent on white liberals, while he argued in turn that “effective political power for Negroes cannot come through separatism.”3,18

His stand against the Vietnam War cost him allies. The Washington Post wrote that the Riverside Church speech had “diminished his usefulness to his cause, to his country, and to his people,” and the NAACP accused him of linking two separate issues.13

The federal government treated him as a threat. The FBI monitored King from the Montgomery boycott onward, and Director J. Edgar Hoover, who believed he was influenced by Communists, publicly called him the “most notorious liar in the country.”20 Attorney General Robert Kennedy authorized wiretaps on his home and SCLC’s offices in 1963. The FBI found no evidence of Communist influence, but it anonymously mailed King a compromising recording and a letter that his staff read as urging him to kill himself.20 A Senate committee later concluded that the FBI had tried “to discredit the supposed target of Communist Party interest—Dr. King himself.”20

Legacy

In the spring of 1968 King went to Memphis to support 1,300 Black sanitation workers who had struck after two of their co-workers were crushed to death by a malfunctioning truck; the strikers marched with signs reading “I Am a Man.”12 On April 3 he told a rally, “I may not get there with you. But I want you to know tonight that we, as a people, will get to the Promised Land.”12 The next evening, at 6:05 p.m. on April 4, he was shot on the balcony of the Lorraine Motel. Rioting followed in more than a hundred cities, and more than forty people died.21 James Earl Ray, an escaped convict, pleaded guilty and was sentenced to ninety-nine years, but soon recanted. In 1997 members of King’s family supported Ray’s request for a new trial, and the Justice Department’s review in 2000 found no evidence that warranted further investigation.21

The Memphis strike ended in a settlement on April 16, after Coretta Scott King led a silent march of some 42,000 people.12 SCLC carried on the Poor People’s Campaign, building a tent city called Resurrection City on the National Mall that spring.11 Coretta Scott King founded the King Center in Atlanta and led the campaign for a national holiday in his honor, first celebrated in January 1986.14 At his funeral, a recording was played in which King said how he wished to be remembered: “that Martin Luther King, Jr., tried to give his life serving others.”21
