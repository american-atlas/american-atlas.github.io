The Voyages of Columbus

Spanish-sponsored voyages that began sustained contact between Europe and the Americas

October 12, 1492 – 1504 · The Bahamas, Cuba, Hispaniola, and the Caribbean

On October 12, 1492, Christopher Columbus, a Genoese sailor sailing for Spain in search of a western route to Asia, came ashore on an island in the Bahamas that its Lucayan people called Guanahani.3,1 He never reached Asia. His four voyages between 1492 and 1504 began lasting contact between Europe and the Americas, and set off the Columbian Exchange and the Spanish conquest.5,1 They also brought catastrophe to the Taíno and other peoples of the Caribbean: enslavement, forced labor, and epidemics destroyed much of the population within a few decades.1,3 Columbus is still honored by some Americans and condemned by others, and the old language of “discovery” describes only one side of the encounter.5

Why it matters

Whatever one thinks of Columbus, 1492 began lasting contact between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres. It transformed the peoples, plants, animals, and diseases of both and began a long collision of cultures.5,1,2

Historical background

By 1492 Europe was changing fast. The Crusades had linked Europe to the wealth and learning of Asia, and Asian goods created a hunger for new trade. Strong monarchies were forming. In Spain the marriage of Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile joined the two most powerful kingdoms of the Iberian peninsula, and in 1492 the crown completed the centuries-long Reconquista by expelling Muslims and Jews from Spain, just as Columbus sailed.1 Italian traders controlled the Mediterranean trade with Asia, so Spain and Portugal, at the edge of Europe, paid middlemen higher prices for Asian goods and looked to the Atlantic for a more direct route.1

Portugal led the way. Under Prince Henry the Navigator, Portuguese sailors perfected the astrolabe and the caravel, a rugged ship that could make long voyages on the open ocean and carry cargo. They built forts along the African coast and, by the end of the fifteenth century, Vasco da Gama had reached India.1 On the Atlantic islands that Iberian sailors came across, the Azores, Canaries, and Cape Verde, Europeans grew sugar with enslaved labor. The Canary Islands’ Native people, the Guanches, were enslaved or died soon after Europeans arrived, which the American Yawp calls a preview of what followed in the Americas.1 Europeans had also crossed the Atlantic before. Norse seafarers reached Newfoundland around the year 1000, but their colony failed.1

Columbus, born in Genoa in 1451, had sailed for Portugal and studied under Portuguese navigators.5,1 He was not trying to prove the earth was round, a fact that educated Europeans and Asians already knew. They also knew it was too large for any caravel to cross to Asia without starving. Columbus believed otherwise because he underestimated the size of the globe by two thirds.1 The World History Encyclopedia says that he based his estimate of about 2,300 miles from the Canaries to Asia on Marco Polo and the map of the Florentine Toscanelli, and that Portugal’s King John II turned him down because the distance seemed too low. The real distance, it notes, is about 12,200 miles.5

Causes and contributing factors

After being refused in several European courts, Columbus won the backing of Isabella and Ferdinand.1 The Spanish monarchs hoped to reach the riches of Asia without the Italian and Portuguese middlemen.1 The World History Encyclopedia adds that they told him to claim any land not already under a sovereign for Spain and the Catholic Church, and gave him a contract making him governor of the lands he took.5 Columbus himself, according to the American Yawp, was “confoundingly wrong about the size of the earth and spectacularly lucky that two large continents lurked in his path.”1

What happened

First voyage (1492–1493). Columbus sailed from Spain on August 3, 1492, with three small ships, the Niña, Pinta, and Santa María, and some ninety men. After about two months at sea they landed on October 12 on an island of the modern Bahamas.5,1 The Lucayan people, who belonged to the wider Taíno world, called it Guanahani. Columbus renamed it San Salvador.3 Which modern island this was is still debated, and Sanama Cay, Rum Cay, Grand Turk, and the Plana Cays have all been proposed. The most popular theory is the island formerly called Watling Island, which was officially renamed San Salvador in 1925.3 Columbus sailed on to Cuba and Hispaniola. The Taíno fished and grew corn, yams, and cassava, and he wrote to the Spanish crown that they were “very gentle and without knowledge of what is evil.”1 But he had come for wealth, and he found little. He saw that some Taíno wore small gold ornaments and reported that “with fifty men they can all be subjugated and made to do what is required of them.”1 He left thirty-nine Spaniards at a fort on Hispaniola and returned to Spain with about a dozen captured Taíno.1,5

Second voyage (1493–1496). Columbus came back as governor with seventeen ships and over a thousand men, still believing he had reached the East Indies.1,5 When gold proved hard to find, the Spanish turned to forced labor and violence. The World History Encyclopedia says that the settlers established the encomienda, a grant of land and Native laborers, and that they used mastiffs to terrorize Native fighters.5 The Yawp calls the encomienda an exploitative feudal arrangement in which Native workers were tied to estates.1

Third and fourth voyages (1498–1504). On the third voyage Columbus reached the mainland of South America. The World History Encyclopedia says that his colonists were by now capturing and selling Native people, and that after charges of tyranny and corruption Columbus and his brother were arrested and sent to Spain, where the monarchs acquitted them.5 On his last voyage, in 1502, he explored the coast of Central America, but a storm drove his ship onto Jamaica, where he and his men were stranded until they were rescued, largely through their own efforts. He returned to Spain, where he died in Valladolid in May 1506.5 He had believed throughout that the lands he found were part of Asia.1,5

Immediate consequences

The Spanish seized Taíno labor and land. The Lucayans of the Bahamas, whom Columbus first met, were taken as slaves, and “in less than 30 years, through enslavement, murder, and disease,” National Geographic writes, they were essentially wiped out.3 On Hispaniola the Taíno, who had at first been friendly, resisted Spanish efforts to kill, enslave, or assimilate them.3 The priest Bartolomé de Las Casas, who sailed to the New World in 1502, was a witness. He wrote of Spanish cruelty and asked: “Who in future generations will believe this?”1 Within a few generations Hispaniola was depopulated. The estimates of its population before contact range from fewer than one million to as many as eight million, and Las Casas thought it was three million.1

Disease did even more than violence. Native peoples had no immunity to smallpox, typhus, influenza, measles and other Old World illnesses, which carried off millions.1 Alfred Crosby notes that the diseases brought from Europe included smallpox, malaria, yellow fever, measles, cholera, typhoid, and bubonic plague, while the list of infections native to the New World was very short.4

Long-term significance

Columbus’s voyages opened the way to the conquest of the Aztec and Inca empires, with Spanish soldiers aided by smallpox and by the enemies of those empires.1 Spain and Portugal divided the New World between them in the Treaty of Tordesillas of 1494, after the pope intervened, and Spanish conquerors set up the framework of the Atlantic slave trade over a century before the first Africans reached Jamestown.2 After Las Casas published his account of Spanish abuses, the crown abolished the encomienda in 1542 and replaced it with the repartimiento, which was meant to be milder but kept many of the same abuses.1 Las Casas’s writings also gave other European powers a humanitarian argument for challenging Spain, and the Spanish called this the “Black Legend.”1,2

No one knows how many people lived in the Americas in 1492. Estimates run from 2 million to 100 million. Henry Dobyns put the figure at 18 million and estimated that 95 percent of Native Americans died in the first 130 years after contact. Some scholars estimate that as much as 90 percent of the population perished within a century and a half.1 Nothing else in history, the Yawp argues, rivals this demographic disaster.1 The exchange of plants and animals that followed changed diets and landscapes across the world. Potatoes, tomatoes, and chocolate came to Europe, and horses and pigs came to the Americas.1

Columbus’s reputation has changed. The World History Encyclopedia argues that the familiar story of Columbus as the hero who discovered America, and who proved the world round, owes much to Washington Irving’s popular biography of 1828 and not to Columbus’s own writings. President Benjamin Harrison called for a national celebration in 1892, and Colorado made it a state holiday in 1906. It adds that Italian American groups, who had faced prejudice, did much to establish Columbus Day, and that since the 1970s there have been calls to honor the Indigenous peoples harmed by the voyages instead. The debate continues.5 National Geographic adds that Indigenous activists in the Caribbean today work to preserve the language, culture, and arts of the peoples who survived.3
