The American Revolution

The war for independence from Britain, 1775–1783, and the revolution in ideas that made a new nation

April 19, 1775 – September 3, 1783 · The thirteen colonies, the Atlantic, and battlefields from Canada to Georgia

In little more than a decade after 1763, colonists who had been proud to be British came to see Parliament’s taxes and controls as a threat to their liberty, and in 1776 they declared independence.10 The war that followed, from the first shots at Lexington and Concord in April 1775 to the Treaty of Paris in September 1783, pitted the new United States, with France as its ally from 1778, against the world’s largest military.10,3,4 The Revolution created a republic founded on the idea that government rests on the consent of the governed, but it left slavery in place and opened Native lands to settlement, contradictions that shaped the nation for generations.10

Why it matters

The American Revolution created the United States and launched what historians call the age of democratic revolutions, followed by revolutions in France, Haiti, and South America.10 Its ideals, above all the Declaration’s claim that “all men are created equal,” became the shared language that later movements, from abolitionists to suffragists and civil rights activists, used to demand that the nation live up to its founding.10,13

Who fought

On one side stood the Patriots, colonists who came to support independence, organized first in local committees and provincial congresses and then in the Continental Congress and the Continental Army under George Washington.10 From 1778 France was their ally, and Spain and the Dutch Republic also went to war with Britain, turning the rebellion into a global conflict.3,10 On the other side stood Great Britain, its regular army and navy, tens of thousands of hired German soldiers, usually called Hessians, and the Loyalists, colonists of every rank who remained faithful to the king.10

Many Americans were neither. A good number of colonists tried to stay neutral.10 The roughly 250,000 Native Americans in British North America, in more than eighty nations east of the Mississippi, mostly tried at first to stay out of the conflict; when they took sides, they did so to protect their lands and sovereignty.8 Many, including Shawnee, Creek, Cherokee, and most of the Six Nations of the Haudenosaunee, sided with Britain, while the Oneida and Tuscarora supported the Americans.10,9 Black Americans, free and enslaved, also weighed which side offered freedom: an estimated 5,000 to 6,000 fought for independence, while about 20,000 fought for the British.7

Political background

Seen from 1763, nothing would have seemed less likely than an American revolution. Colonists had just helped Britain win the Seven Years’ War, and most had never been prouder to be British.10 Over the previous century they had grown used to running their own affairs. Each colony had an elected assembly that taxed residents and paid royal officials, and because land was easier to get than in England, a larger share of colonial men could vote.10 Many colonists embraced the ideology of republicanism, which warned that power corrupts and that a free people must be virtuous and watchful against conspiracy and tyranny.10 The ideas of John Locke and the religious revivals of the Great Awakening both encouraged ordinary people to question inherited authority.10

Victory left Britain in control of North America east of the Mississippi, but it also doubled the national debt and left the empire with expensive new frontiers to defend.10,1 King George III’s government set out to bring the colonies under firmer control. The Royal Proclamation of 1763 forbade settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains, hoping to avoid costly wars with Native nations after Pontiac’s War, and colonists who had fought for that land protested.2,10

Major phases

The imperial crisis, 1764–1774. Parliament’s attempts to raise revenue set off a decade of escalating resistance. The Sugar Act of 1764 tightened enforcement of duties on molasses and sent smugglers to courts without juries, and the Currency Act restricted colonial paper money in the middle of a postwar recession.10,1 The Stamp Act of 1765, the first direct tax Parliament had ever laid on the colonies, required stamps on newspapers, legal documents, diplomas, and even playing cards.10 Colonists resisted in three ways: assemblies passed resolutions such as the Virginia Resolves, and nine colonies sent delegates to a Stamp Act Congress; merchants organized boycotts of British goods; and crowds burned effigies and drove every stamp distributor to resign.10 Parliament repealed the act in 1766 but declared its full power to bind the colonies “in all cases whatsoever.”10

The Townshend Acts of 1767 placed duties on everyday imports such as lead, glass, paint, and tea and used the money to pay royal officials, freeing governors from the assemblies’ control.10 Resistance now drew in colonists of every rank. Merchants and consumers signed nonimportation and nonconsumption agreements, committees policed them, and women organized spinning clubs so that families could wear homespun instead of British cloth.10 Troops sent to Boston in 1768 clashed with townspeople until the Boston Massacre of March 1770.10 Parliament then repealed every Townshend duty except the one on tea. When the Tea Act of 1773 tried to rescue the East India Company by selling its tea in America, Bostonians dumped 342 chests into the harbor at the Boston Tea Party.10 Parliament answered with the Coercive Acts of 1774, which closed Boston’s port, took over Massachusetts’s government, and allowed troops to be quartered in colonists’ houses.10 Rather than isolating Massachusetts, the acts united the colonies. The First Continental Congress met in September 1774 and adopted the Continental Association, a continent-wide boycott enforced by local committees of ordinary colonists.10

From war to independence, 1775–1776. Fighting began on April 19, 1775, at Lexington and Concord, and some twenty thousand militiamen soon besieged the British in Boston.10,5 At Bunker Hill (actually Breed’s Hill) in June, the British took the colonists’ fortifications but suffered twice as many casualties as the Americans.11 The Second Continental Congress adopted the army besieging Boston and made George Washington its commander, while still sending the king an Olive Branch Petition; George III instead proclaimed the colonies in rebellion.10 In the opening months of 1776 Thomas Paine’s pamphlet Common Sense attacked monarchy itself and turned independence into a popular cause. On July 2, 1776, Congress voted for Richard Henry Lee’s resolution that the colonies “are, and of right ought to be, Free and Independent States,” and on July 4 it approved the Declaration of Independence, drafted by Thomas Jefferson.10

The war in the North, 1776–1778. That summer the largest expeditionary force in British history landed at New York, and the Continental Army, badly beaten in Brooklyn and Manhattan, retreated across New Jersey.10 With morale collapsing and enlistments expiring, Washington crossed the icy Delaware River on Christmas night 1776 and surprised a garrison of 1,500 Hessians at Trenton, then won again at Princeton a week later.12 In 1777 the British took Philadelphia, but a British army marching south from Canada under General John Burgoyne was surrounded and forced to surrender at Saratoga, New York.10 The Continental Army suffered through the winter of 1777–1778 at Valley Forge, where more than 2,500 men died of disease and exposure.10 Washington learned that his untrained army could not win head-on battles against British professionals; as long as he kept it intact, the war would go on, however many cities the British captured.10

The war in the South and the world, 1778–1783. In 1778 Britain shifted its effort to the South, where it expected more Loyalist support. British campaigns captured major cities from Georgia to Virginia, but Britain lacked the troops to hold the countryside, and bitter fighting between local Patriots and Loyalists made the Southern war a true civil war.10 In 1781 Washington and the French army marched south and, with a French fleet blocking the coast, trapped General Charles Cornwallis at Yorktown, Virginia, forcing his surrender in 1781.10,6

Turning points

Saratoga (1777) was the decisive turning point. The surrender of an entire British army convinced the French government that the American cause could succeed. France had already been sending secret aid; when news of Saratoga reached Foreign Minister Vergennes in December 1777, he decided for an alliance, and the Treaty of Alliance and the Treaty of Amity and Commerce were signed on February 6, 1778.3,10 The war became a global conflict that stretched British forces from Europe to India.10

Yorktown (October 1781) left Britain with no new strategy and no public support for continuing the war. By then Britain was also fighting France, Spain, and the Netherlands, and the capture of a second army brought the government to the peace table.10,6

Outcome

Peace talks opened in Paris in September 1782. Benjamin Franklin, John Adams, and John Jay insisted on British recognition of independence and refused a separate peace without France.4 Two months of hard bargaining produced preliminary articles in which Britain accepted American independence and the new nation’s boundaries and settled questions of fishing rights off Newfoundland, prewar debts, and Loyalist property.4 The final Treaty of Paris was signed on September 3, 1783.4,10 Three months later, on December 23, 1783, Washington resigned his command, a gesture that ensured civilian rule over the new republic.10

Consequences for American society

The Revolution’s most immediate result was a wave of constitution writing. In 1776 and 1777 the new states drafted written constitutions based on popular sovereignty, the idea that government’s authority comes from the people; several, following Virginia, included a bill of rights.10 Pennsylvania’s constitution allowed all free men to vote, and Massachusetts’s 1780 constitution, debated in town meetings, created a government of three branches with checks and balances.10 The Articles of Confederation, ratified in 1781, joined the states in a weak union whose Congress could not tax or regulate trade.10 Politics became more democratic and society less deferential, and the end of British trade restrictions opened new markets and manufacturing.10

The Revolution’s promise of equality also had sharp limits. Women had organized boycotts and run farms and shops during the war, but they gained no political rights; instead they were praised as “republican mothers” responsible for raising virtuous citizens.10 About sixty thousand Loyalists left the country, many for Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, and Quebec, and states kept seizing Loyalist property despite the peace treaty.10 For enslaved people, the war was the largest flight from slavery before the Civil War: historians estimate that as many as 80,000 to 100,000 escaped their Patriot enslavers, and 8,000 to 10,000 Black Loyalists left with the British in 1783 to begin new lives across the Atlantic world.7 Several Northern states passed gradual emancipation laws, but in the South slavery endured, and the Declaration’s words about equality became a challenge the nation would face again and again.10 For Native nations, American victory brought a rush of settlers into the West and governments hostile to their land claims, and Native peoples would continue to be pushed west throughout the next century.10
