Government, Laws, and Institutions

Institution·The Early Republic

Congress

The legislative branch: the Senate and the House of Representatives

1789 · U.S. Capitol, Washington, D.C.

Article I of the Constitution vests “All legislative Powers” in a Congress made up of a Senate and a House of Representatives.1 The House, elected every two years, gives each state seats according to its population; the Senate gives every state two members, whatever its size.1,2 This design, the Great Compromise of 1787, saved the Constitutional Convention from collapse.2 Congress makes the laws, controls taxing and spending, declares war, confirms the president’s nominees, and can impeach and remove officials.1,8 As the branch closest to the people, it has been the stage for many of the great debates of American history.

Why it matters

Congress is the first branch of government in the Constitution and the institution through which Americans’ elected representatives make law, control spending and hold presidents to account. Its structure still reflects the bargains of 1787 between large states and small, and between free states and slave states.1,2

Origins

The Revolution was partly a quarrel about representation. Colonists who were used to their own elected assemblies rejected the British idea that they were “virtually represented” in Parliament, and rallied behind the cry “No taxation without representation!”2 Under the Articles of Confederation each state sent two to seven delegates to Congress but had only one vote.2

At the Constitutional Convention in 1787 the large and small states fought over representation for weeks, and the dispute nearly ended hopes of a national government. Benjamin Franklin compared the solution to a carpenter’s: when the planks of a table do not fit, “the artist takes a little from both, and makes a good joint.”2 The “good joint” was the Great Compromise: a House apportioned by population and a Senate with two seats for every state.2 A census every ten years would adjust the House seats. The convention also adopted the Three-Fifths Compromise, counting each enslaved person as three-fifths of a free person, which gave the slave states extra seats.2,1

The Constitution set a ceiling of one representative for every thirty thousand people. The first House, which met in 1789, had 65 members.1,9 Senators were chosen by state legislatures; House members have always been elected directly by the people.1,7

Powers and limits

Article I, Section 8 gives Congress power “To lay and collect Taxes,” to borrow money, to regulate commerce, “To declare War,” to raise armies and a navy, and “To make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper” for carrying out its powers.1 Every bill must pass both houses and go to the president, and Congress can override a veto by two-thirds of each house.1,8

  • The purse. “All Bills for raising Revenue shall originate in the House,” the chamber the people elect directly, and no money can be spent without an appropriation by law.6 The House Ways and Means Committee has set tariffs and taxes since the earliest Congresses; an Appropriations Committee was created in 1865.6
  • War. The framers would not let the president take the country to war alone. Madison and Elbridge Gerry changed the draft from Congress’s power to “make” war to the power to “declare” war, leaving the president room to repel a sudden attack.5
  • Impeachment and appointments. The House has “the sole Power of Impeachment” and the Senate tries impeachments; the Senate also approves treaties and confirms or rejects the president’s nominees.1,8

How it changed over time

Parties and the Speaker. The House chose Frederick Muhlenberg of Pennsylvania as its first Speaker, partly so his state would have a high office alongside Washington’s Virginia, Adams’s Massachusetts and Jay’s New York.4 Henry Clay, elected Speaker in 1811 in his first House term, made the office nationally important.4 Political parties, which many founders had hoped to avoid, quickly organized Congress: by the 1790s the House divided into pro- and anti-administration blocs and then into Federalists and Democratic-Republicans.9 After the Civil War strong Speakers such as Thomas Reed and Joseph Cannon controlled the House, until a revolt in 1910 cut back the Speaker’s powers.4

Amending the system. After the Civil War, Congress led Reconstruction and proposed the Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments. The Fourteenth, ratified in 1868, ordered the census to count every person in full, ending the three-fifths rule.2 By the late 1800s, deadlocked state legislatures left Senate seats empty for months or years, and reformers attacked the Senate as a “millionaires’ club.” Congress proposed the Seventeenth Amendment, providing for the direct election of senators, in 1912, and it was ratified in 1913.7

The modern era. In the twentieth century Congress enacted the New Deal, the Civil Rights Act and the Great Society. After Vietnam it passed the War Powers Resolution of 1973, which, the American Yawp writes, “dramatically reduced the president’s ability to wage war without congressional consent.”10

Why a declaration of war belongs to Congress

In 1848, during the war with Mexico, a first-term congressman named Abraham Lincoln explained the framers’ thinking. “Kings had always been involving and impoverishing their people in wars,” he wrote, and the convention “resolved to so frame the Constitution that no one man should hold the power of bringing this oppression upon us.”5

The three-fifths rule

The House historians call the Three-Fifths Compromise “notorious” and the Constitution’s “original sin.” Madison defended it in Federalist No. 54, admitting that the reasoning seemed “a little strained in some points.” The Fourteenth Amendment of 1868 ordered the census to count every person in full.2

A note on the first House

The House historians write that in the First Congress each member “represented 30,000 citizens.”3 The Constitution itself sets thirty thousand as a ceiling, not a fixed size: the number of representatives “shall not exceed one for every thirty Thousand.”1
