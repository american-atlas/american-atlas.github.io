The Emancipation Proclamation

Lincoln declares freedom for enslaved people in the Confederacy, 1863

January 1, 1863

On January 1, 1863, President Abraham Lincoln declared that “all persons held as slaves” in the areas then in rebellion against the United States “are, and henceforward shall be free.”1 He issued it as commander in chief, calling it “a fit and necessary war measure.” It did not apply to the loyal border states or to parts of the Confederacy already under Union control, and it depended on Union victory to take effect.1,2 Even so, it changed the character of the Civil War. Every advance of Union troops now widened the territory of freedom, and the proclamation opened the Army and Navy to Black men, almost 200,000 of whom served.1

Why it matters

The Emancipation Proclamation did not end slavery, but it turned a war for the Union into a war for freedom and made the destruction of slavery a Union war aim.1,4 It helped keep Britain and France from recognizing the Confederacy, and the Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in 1865, completed its work by abolishing slavery throughout the country.2,5,9

Historical circumstances

When the war began in April 1861, the Union fought to restore the nation, not to end slavery. Lincoln wanted to keep the loyal slave states of the border in the Union and to win over Northern Democrats, and he held that the Constitution did not give the federal government power to abolish slavery.3 Enslaved people forced the question. From the first days of the war they fled to Union lines.2,4 In May 1861 General Benjamin Butler, at Fortress Monroe in Virginia, refused to return them, calling them “contraband of war,” and Congress approved his policy that summer in the First Confiscation Act.4

Pressure grew in 1862. Republican senators such as Charles Sumner and Ben Wade urged Lincoln to use his war power to free the slaves, and Northern free Black leaders called for an emancipation proclamation.3 Lincoln first proposed paying border-state slaveholders to free their slaves gradually. The border states refused, and he concluded that few slaveholders would ever give up slavery voluntarily.3 He also talked of colonization, settling freed people abroad, and funded projects in Central America and Haiti that came to nothing.3 In April 1862 Congress abolished slavery in the District of Columbia, and in July the Second Confiscation Act freed the slaves of those who supported the rebellion, though it exempted loyal slaveholders.4,3

That summer Lincoln presented a draft proclamation to his cabinet. The Miller Center places this in July and the American Yawp in August.3,4 Secretary of State William Seward urged him to wait for a victory so that it would not look like the act of a failing government.4,3 The chance came at Antietam, in Maryland, on September 17, 1862, the bloodiest single day in American history, when Union forces stopped Robert E. Lee’s advance into Maryland.3 It was not a decisive Union victory, but it was enough.4 On September 22 Lincoln issued the preliminary proclamation. It warned that slaves in any state still in rebellion on January 1, 1863, would be “then, thenceforward, and forever free.”1,3 No Confederate state returned, and one hundred days later Lincoln signed the final proclamation.1

Principal ideas

The proclamation is short and written in the dry language of law. Lincoln relied on his authority “as Commander-in-Chief, of the Army and Navy of the United States in time of actual armed rebellion,” and called the order “a fit and necessary war measure for suppressing said rebellion.”1 That legal basis explains its limits. A war measure could reach only enemy territory, so the proclamation listed ten states in rebellion and then excepted the parts of Louisiana and Virginia already under Union control, including New Orleans and the counties that became West Virginia, which were “left precisely as if this proclamation were not issued.”1 Tennessee, largely occupied by Union forces, was not named at all. Andrew Johnson, Lincoln’s military governor there, had persuaded him to leave it out.1,7 The four loyal slave states, Delaware, Kentucky, Maryland, and Missouri, were untouched. The American Yawp estimates that more than 700,000 people remained enslaved in those states and in the exempted Union-held areas.8,2

Within the designated areas, Lincoln declared all enslaved persons “are, and henceforward shall be free,” and he committed the executive branch, “including the military and naval authorities thereof,” to “recognize and maintain” that freedom. (The often-quoted words “forever free” come from the preliminary proclamation, which the final text repeats.)1 He urged the freed people to “abstain from all violence, unless in necessary self-defence,” and to work “for reasonable wages.” He announced that Black men “of suitable condition” would be received into the armed forces. He closed by calling the act “sincerely believed to be an act of justice, warranted by the Constitution, upon military necessity.”1

Liberated people become liberators

The proclamation’s most practical clause opened the armed forces to Black men. Although a few commanders had formed Black units in 1862, such as Thomas Wentworth Higginson’s First South Carolina Volunteers, widespread enlistment began only after January 1, 1863.4 Massachusetts governor John Andrew quickly raised the 54th Massachusetts Volunteer Infantry, whose assault on Battery Wagner, South Carolina, in July 1863 helped inspire further enlistment.10 Black soldiers were paid less than white soldiers, served under white officers, and faced death or enslavement if captured. In September 1863 Corporal James Henry Gooding of the 54th wrote to Lincoln asking that they be treated “as American SOLDIERS, not as menial hirelings.”4 Lincoln came to see Black enlistment as decisive: “It works doubly, weakening the enemy and strengthening us,” he said in August 1863.4 The numbers differ by what they count. The National Archives says almost 200,000 Black soldiers and sailors fought for the Union, while the American Yawp counts more than 180,000 Black men in the Army, about 10 percent of it, most of them assigned to garrison duty behind the lines.1,4

Reactions at home

The proclamation was a political gamble. Peace Democrats opposed any talk of emancipation, and the Democrats gained seats in the 1862 congressional elections.3 In his annual message to Congress in December 1862, Lincoln tied emancipation to saving the Union: “In giving freedom to the slave we assure freedom to the free—honorable alike in what we give and what we preserve. We shall nobly save or meanly lose the last best hope of earth.”6 In Northern cities, the American Yawp notes, many immigrants feared that freed people would take their jobs. That fear fed the New York City draft riots of July 1863, in which white rioters killed some 120 people, including at least eleven Black New Yorkers.4 Still, according to the Miller Center, most Northerners came to accept emancipation and Black enlistment as military necessities, and the exemption of loyal areas reassured many Democrats.3

Reactions abroad

The Confederacy hoped that Britain’s need for cotton would bring it into the war, and members of the British upper class sympathized with the South, while the working class was hostile to a nation of “aristocrats and slavemasters.” The proclamation turned British antislavery feeling into strong political opposition to the South. Once the Union Army presented itself as an army of liberation, the Miller Center argues, no British government could have sided with the Confederacy.5 The National Archives notes that Lincoln hoped the step would keep England and France from giving the Confederacy recognition or military aid.1

From proclamation to amendment

Because the proclamation was a war measure, its legal force after the war was uncertain, and it left slavery standing in the loyal states. Republicans therefore wrote the Thirteenth Amendment, which their 1864 platform endorsed and which abolished slavery throughout the United States in 1865.3,4,9 News of freedom reached some places slowly. On June 19, 1865, Union General Gordon Granger arrived in Galveston and informed the people of Texas that “all slaves are free,” the origin of the Juneteenth holiday.11 Legal freedom did not bring equality. Freed people faced segregation and violence, and most Black Southerners went on working on plantations as tenants or sharecroppers.4

The document itself

The original proclamation covers five pages. It was once tied with narrow red and blue ribbons fixed to the signature page with the seal of the United States, and most of the ribbon survives. The State Department bound it with other proclamations and numbered it 95; it was transferred to the National Archives in 1936.2 The Archives puts it on public display only for a few days at a time.2
