Roe v. Wade

The Supreme Court recognizes a constitutional right to abortion, 1973

1973 · Washington, D.C.; Dallas County, Texas

In Roe v. Wade (1973) the Supreme Court ruled 7 to 2 that a Texas law banning abortion except to save the mother’s life violated the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.1,2 Writing for the Court, Justice Harry Blackmun held that the constitutional right of privacy “is broad enough to encompass a woman’s decision whether or not to terminate her pregnancy,” but that the right was “not unqualified” and had to be weighed against the state’s interests in health and in “potential life.”1 The Court set out a framework based on the trimesters of pregnancy.1 Roe became one of the most debated decisions in American history. The Court modified it in 1992 and overruled it in 2022, returning the question to the states.3,4,5

Why it matters

Roe v. Wade made the right to abortion a question of constitutional law for half a century and shaped American politics, elections, and Supreme Court nominations. Its overruling in 2022 returned the issue to the states and remains fiercely debated.2,4,5

Historical context

Texas first made abortion a crime in 1854. Its law, which had remained substantially unchanged since 1857, allowed an abortion only “by medical advice for the purpose of saving the life of the mother,” and similar laws existed in a majority of states.1 By the early 1970s some states, such as Georgia, had adopted more modern laws, which the Court described as reflecting “recent attitudinal change” and “new thinking about an old issue.”1 The Court’s decision built on earlier rulings that recognized a constitutional “right to privacy,” among them a 1965 case striking down a Connecticut law that banned married couples from using birth control.5

In March 1970 a single woman living in Dallas County, using the pseudonym Jane Roe, sued the county district attorney, Henry Wade. She said she was unmarried and pregnant, could not get a legal abortion in Texas because her life was not in danger, and could not afford to travel to another state.1 A doctor facing prosecution under the law, and a married couple, joined the challenge.1 A three-judge federal court ruled the Texas law unconstitutional, and Texas appealed.1 The Court held that her case was not moot even though her 1970 pregnancy had ended.1

The legal question

Does the Constitution protect a woman’s decision to end a pregnancy, and if so, how far may a state restrict or forbid abortion?1,2

The ruling

The Court held that a law like Texas’s, which allowed abortion only to save the mother’s life “without regard to pregnancy stage and without recognition of the other interests involved,” violated the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.1 It then set out a framework.1

  • Before about the end of the first trimester, the decision had to be left to “the medical judgment of the pregnant woman’s attending physician.”
  • After the first trimester, the state could regulate abortion “in ways that are reasonably related to maternal health.”
  • After viability, the state could “regulate, and even proscribe, abortion except where it is necessary… for the preservation of the life or health of the mother.”1

The vote was 7 to 2. Chief Justice Warren Burger and Justices Douglas and Stewart wrote concurrences, and Justices Byron White and William Rehnquist dissented.2

The Court’s reasoning

The right of privacy. Blackmun acknowledged that the Constitution does not mention a right of privacy, but he wrote that the Court had found its roots in the First, Fourth, Fifth, Ninth, and Fourteenth Amendments, and that it covered matters of marriage, procreation, contraception, family, and child rearing.1 That right, “whether it be founded in the Fourteenth Amendment’s concept of personal liberty… or… in the Ninth Amendment,” was “broad enough to encompass a woman’s decision whether or not to terminate her pregnancy.”1 He pointed to the medical, psychological, and social harms that denying the choice could impose.1

State interests. The Court rejected the idea that the right was absolute. The state had legitimate interests “in safeguarding health, in maintaining medical standards, and in protecting potential life,” and those interests grew stronger as pregnancy advanced, becoming “compelling” at different points.1 The Court concluded that “the right of personal privacy includes the abortion decision, but that this right is not unqualified and must be considered against important state interests in regulation.”1

The dissents. Justice Rehnquist argued that an abortion performed by a doctor was not “private” in the ordinary meaning of the word. He noted that at least 36 state and territorial laws restricted abortion when the Fourteenth Amendment was adopted in 1868, and he concluded that its framers had not meant to take that power from the states.1 Justice White also dissented.2

Consequences

The ruling made the abortion decision a matter of federal constitutional rights, and laws like the Texas statute could no longer stand.1,2 The American Yawp writes that Roe and another 1973 ruling “established the battle lines for the ‘sex wars’ of the 1970s,” and abortion became one of the most divisive issues in American politics.5 In Planned Parenthood v. Casey (1992) the Court reaffirmed what it called Roe’s “essential holding,” the right to choose abortion before viability. But it rejected the trimester framework and replaced it with an “undue burden” test, under which a law was invalid if it placed “substantial obstacles in the path of a woman seeking an abortion before the fetus attains viability.”3

Later interpretation

How later courts, scholars, and the public have judged the case. This is not part of the ruling itself.

On June 24, 2022, in Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization, a case about a Mississippi law banning most abortions after fifteen weeks, the Court overruled Roe and Casey.4 Writing for a majority of five, Justice Samuel Alito held that “the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion” and that the right was not “deeply rooted in this Nation’s history and tradition.” He called Roe “egregiously wrong from the start” and returned the authority to regulate abortion “to the people and their elected representatives.”4 Chief Justice John Roberts concurred in the judgment, and Justices Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan dissented.4,6
