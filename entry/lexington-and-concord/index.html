Lexington and Concord

April 19, 1775: the first fighting of the American Revolution and “the shot heard ’round the world”

April 19, 1775 · Lexington, Lincoln, and Concord, Massachusetts

Before dawn on April 19, 1775, about 700 British soldiers marched from Boston to seize military supplies that Massachusetts patriots had stockpiled at Concord.1 Warned by Paul Revere, William Dawes, Samuel Prescott, and church bells across the countryside, militia turned out.1,2 On Lexington Green a shot was fired, no one knows by whom, and British volleys killed eight militiamen.2 At Concord’s North Bridge the militia fired back, and on the long road back to Boston thousands of them harried the retreating regulars.2 The day’s fighting, by the Park Service’s count involving some 1,700 British regulars and over 4,000 colonial militia, began the Revolutionary War.1

Why it matters

The fighting at Lexington and Concord was the first armed conflict of the American Revolution. The colonists’ alarm network, local militias, and determination to defend their towns showed that Britain faced not a few agitators but an armed population, and the contest over who fired first showed how quickly both sides fought for public opinion as well.1,2

Historical background

In 1774, after the Boston Tea Party, British warships closed Boston Harbor, and General Thomas Gage, now royal governor, dismissed the elected Massachusetts legislature under the Massachusetts Government Act.1 Patriots answered by forming a Provincial Congress, an “essentially illegal body” that took control of the militia and began stockpiling arms, ammunition, and supplies, aiming to raise and equip 15,000 men.1 Militia companies trained, and some towns organized “minute men” who could turn out quickly.1,2 Isolated in Boston, Gage followed advice from London and decided to strike at the supplies before the colonists were ready.1

Causes and contributing factors

Gage ordered Lieutenant Colonel Francis Smith to march “with the utmost expedition and secrecy to Concord,” where he was to “seize and destroy all artillery, Ammunition, Provisions, Tents, Small Arms, and all Military Stores whatever,” while taking care that the soldiers did not plunder.1 The orders said nothing about arresting political leaders, though patriots feared that too: John Hancock and Samuel Adams were staying in Lexington.1 Patriot intelligence learned of the plan almost at once.1

What happened

The alarm. On the night of April 18, Dr. Joseph Warren sent Paul Revere and William Dawes to warn Hancock, Adams, and the countryside. Dawes took the land route over Boston Neck, while Revere was rowed past a British warship to Charlestown.1 Two lanterns in the North Church steeple, a signal Revere had arranged, told patriots the troops were crossing by water.1,2 After warning Hancock and Adams, Revere, Dawes, and Samuel Prescott rode on toward Concord. A British patrol captured Revere; Dawes fled; Prescott jumped a fence and reached Concord around 1:30 a.m.1 Ferrying the regulars across the Charles took nearly three hours, and by the time they marched at about 2 a.m., bells and gunfire were spreading the alarm.1

Lexington. About 5 a.m. the British vanguard reached Lexington Green, where Captain John Parker’s militia company of just over seventy men stood. Parker ordered his men to disperse. A shot rang out, “historians still debate who fired it,” and the British fired volleys that killed seven militiamen and mortally wounded another.2 Each side blamed the other. The Provincial Congress rushed its account to London, describing British troops who “unprovoked, shed the blood” of Lexington men, while Gage reported that the militia, “about 200 armed Men,” had fired first.2 The American Battlefield Trust concludes that “we still don’t know the truth.”2

Concord. The British reached Concord about 8 a.m. and sent about 220 men to hold the North Bridge and search a farm for arms.2 Seeing smoke rise from the town, close to 400 militia on the high ground assumed the regulars were burning it and advanced. When the British fired, killing Captain Isaac Davis and another Acton man, Major John Buttrick shouted “For God’s sake, fire!” The militia’s volley killed three British soldiers and wounded nine.2 It became known as “the shot heard round the world.”2,1 In town, the widow Mary Moulton persuaded soldiers to put out a fire that threatened the Town House.2

The Battle Road. As Smith’s column turned back, militia from the surrounding towns attacked it from behind trees, walls, and buildings for the next twelve miles, and Parker’s Lexington men took revenge from cover.2 Reinforcements met the column near Lexington, but the running fight continued until the troops reached the protection of British ships around Boston.2

Immediate consequences

Militiamen poured in from across New England and besieged the British in Boston. The Yawp and the Battlefield Trust both put the force at about twenty thousand.3,2 In June the militia fortified Breed’s Hill, and in the misnamed Battle of Bunker Hill the British took the hill only at a heavy cost.3 When the Second Continental Congress met in May 1775, war had already begun.3

Long-term significance

April 19 turned a political struggle into a war. That summer the Continental Congress adopted the New England militia as the Continental Army under George Washington, and eight years of fighting followed.3,2 The Park Service notes that the day has been remembered by later generations as the “shot heard ’round the world,” and that Minute Man National Historical Park is both a battlefield and a graveyard where many soldiers killed that day remain.1
