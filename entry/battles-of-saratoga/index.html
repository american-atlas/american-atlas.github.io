Battles of Saratoga

The 1777 American victory that brought France into the war

September 19 – October 17, 1777 · Saratoga County, New York

In the autumn of 1777 a British army under General John Burgoyne, marching south from Canada to take the Hudson River Valley, was stopped and forced to surrender near Saratoga, New York.1,2 American forces under Horatio Gates fought Burgoyne at Freeman’s Farm on September 19 and at Bemis Heights on October 7, then surrounded his army, which surrendered on October 17.1 The American Yawp calls the victory “a major turning point in the war”: it convinced France that the American cause could succeed, and France signed treaties with the United States in February 1778.3,1

Why it matters

Saratoga ended Burgoyne’s attempt to control the Hudson River Valley. It proved the Americans could defeat a professional army in the field and persuaded France to enter the war, which made eventual independence far more likely.1,2,3

Historical background

British strategy for 1777 called for a three-pronged attack on New York, with armies converging near Albany. The aim was to control the Hudson River Valley, the route between Canada and New York City.1 Burgoyne set out south from Canada with about 7,500 men. By August he had taken Fort Ticonderoga, defeated retreating Americans at Hubbardton in Vermont, and reached Fort Edward on the Hudson.1

Causes and contributing factors

  • The Hudson strategy. Burgoyne’s campaign depended on the river route and on help from other British forces, above all Gen. Henry Clinton coming up from New York City.1
  • Losses on the march. A detachment of Burgoyne’s troops was defeated at the Battle of Bennington, and his reduced army marched toward Saratoga in early September.1
  • A strong American position. Gates had his men fortify Bemis Heights, bluffs overlooking both the Hudson and the only road south. The dense forest to the east meant the British would have to use that road, under American guns.1

What happened

Freeman’s Farm, September 19. Burgoyne split his army into three columns to probe the American defenses. Daniel Morgan’s riflemen and light infantry met the center column near Freeman’s Farm, and the field changed hands several times. By evening the British, reinforced by German troops, held the ground, but at the cost of almost 600 men, and their advance had stalled.1 Expecting Clinton’s relief, Burgoyne dug in.1

Waiting. No relief came. Food ran short and the British went on half rations, while the American army grew to about 13,000.1

Bemis Heights, October 7. Burgoyne sent a reconnaissance force against the American lines. The Americans drove it back to the Balcarres Redoubt, then captured the weakly held Breymann Redoubt, with Benedict Arnold riding in front to rally the attack. Arnold was badly wounded in the left leg.1

Surrender, October 17. On October 8 the British tried to retreat north, but heavy rain stopped them near Saratoga. Within two days they were surrounded, and after negotiations Burgoyne’s army surrendered on October 17.1

Immediate consequences

Burgoyne’s men became prisoners of war. Burgoyne himself returned to England in the spring of 1778, was severely criticized, and never received another command.1 Gates became a celebrated figure. He reported his victory directly to Congress rather than to Washington, and some in Congress talked of putting him in Washington’s place; the scheme, known as the Conway Cabal, failed.1 News of the victory reached France, and on February 6, 1778, France and the United States signed treaties of commerce and alliance.3,1

Long-term significance

Saratoga turned a rebellion into an international war. The National Park Service says the victory “renewed patriots’ hopes for independence” and secured “essential foreign recognition and support.”2 French money, troops and ships followed, and the Battlefield Trust notes that French support contributed to the victory at Yorktown in 1781; Spain and the Dutch Republic later aided the Americans as well.1,3

Benedict Arnold at Saratoga

Arnold argued with Gates on September 19, pressing him to meet the British advance instead of waiting behind the fortifications. After the battle Gates did not credit Arnold in his report to Congress, and Gates, not Arnold, was hailed as the hero of Saratoga.1 The American Battlefield Trust suggests this resentment, together with his marriage to the Loyalist Peggy Shippen in 1779, may help explain why in 1780 Arnold tried to hand West Point to the British.1

Remembering the battlefield

The battlefield is preserved as Saratoga National Historical Park. John Trumbull’s painting The Surrender of General Burgoyne hangs in the U.S. Capitol.1,2
