The White House

The president’s home and office since 1800, built partly by enslaved workers, burned in 1814 and rebuilt more than once

October 13, 1792–present · 1600 Pennsylvania Avenue NW, Washington, D.C.

The White House has been the home and workplace of every American president since John and Abigail Adams moved in, in November 1800. Designed by the Irish-born architect James Hoban, it was built between 1792 and 1800 by free workers and by enslaved people hired out by their owners. British troops burned it in 1814, and Hoban rebuilt it. Long called the “President’s House” or “Executive Mansion,” it got its official name from Theodore Roosevelt in 1901. Today it is a residence, an office, a stage for ceremonies, and a museum visited by hundreds of thousands of people a year.

Why it matters

The White House is one of the best-known symbols of the American presidency and of the nation itself. Its history also holds a contradiction that the White House Historical Association now highlights: the home of the leader of a nation founded on liberty was built in part by people held in slavery.

About the place

The White House stands on Pennsylvania Avenue in Washington, D.C., in President’s Park, on a site chosen by George Washington and the city’s planner, Peter Charles L’Enfant. The main house, faced with sandstone from a quarry on Virginia’s Aquia Creek, is flanked by the West Wing, with the president’s offices, and the East Wing. It serves at once as a family home, the president’s office, a ceremonial stage for the nation’s most important visitors, and a museum.

History

In March 1792 the commissioners of the new federal city announced a national design competition for the President’s House; Thomas Jefferson himself submitted a design, but in July the commissioners, with Washington’s approval, chose Hoban’s. The cornerstone was laid at a Masonic ceremony on October 13, 1792. Enslaved people worked on every stage of construction from 1792 to 1800 — quarrying stone, cutting timber, making bricks, and working as carpenters, sawyers, and laborers — alongside free white workers and craftsmen. Because the new capital had few residents, the commissioners hired enslaved people from slave owners in Maryland, Virginia, and the District of Columbia, and the owners collected their wages. Hoban himself owned enslaved carpenters — payrolls from 1794 to 1797 list four of them, Ben, Daniel, Harry, and Peter — and he grew rich building the new capital. In 1798 the stone walls were coated with a lime whitewash to protect them from freezing; contrary to legend, the house was white long before the British burned it.

On August 24, 1814, during the War of 1812, British troops set the house on fire. Hoban rebuilt it by 1817, again partly with enslaved labor. Presidents kept changing it: Jefferson added colonnades, James Monroe the South Portico, and Andrew Jackson the North Portico in 1829–30. In 1902 Theodore Roosevelt replaced the old glass conservatories with the West Wing, separating the president’s offices from the family’s home; Franklin Roosevelt added the East Wing in 1942.

By the late 1940s, decades of careless alterations had left the house in danger of collapse. From 1948 to 1952, while Harry Truman’s family lived across the street at Blair House, workers tore out everything inside the outer stone walls and rebuilt the interior on a steel frame with deep new concrete foundations. The project cost $5.7 million and changed the house more than the fire of 1814 had. Some of Hoban’s old pine beams were milled into paneling for the ground-floor rooms.
