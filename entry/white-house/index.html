The White House

The president’s home and office since 1800

October 13, 1792–present · 1600 Pennsylvania Avenue NW, Washington, D.C.

The White House has been the home and workplace of every American president since John and Abigail Adams moved in, in November 1800.6 Designed by the Irish-born architect James Hoban, it was built between 1792 and 1800 by free workers and by enslaved people hired out by their owners.1,2 British troops burned it in 1814, and it was rebuilt by 1817.4 Long called the “President’s House” or “Executive Mansion,” it got its official name from Theodore Roosevelt in 1901.3 Today it is a residence, an office, a stage for ceremonies and a museum that welcomes over 500,000 visitors a year.5

Why it matters

The White House is one of the best-known symbols of the American presidency and of the nation itself. Its history also holds a contradiction that the White House Historical Association now highlights: the home of the leader of a nation founded on liberty was built in part by people held in slavery, a “paradoxical relationship” between slavery and “the ideals of freedom and liberty enshrined in America’s founding documents.”1

About the place

The White House stands on Pennsylvania Avenue in Washington, D.C., on a site chosen by George Washington’s commissioners and the city’s planner, Pierre (Peter) Charles L’Enfant.1 Its sandstone came from a quarry on Aquia Creek in Stafford County, Virginia, which the federal government bought in 1791.1 The main house is flanked by the West Wing, with the president’s offices, and the East Wing. The White House Historical Association describes it as at once a family home, the president’s office, “the ceremonial stage upon which our nation welcomes its most important visitors,” and a museum.5

History

Building the President’s House. After the Residence Act of 1790 placed the capital on the Potomac, Washington appointed three commissioners to manage construction. In March 1792 they announced a national design competition, and in July they chose Hoban’s design.1 The cornerstone was laid on October 13, 1792.1

Enslaved people worked on every stage of construction from 1792 to 1800, as axemen, stone cutters, carpenters, brick makers, sawyers and laborers, alongside free white workers and craftsmen.1 All three original commissioners were enslavers. On April 13, 1792, they resolved to hire “good labouring negroes by the year,” paying the owners, who collected the wages.1 Historian Bob Arnebeck has identified over 200 enslaved people who worked on the White House and the Capitol, and there were likely many more.1 Hoban himself owned enslaved carpenters: a May 1795 payroll lists Peter, Ben, Harry and Daniel, whose wages Hoban signed for.2

Fire and rebuilding. In 1798 the stone walls received a lime whitewash to protect them from winter freezes.3 On August 24, 1814, during the War of 1812, British troops burned the house. It was rebuilt by 1817, again partly with enslaved labor.4,1 Presidents kept changing it: Jefferson added colonnades, James Monroe the South Portico, and Andrew Jackson the North Portico.5

The modern White House. In 1902 Theodore Roosevelt modernized the house and added the West Wing as the president’s office space; Franklin Roosevelt added the East Wing in 1942.4,5 By the time the Trumans moved in in 1945, decades of careless alterations and the lack of foundations under its interior walls had left it “on the verge of literal collapse.”6 From 1948 to 1952, while the Trumans lived across the street at Blair House, the interior was gutted and rebuilt on a steel frame with 22-foot-deep foundations, at a cost of $5.7 million.6,5 Truman had the old cracked beams milled into paneling for the ground-floor rooms.6

Why “white”?

A popular story says the house was painted white to cover the scorch marks of 1814. In fact it was first whitewashed in 1798. The name “White House” appeared now and then in nineteenth-century newspapers, but most people said “President’s House” or “Executive Mansion.” On October 17, 1901, at Roosevelt’s direction, his secretary asked that official papers be headed “White House.”3

Four dates in the floor

A marker in the Entrance Hall floor records four dates: 1792 (the cornerstone), 1817 (the rebuilding after the fire), 1902 (Theodore Roosevelt’s renovation) and 1952 (the end of the Truman reconstruction).4 The cornerstone itself has never been found, even during the Truman gutting.1

James Hoban

Hoban, born to a modest family in County Kilkenny, Ireland, studied architectural drawing in Dublin before emigrating. In Charleston, South Carolina, he bought at least one enslaved person, a carpenter named Peter, and in 1789 advertised a reward for his return after Peter “absented himself.” The White House Historical Association writes that enslaved workers played a vital role in Hoban’s “rise to prominence and prosperity” in Washington.2
