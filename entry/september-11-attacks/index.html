September 11 Attacks

The al-Qaeda terrorist attacks of 2001

September 11, 2001 · New York City; Arlington, Virginia; near Shanksville, Pennsylvania

On the morning of September 11, 2001, nineteen al-Qaeda terrorists hijacked four passenger planes. Two were flown into the towers of the World Trade Center in New York, which collapsed, and one into the Pentagon outside Washington.1,2 The fourth, United Flight 93, crashed in a Pennsylvania field after its passengers and crew fought back.5 Nearly 3,000 people were killed, more than at Pearl Harbor.1 The attacks led to the war in Afghanistan, a global “war on terror,” and the Iraq War, and to new security powers at home.2,3

Why it matters

September 11 was the defining event of the early twenty-first century for the United States. It transformed its foreign policy, its sense of security, and the balance between liberty and security at home.2,4

Historical background

Al-Qaeda grew out of the war against the Soviet occupation of Afghanistan in the 1980s. Young Muslims from around the world went to Afghanistan to join the fight, among them Osama bin Laden, a wealthy Saudi, and afterwards he and others formed al-Qaeda to wage holy war elsewhere.1,2 The United States had armed and financed the Afghan resistance against the Soviets.2 Bin Laden attacked the United States for keeping troops in Saudi Arabia, home of Islam’s holiest sites, and for its policies in the Middle East.1 In February 1998 he and four others issued a “fatwa” declaring it the duty of every Muslim to kill Americans, military or civilian, anywhere in the world.1 After the Taliban took power in Afghanistan in 1996, al-Qaeda based itself there, and by 2001, the American Yawp writes, it was active in more than sixty countries.2,1

The 9/11 Commission traced a decade of warnings: the 1993 truck bomb at the World Trade Center, which killed six, and al-Qaeda’s bombings of the U.S. embassies in Kenya and Tanzania in August 1998, which killed 224 people, including 12 Americans.1 In October 2000 an al-Qaeda team blew a hole in the destroyer USS Cole in Yemen, killing 17 sailors.1 The Clinton administration fired cruise missiles at al-Qaeda camps in Afghanistan and Sudan and pressed the Taliban to expel bin Laden, but neither step dislodged him.1,2

Causes and contributing factors

By late 1998 or early 1999 bin Laden had approved a plan brought to him by Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, the “planes operation.” Mohammed said his original idea had called for ten planes to attack both coasts.1 Training began in the fall of 1999. Four recruits came from a cell of Islamist extremists in Hamburg, Germany, and one of them, Mohamed Atta, became the tactical commander in the United States.1 Two operatives arrived in Los Angeles in January 2000 after U.S. intelligence briefly tracked them in Asia and then lost them.1 By the summer of 2000 the future pilots had begun flight training in the United States.1

In the spring and summer of 2001 American intelligence received a stream of warnings that al-Qaeda planned “something very, very, very big.” CIA Director George Tenet told the Commission that “the system was blinking red.”1 An August 2001 briefing reminded President George W. Bush that bin Laden was determined to strike in the United States, but the specific threat reports pointed overseas, and domestic agencies were not mobilized.1 Zacarias Moussaoui was arrested in Minnesota on August 16 after he aroused suspicion at a flight school, and in late August officials realized that the two operatives spotted in Asia were in the country.1 The Commission concluded that the attacks “were a shock, but they should not have come as a surprise.”1

What happened

The hijackers chose nonstop coast-to-coast flights that were lightly booked, full of fuel, and scheduled to leave at about the same time.5,4 In groups of four or five, armed only with small knives, box cutters, and Mace or pepper spray, they seized the planes.1

At 8:46 a.m. American Airlines Flight 11 crashed into the North Tower of the World Trade Center. At 9:03 United Airlines Flight 175 hit the South Tower.1,2 Up to 50,000 people worked in the towers each day.1 At 9:37 American Airlines Flight 77 struck the western face of the Pentagon.1 At 9:45 the Federal Aviation Administration ordered every aircraft in the sky to land, the first such order in American history, and an estimated 4,500 planes landed safely.5 The South Tower collapsed at 9:59, and the North Tower at 10:28.2

United Flight 93, bound from Newark to San Francisco, had left more than 25 minutes late because of heavy traffic. It carried 33 passengers, 7 crew members, and 4 hijackers.6 At 9:28, near Cleveland, the hijackers stormed the cockpit and turned the plane toward Washington.6 Passengers and crew, forced to the back, made 37 phone calls and learned of the other attacks. They voted to fight back, and at 9:57 they began their assault on the cockpit.6,5 The hijacker at the controls rolled and pitched the plane, and the hijackers chose to crash it rather than lose control. It hit an open field near Shanksville at 10:03, about 20 minutes’ flying time from Washington.5,6 Everyone on board died.5 The sources describe its target in slightly different terms. The 9/11 Commission says it was aimed at the Capitol or the White House, and the National Park Service says the Capitol was most likely, noting that at least one victim’s relative was told it was the White House.1,5,6

Immediate consequences

More than 2,600 people died at the World Trade Center, 125 at the Pentagon, and 256 on the four planes.1 The 9/11 Memorial lists the names of nearly 3,000 victims, together with those of the 1993 bombing, on bronze panels around two pools set in the footprints of the Twin Towers.7 U.S. intelligence quickly identified al-Qaeda, which the Taliban was sheltering in Afghanistan.2,4

On September 18 Bush signed a joint resolution of Congress authorizing force against those responsible.4 Two days later he told Congress: “Our war on terror begins with al Qaida, but it does not end there. It will not end until every terrorist group of global reach has been found, stopped, and defeated.” Every nation, he said, had to decide: “Either you are with us, or you are with the terrorists.”8 On October 7 American air strikes began against the Taliban and al-Qaeda. With American special forces, the anti-Taliban Northern Alliance took Kabul on November 13, and bin Laden and his followers escaped into the mountains along the Pakistani border.4,2 In October 2001 Bush signed the USA PATRIOT Act to expand domestic surveillance and crack down on terrorist financing, and Congress created a new Department of Homeland Security.4,3

Long-term significance

The attacks transformed American foreign policy. The Bush Doctrine claimed the right to strike preemptively at any regime or terrorist group that threatened the United States. It led to long wars in Afghanistan, the longest in American history, and in Iraq. The journalist Dexter Filkins called it a “forever war.”2 The Miller Center notes that the response greatly expanded presidential power in matters of national security.4

The war on terror also raised hard questions at home. The administration held accused terrorists at Guantánamo Bay without the right to challenge their detention in court, and classified them as unlawful enemy combatants outside the protections of the Geneva Conventions. It also approved interrogation methods including waterboarding, which many critics called torture.4,2 Some cases reached the Supreme Court, and critics argued that the country was abandoning principles such as the rule of law.4 A team of Navy SEALs killed Osama bin Laden at his hideout near Abbottabad, Pakistan, on May 2, 2011.9

The bipartisan 9/11 Commission, five Republicans and five Democrats, issued its unanimous report in 2004.1 It found that the nation “was unprepared” and that the government had failed in imagination, policy, capabilities, and management.1 The Flight 93 National Memorial and the 9/11 Memorial & Museum now mark the crash site and Ground Zero.5,7
