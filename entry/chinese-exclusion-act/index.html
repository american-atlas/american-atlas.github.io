The Chinese Exclusion Act

The first federal law to bar immigrants by nationality, 1882

May 6, 1882

The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred Chinese laborers from immigrating to the United States for ten years, the first time federal law excluded people on the basis of nationality and race. Extended and tightened in 1892 and made permanent in 1902, exclusion lasted until 1943. It shaped Chinese American life for generations and set the pattern for later laws restricting immigration from Asia and other parts of the world.

Why it matters

The act made race and nationality explicit grounds for excluding immigrants. The Geary Act of 1892 added a requirement that every Chinese resident register and carry a certificate of residence or face deportation. On the West Coast, the Angel Island Immigration Station was built to detain and interrogate Chinese arrivals. The precedent shaped the national-origins quotas of the Immigration Act of 1924, which barred nearly all immigration from Asia. Congress repealed Chinese exclusion in 1943, when China was a wartime ally.

Historical circumstances

Chinese immigrants, mostly from Guangdong Province, came to California from the 1850s, driven by famine, rebellion, and economic collapse at home. Welcomed at first as laborers in the gold fields and on the railroads, they became scapegoats when the economy faltered in the 1870s. Politicians and labor leaders in the West stirred anti-Chinese hostility, and states and cities passed laws restricting where Chinese people could live and work. Because the 1868 Burlingame-Seward Treaty with China protected Chinese immigration, the federal government first negotiated the Angell Treaty of 1880, which allowed the United States to restrict — though not completely prohibit — Chinese immigration.

Principal ideas

  • Exclusion of laborers. Chinese laborers, defined as “skilled and unskilled… and Chinese employed in mining,” were barred for ten years.
  • Narrow exceptions. Merchants, diplomats, students, and other non-laborers could enter only with certification from the Chinese government — difficult to obtain in practice.
  • No naturalization. Federal and state courts were forbidden to grant citizenship to Chinese immigrants, who could still be deported.
  • Re-entry certificates. Chinese residents who left the country needed certificates to return.
