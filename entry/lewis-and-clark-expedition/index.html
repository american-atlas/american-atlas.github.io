The Lewis and Clark Expedition

The Corps of Discovery crosses the continent, 1804–1806

May 14, 1804 – September 23, 1806 · From St. Louis up the Missouri River, across the Rockies, to the Pacific coast and back

At Thomas Jefferson’s direction, Meriwether Lewis and William Clark led the Corps of Discovery, a small party made up mostly of U.S. Army enlisted men, up the Missouri River from near St. Louis in May 1804.2 They crossed the Rocky Mountains, reached the Pacific coast in November 1805 and returned to St. Louis on September 23, 1806, after a journey the National Archives puts at 8,000 miles.1,2,6 They mapped the West, recorded its plants and animals, and met more than fifty Native nations.1,3 Their survival depended on Native hosts and guides, among them Sacagawea, a Lemhi Shoshone woman, and on the labor of York, a man enslaved by Clark.3,4,8

Why it matters

The Lewis and Clark Expedition carried American claims across the continent and gave the government its first detailed knowledge of the West. It succeeded because of Native hosts and guides, a young Shoshone mother and an enslaved man, and it opened the way for the settlement that would take most of the West’s Native land.1,3

Historical background

Even before the Louisiana Purchase, Jefferson believed the United States should stretch across the continent. In a secret message of January 18, 1803, while the territory still belonged to France, he asked Congress for $2,500 to explore the West to the Pacific.1 The National Archives notes that the modest request, explained “principally in terms of promoting commerce,” concealed his larger expansionist goals.1 In the same message Jefferson described Native nations as increasingly unwilling to sell their land, and proposed ways to bring them to give up hunting and cede territory.1

Causes and contributing factors

Jefferson chose Lewis, his former private secretary, to lead the expedition, and Lewis chose his friend and former commanding officer, William Clark, to share the command.2 Their orders were to explore the Missouri to its source, find the most direct water route to the Pacific, report on the geography, plants, animals and climate of the West, and establish relations with the Native nations they met while impressing them with American power.1,2 Jefferson helped plan the supplies: arms, food, medicines, scientific instruments and gifts for Native leaders. The expedition ran out of whiskey, tobacco and salt, but never of rifles, powder, ink or paper.1

What happened

The Corps started up the Missouri from near St. Louis on May 14, 1804. After five months it built Fort Mandan near the Mandan villages, 1,600 miles upriver in present-day North Dakota, and wintered there. There the captains hired the French-Canadian trader Toussaint Charbonneau and his young Shoshone wife, Sacagawea, as interpreters.2

In April 1805 the Corps left Fort Mandan and followed the Missouri into country no American had mapped. Along the Lemhi River in present-day Idaho, Sacagawea’s people provided horses and a guide for the crossing of the Continental Divide.2 After some 600 miles by water down the Clearwater, Snake and Columbia rivers, Clark wrote on November 7, 1805, “Ocian in view! O! the joy,” though he was actually looking at the Columbia’s estuary; the Pacific itself was still a couple of weeks away.2 The party wintered at Fort Clatsop, named for the local Clatsop people, from December 1805 to March 1806.2

On the way home the Corps split up to explore more country. Sacagawea guided Clark’s party over Bozeman Pass into the Yellowstone valley, and on July 25, 1806, Clark carved his name on the rock he called “Pompy’s Tower,” after Sacagawea’s son.5 Lewis’s group had the expedition’s only violent encounter, a fight with a small party of Blackfeet (Pikuni).6 The Corps reunited, left Sacagawea’s family at the Mandan villages, and reached St. Louis at about noon on September 23, 1806, where, Sergeant John Ordway wrote, “the people gathred on the Shore and Huzzared three cheers.”6,7

Immediate consequences

The expedition did not find an all-water route to the Pacific. But it gave the U.S. government “its first glimpse of the vast lands that lay west of the Mississippi River.”1 Its maps and specimens went to the American Philosophical Society in Philadelphia.6 Clark became Indian agent at St. Louis, governor of the Missouri Territory and superintendent of Indian affairs; Lewis became governor of the Louisiana Territory but struggled in the post and died of gunshot wounds in 1809, commonly believed to have been suicide.6 York, after all his service, was denied both his payment and his freedom.4

Long-term significance

Lewis and Clark became national heroes and their journey a symbol of American exploration. The National Park Service also calls it “a watershed moment that set forth untold loss” to the very tribes who helped the expedition survive. The Corps traded with, learned from and depended on more than fifty Native nations, whose food, knowledge and skills were “essential to the expedition’s survival.”3 Today the 4,900-mile Lewis and Clark National Historic Trail tells the story from many perspectives, including those of “a president and an enslaved man, women and men, French-speaking boatmen and American Indians.”3

Where and when did it begin?

The start of the expedition can be dated in several ways. Jefferson’s secret request to Congress came in January 1803, while the Park Service’s Fort Clatsop page says he “announced plans” for the expedition in June 1803.1,2 The National Historic Trail commemorates a Corps that “departed Pittsburgh” in 1803; the voyage up the Missouri began from near St. Louis on May 14, 1804.3,2

York

York was a frontiersman and hunter, enslaved by Clark, and likely the first African American to cross the continent. The Park Service calls him “an American explorer who made important contributions” to the expedition; after it returned, he “was denied his payment and his freedom.”4
