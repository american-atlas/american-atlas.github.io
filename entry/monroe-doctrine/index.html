The Monroe Doctrine

The United States warns Europe away from the Americas, 1823

December 2, 1823

In his seventh annual message to Congress, on December 2, 1823, President James Monroe declared that the American continents were “henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers.”1,2 In return, the United States would stay out of Europe’s wars and leave its existing colonies alone.1 Shaped largely by Secretary of State John Quincy Adams, the statement was meant to protect the newly independent Latin American republics and American interests.2,4 Europe’s great powers barely noticed it at the time, and the young country lacked the power to enforce it, but it became, in the National Archives’ words, “the best known U.S. policy toward the Western Hemisphere.”1,2,4

Why it matters

The doctrine began as an aspiration. The Yawp writes that “the reality of limited military power kept the Monroe Doctrine as an aspirational assertion,” and the Office of the Historian notes it was “little noted by the Great Powers of Europe.”4,2 Over the following century it became a guiding principle of U.S. policy in the hemisphere, and later presidents used it to justify interventions that Monroe and Adams had not imagined.1,2

Historical circumstances

By the early 1820s most of Spain’s American colonies had won their independence. Americans feared that Spain and France might try to restore colonial rule, and Russia was pushing south from Alaska toward the Oregon country.2 The American Yawp adds British claims in Canada and British interest in Cuba, America’s chief Latin American trading partner, to the list of worries.4

Britain, too, wanted Spain’s empire to stay broken up, since Spanish trade restrictions had shut British merchants out. Earlier in 1823 the British foreign secretary, George Canning, suggested that the two countries issue a joint warning against intervention.2 Adams opposed it. A joint statement, he argued, might limit future American expansion, and the British had imperial motives of their own. So the proposed joint declaration became a statement by the United States alone.2

Principal ideas

  • No new colonization. The American continents, “by the free and independent condition which they have assumed and maintain,” were closed to future European colonization.1
  • Separate spheres. Any European attempt “to extend their system to any portion of this hemisphere” would be considered “dangerous to our peace and safety.”1
  • No intervention against the new republics. European interference with the independent states the United States had recognized would be seen as “the manifestation of an unfriendly disposition toward the United States.”1
  • Hands off in return. “With the existing colonies or dependencies of any European power we have not interfered and shall not interfere,” and in Europe’s own wars “we have never taken any part.” Monroe’s administration stayed out, for example, of the Greek struggle for independence from the Ottoman Empire.1,2

Roots in earlier policy

Monroe and Adams built on older ideals: staying out of European quarrels, as Washington had urged in his Farewell Address, and defending neutral rights, Madison’s reason for the War of 1812.2 Adams had stated his own view in a speech of 1821, warning that if America enlisted under any banners but her own, “the fundamental maxims of her policy would insensibly change from liberty to force.”4 The Yawp dates this speech July 4, 1821, while its footnote cites it as an oration of July 21, 1821.4

From warning to intervention

In 1865 the United States used diplomatic and military pressure to support the Mexican president Benito Juárez against the emperor Maximilian, whom France had placed on the throne.1 In 1904 Theodore Roosevelt claimed for the United States an “international police power” in the hemisphere. This Roosevelt Corollary, the Office of the Historian writes, “inverted the original meaning of the doctrine.”1,2 U.S. Marines went to Santo Domingo in 1904, Nicaragua in 1911 and Haiti in 1915, and Latin American nations viewed these interventions with misgiving.1 In 1962 the doctrine was invoked again during the Cuban Missile Crisis.1

A sign of postwar confidence

The Yawp suggests that the doctrine “may have been the boldest declaration of America’s postwar pride” after the War of 1812. Although Monroe was a Jeffersonian, he favored a strong military and an assertive foreign policy, positions closer to the old Federalists.3
