The Monroe Doctrine

The United States warns Europe away from the Americas, 1823

December 2, 1823

In his annual message to Congress on December 2, 1823, President James Monroe declared that the Americas were “henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers,” and that the United States would stay out of European affairs. Largely the work of Secretary of State John Quincy Adams, the policy aimed to protect newly independent Latin American republics. Over time it became the cornerstone of U.S. policy in the hemisphere, and a justification for U.S. intervention there.

Key facts

Date
December 2, 1823
Authors
President James Monroe; Secretary of State John Quincy Adams (principal architect)
Announced
December 2, 1823, in Monroe’s seventh annual message to Congress
Principles
No new European colonization in the Americas; no European interference with independent American states; U.S. non-involvement in European affairs
Proposed alternative
A joint U.S.–British declaration, suggested by British Foreign Secretary George Canning — rejected by Adams
Later extension
The Roosevelt Corollary (1904–05)

Read the text at the National Archives

Why it matters

When it was issued, the doctrine was a bold statement from a young country with limited power to enforce it. In the late 1800s, growing U.S. economic and military strength made enforcement possible. Combined with ideas of Manifest Destiny, it supported U.S. expansion on the continent; in 1865 it was invoked to support Mexico’s Benito Juárez against the French-installed Emperor Maximilian; and Theodore Roosevelt’s Corollary turned it into a justification for unilateral U.S. intervention in Latin America — inverting its original meaning. As the National Archives notes, U.S. expressions of concern for its neighbors “have not always been favorably regarded by other American nations.”

Historical circumstances

In the early 1820s most of Spain’s American colonies had won independence. Americans feared that Spain and its allies in Europe’s conservative monarchies might try to reconquer them, and Russia was pressing south from Alaska toward the Oregon country. In 1823 British Foreign Secretary George Canning proposed that Britain and the United States jointly warn other powers against intervening in Latin America. John Quincy Adams argued that a joint statement might limit future U.S. expansion and that Britain had imperial motives of its own; he persuaded Monroe to act alone.

Principal ideas

  • No new colonization. The American continents were closed to future European colonization.
  • Separate spheres. The political systems of Europe and the Americas were different; European attempts to extend their system to the Western Hemisphere would be considered dangerous to U.S. peace and safety.
  • Non-interference both ways. The United States would not interfere with existing European colonies or take part in European wars and politics — such as the Greek war of independence then under way.
