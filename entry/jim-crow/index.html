Jim Crow

The system of legal segregation and disenfranchisement, 1880s–1960s

1880s – 1965

“Jim Crow” was the system of laws and customs that segregated Black Americans and stripped them of the vote after Reconstruction, named after a Black character in minstrel shows.3 From the 1880s into the 1960s, Southern states, and many others, required separate schools, railroad cars, waiting rooms, and cemeteries, and they barred Black men from voting with poll taxes and literacy tests.3,2,5 The Supreme Court approved “separate but equal” facilities in Plessy v. Ferguson in 1896, and the whole system was enforced by violence, including thousands of lynchings.4,1 The civil rights movement, Brown v. Board of Education, and the civil rights laws of 1964 and 1965 finally dismantled it.9,10,11

Why it matters

Jim Crow made a mockery of the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments for most of a century. It denied Black Americans equal schooling, fair wages, justice in the courts, and the vote, and it kept the South poor and divided.1,2 The struggle to end it, from Ida B. Wells to the March on Washington, produced the civil rights movement and the laws of 1964 and 1965, among the most important reforms in American history.7,10,11

Definition

Jim Crow was a system of white supremacy built on three foundations: segregation, disenfranchisement, and violence.1,2 Segregation laws separated Black and white Americans in public life, from schools and trains to restaurants, theaters, restrooms, hospitals, and graveyards, and banned marriage between the races, punishing violators under the law.3,1 Disenfranchisement laws used poll taxes, literacy tests, and other devices to keep Black men from voting despite the Fifteenth Amendment.2,5 And lynching and mob violence punished anyone who challenged the order.1 The name came from “Jim Crow,” a Black character in minstrel shows.3 Although Jim Crow is most associated with the South, the National Park Service notes that a majority of states, from Delaware to California and from North Dakota to Texas, had such laws at some point.3

Historical context

The end of Reconstruction. When federal troops withdrew from the South in 1877, white Democrats who called themselves “Redeemers” regained power across the region, and the number of Black officeholders declined significantly.6 At first segregation was often a matter of custom. Soon white Southerners began writing it into law.1 In 1883 the Supreme Court struck down the Civil Rights Act of 1875, which had banned discrimination in inns, theaters, and public transportation, ruling that the Fourteenth Amendment did not let Congress prevent discrimination by private individuals.4,2

Separate cars. Railroads were among the first targets. Beginning with a Florida law of 1887, states required railroads to provide separate accommodations for each race.4 (The American Yawp dates the first such law to 1888, or to Tennessee in the 1880s.)2,1 In 1890, despite protests from New Orleans’s Black community and the presence of sixteen Black legislators, Louisiana passed a Separate Car Act. A Citizens’ Committee formed to challenge it arranged a test case: on June 7, 1892, Homer Plessy, a man of mixed ancestry, sat in a white car on the East Louisiana Railroad and was arrested.4 On May 18, 1896, the Supreme Court upheld the law. Justice Henry Brown wrote that if Black people saw segregation as “a badge of inferiority,” it was “solely because the colored race chooses to put that construction upon it.”4 Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented alone: “Our Constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens.”2 Harlan warned that the decision would “permit the seeds of race hatred to be planted under the sanction of law.” Southern whites soon segregated nearly every public space.2

Taking the vote. Southern Democrats had suppressed Black voting with fraud and intimidation since Reconstruction, but between about 1890 and 1908 they wrote disenfranchisement into law.1 Mississippi led the way in 1890 with a new state constitution. It imposed a poll tax, took the vote from people convicted of petty crimes thought common among Black residents, and required a literacy test. An “understanding clause” let local officials pass illiterate whites who could “explain” a passage read to them.2 Other states added “grandfather clauses” that excused men whose ancestors had voted in the 1860s.5 Supporters claimed these measures would “purify” the ballot and end corruption.2,1 The laws, the American Yawp writes, moved the fight from the ballot box to the registrar’s office, where “supposedly color-blind laws allowed local party officials to deny the ballot without the appearance of fraud.”2

Terror. Lynching, the murder of accused people by mobs without trial, enforced the system. White mobs murdered roughly five thousand African Americans between the 1880s and the 1950s. At the height of the violence in the late 1890s, two or three Black Southerners were lynched every week.1 Mississippi mobs killed more than 500 people and Georgia mobs more than 400 between 1880 and 1930.1 Lynchings often became public spectacles. In 1899, special trains carried some four thousand people from Atlanta to Newnan, Georgia, to watch Sam Hose tortured and burned alive.1 White political violence also targeted Black political power. In Wilmington, North Carolina, in 1898, armed white Democrats overthrew the city’s elected biracial government in what the American Yawp calls “a full-blown coup,” killing dozens of Black residents and driving hundreds from the city.1

Resistance. Black Southerners never accepted Jim Crow. In 1884 the young teacher Ida B. Wells was dragged from the ladies’ car of a train in Tennessee and sued the railroad. After a white mob lynched three of her friends in Memphis in 1892, she began a lifelong investigation of lynching. Her Southern Horrors (1892) and A Red Record (1895) exposed the lie that lynching punished Black men for assaulting white women.7,1 In 1909 a National Negro Conference in New York, where Wells presented her research on lynching, led to the founding of the NAACP, whose lawyers would eventually bring down legal segregation.7,9 During World War II, Black newspapers called for a “Double V,” victory over fascism abroad and racism at home. NAACP membership grew tenfold, and some 1.5 million Black Southerners left the South in the 1940s alone in the Great Migration.8 In 1954 the Supreme Court ruled school segregation unconstitutional in Brown v. Board of Education, and the mass protests of the civil rights movement followed.9

The end of legal Jim Crow. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 outlawed segregation in public accommodations and discrimination in employment.10 The Voting Rights Act of 1965 banned literacy tests and sent federal examiners to register voters. By the end of 1966, only four of thirteen Southern states had fewer than half of their eligible Black citizens registered to vote.11

Examples

Laws collected by the National Park Service show how far segregation reached:3 - Georgia barred white and Black amateur baseball teams from playing within two blocks of a playground set aside for the other race. - Alabama required separate waiting rooms and ticket windows for the races at bus stations, and separate toilets at workplaces. - Georgia forbade burying Black people in ground set apart for whites. - Louisiana required separate buildings, on separate grounds, for blind people of each race.

Other examples: - Louisiana’s Separate Car Act (1890), upheld in Plessy v. Ferguson, fined passengers $25 or jailed them up to twenty days for sitting in the wrong car. It exempted Black nurses caring for white children.4 - Mississippi’s constitution of 1890, the model for disenfranchisement across the South.2 - Segregated work, in which the best-paid factory jobs went to whites and Black workers got the dirtiest and most dangerous ones; Southern mill villages were for whites only.1
