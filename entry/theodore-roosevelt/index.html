Theodore Roosevelt

Twenty-sixth president and champion of the “Square Deal”

1858–1919

Theodore Roosevelt became president in 1901, at forty-two, after the assassination of William McKinley. He was the youngest person ever to hold the office.2 Historians often call him the first modern president, because he made the White House, rather than Congress or the parties, the center of American politics.1,8 His “Square Deal” used federal power to regulate big business, protect consumers, and conserve the nation’s forests and wild lands.4 Abroad he built up the Navy, secured the Panama Canal, and declared the United States the “policeman” of the Western Hemisphere.5 In 1912 he ran again as the candidate of his own Progressive Party.3

Key facts

Roles
New York assemblyman (1882–1884); U.S. Civil Service commissioner; President of the New York City Police Board; Assistant secretary of the Navy (1897–1898); Rough Rider in the Spanish-American War; Governor of New York; Vice president (1901); Twenty-sixth president (1901–1909); Progressive (“Bull Moose”) Party candidate (1912)
Born
October 27, 1858, New York City
Presidency
1901–1909; Republican; took office after McKinley’s assassination and was elected in his own right in 1904
Domestic program
The Square Deal: the Northern Securities suit, the Hepburn Act, the Meat Inspection and Pure Food and Drug Acts (1906)
Conservation
150 national forests, 51 bird reserves, 5 national parks, and 18 national monuments
Foreign policy
“Speak softly and carry a big stick”; Panama Canal; Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine
Nobel Peace Prize
For mediating the end of the Russo-Japanese War (1905); the first U.S. president to win it
Died
January 6, 1919, Sagamore Hill, Oyster Bay, New York

Why it matters

Roosevelt changed what Americans expected of a president. He argued that the president could use any power the Constitution did not specifically deny him, and he lent the prestige of the White House to the progressive causes of regulation, consumer protection, and conservation.8,1 He also led the United States toward an active, often interventionist role in the world.5,10

Early life and context

Theodore Roosevelt was born in New York City on October 27, 1858, the second of four children of Theodore Roosevelt Sr., a wealthy businessman and philanthropist, and Martha “Mittie” Roosevelt, who had grown up on a Georgia plantation.2 A sickly child with asthma, “Teedie” resolved as a teenager to “make his body” through gymnastics and weight-lifting, and he remained a lifelong champion of exercise and what he called the “strenuous life.”2,1 He was taught at home, traveled with his family in Europe and the Middle East, and entered Harvard College in 1876, where he studied natural history and took up boxing and wrestling.2 In October 1880 he married Alice Hathaway Lee. He enrolled at Columbia Law School but left after a year for politics, and from 1882 to 1884 he served in the New York Assembly.2 That same year he published his first book, The Naval War of 1812.2

On February 12, 1884, Alice gave birth to a daughter. Two days later Roosevelt’s mother died of typhoid fever and his wife died of kidney disease, within hours of each other and in the same house.2 Leaving the baby with his sister, he went to the Dakota Badlands, where he had first come to hunt in 1883. He bought two ranches, herded cattle, and served as a frontier sheriff, and what he saw there of vanishing bison and overgrazed land shaped his later interest in conservation.2,12 In 1886 he returned east, ran unsuccessfully for mayor of New York City, and married his childhood sweetheart, Edith Kermit Carow. They settled at Sagamore Hill in Oyster Bay, New York, and had five children, Theodore, Kermit, Ethel, Archibald, and Quentin, besides Alice.2,7

Roosevelt kept writing, producing biographies and the four-volume The Winning of the West.2 He was appointed to the U.S. Civil Service Commission by the Republican Benjamin Harrison and reappointed by the Democrat Grover Cleveland, and he clashed constantly with politicians who wanted jobs handed out as party rewards. In 1895 he became president of the New York City Police Board, where he fought corruption.2 In 1897 President McKinley made him assistant secretary of the navy, a post that the American Yawp connects to his support for McKinley in 1896.2,10 A believer in sea power, influenced by the naval theorist Alfred Thayer Mahan, he put the Navy on alert after the battleship Maine exploded in Havana Harbor in 1898 and told Commodore George Dewey to prepare for war with Spain.2,10

When war came, Roosevelt resigned and became commander of the 1st U.S. Volunteer Cavalry, the “Rough Riders,” a mix of Ivy League gentlemen, cowboys, sheriffs, prospectors, police officers, and Native Americans. In Cuba he led them in a charge on foot up San Juan Hill (in fact Kettle Hill) outside Santiago.2,10 “I would rather have led that charge and earned my colonelcy than served three terms in the United States Senate,” he wrote.2 He came home a hero and was elected governor of New York in 1898 by a few thousand votes. In office he refused to give the top patronage jobs to party regulars and backed a tax on utility companies, angering the state Republican boss Thomas Platt.2 Platt and the national party leader Mark Hanna decided to “kick him upstairs” to the vice presidency in 1900. Roosevelt campaigned tirelessly for McKinley, traveling more than 21,000 miles.2 When McKinley was shot in September 1901, “that damned cowboy,” as Hanna called him, became president.2

Major actions and achievements

The Square Deal. Roosevelt believed government had the right to regulate big business for the public good, though he did not want to abolish it.4 In 1902 his Justice Department sued the Northern Securities Company, a giant railroad combination organized by J. P. Morgan and other financiers, under the Sherman Antitrust Act. In 1904 the Supreme Court ordered it broken up.4,9 When an anthracite coal strike threatened the nation’s winter heating in 1902, he called the mine owners and labor representatives to the White House. When the owners refused to negotiate, he threatened to have troops seize and run the mines, and both sides agreed to arbitration. He called the result a “square deal,” and the phrase became the name of his domestic program.4 The Elkins Act of 1903 banned railroad rebates, and the Hepburn Act of 1906, passed after he took his case directly to the public on a speaking tour, let the Interstate Commerce Commission regulate railroad rates.4,9 After reading Upton Sinclair’s The Jungle, he pushed for the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906.4

Conservation. Roosevelt was, in the Miller Center’s words, the nation’s first conservationist president. He wanted the country to move from exploiting its natural resources to managing them carefully.4 With Gifford Pinchot he built up the Forest Service. He signed the Newlands Reclamation Act of 1902 for western irrigation and the Antiquities Act of 1906, which let presidents declare national monuments without Congress.4,12 He created 150 national forests, 51 bird reserves, five national parks including Mesa Verde, and eighteen national monuments, among them Devil’s Tower and the Grand Canyon, which he protected as a monument when Congress resisted making it a park.12,4 The sources give different totals for the land he protected. The Miller Center’s overview says nearly 200 million acres, its domestic-affairs essay says the national forests grew from 42 million to 172 million acres, and the National Park Service says about 230 million acres of public land.1,4,12

The bully pulpit. Roosevelt was the first president to use the press systematically to appeal to the public over the heads of Congress and party bosses. He used the office as a “bully pulpit” to shape public opinion.4,1 He also named the investigative reporters of the day “muckrakers,” in a 1906 speech.4,9 In 1904 he won the presidency in his own right, defeating Alton Parker 336 electoral votes to 140. He was the first president who had inherited the office to win election to it. He then promised not to seek another term.3,1

A world power. Roosevelt’s maxim was “speak softly and carry a big stick.”5 He made the Navy one of the largest in the world and in 1907 sent sixteen battleships, the “Great White Fleet,” on a fourteen-month voyage around the globe.5 When Colombia balked at an American canal across Panama, then part of Colombia, the United States backed a Panamanian revolution with money and a naval blockade. The 1903 Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty gave it perpetual control of the canal zone.5 The canal opened in 1914.5 In 1906 Roosevelt visited the works, the first president to leave the country while in office.5 As the Russo-Japanese War dragged on, he brought the two sides to peace talks at Portsmouth, New Hampshire, in 1905, and he won the Nobel Peace Prize for it, the first American president to do so. He also helped settle a crisis between France and Germany over Morocco at Algeciras in 1906.5

Beliefs and ideas

Roosevelt believed that the president could use any power not specifically denied him by the Constitution or the laws.1,8 He was a reformer but not a radical. He saw big business as a natural stage of economic development and often argued that reform was needed to head off socialism.4 He distinguished between “good” and “bad” trusts and preferred to regulate corporations rather than break them up.9,3 His 1912 program, which he called the “New Nationalism,” cast the federal government as a “trustee” for the people, supervising the economy in the public interest.3 Abroad he believed that American power and values would benefit the world. He also spoke of a “manly duty” to police the Caribbean and spread what he called the benefits of Anglo-Saxon civilization.5,10

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Roosevelt’s record on race was, in the Miller Center’s words, “a mixed bag.”4 He invited Booker T. Washington to dine at the White House in October 1901 and refused to apologize when the Southern press attacked him, but he did little to stop Southern states from taking the vote away from Black citizens, and he believed Black Americans as a race were inferior to whites.4 In 1906, after a shooting in Brownsville, Texas, was blamed on Black soldiers despite conflicting accounts and no physical evidence, he ordered the dishonorable discharge of three companies, 160 men, without a trial.4

His foreign policy also drew criticism. In December 1904 he announced what became the Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine. Where Monroe had warned Europe out of the Americas, Roosevelt claimed for the United States the right to intervene in Latin American countries guilty of “chronic wrongdoing.”11,10 The policy, the Miller Center notes, created much resentment in Latin America.5 The sources describe his first use of it in the Dominican Republic differently. The Miller Center says an American collector took over its customs houses, while the American Yawp says Roosevelt sent the Marines to occupy the country in 1905 and imposed financial supervision.5,10 Historians have also questioned his reputation as a “trust buster.” The Yawp calls it “wildly exaggerated” and notes that his successor, William Howard Taft, brought more than twice as many breakups in four years as Roosevelt did in seven.9

Legacy

Roosevelt chose Taft as his successor in 1908 but soon fell out with him over conservation and appointments.3 In 1912 he won a series of Republican primaries that put him ahead in the race for delegates, but Taft’s supporters controlled the convention and renominated the president. Roosevelt and his followers formed the Progressive Party. Saying he felt “as strong as a Bull Moose,” he campaigned for a minimum wage for women, an eight-hour day, social insurance, and the direct election of senators.3 Near the end of the campaign he was shot and insisted on finishing his speech, the bullet slowed by the thick manuscript in his coat.3 He finished second with 27.4 percent of the vote, ahead of Taft but behind the Democrat Woodrow Wilson. His new party became one of the most significant third parties in American history.1,3

After the 1912 defeat Roosevelt explored the River of Doubt in the Brazilian Amazon with his son Kermit and came back weakened by malaria and infection.6 When World War I began, he argued loudly for military preparedness and attacked Wilson’s neutrality. After the United States entered the war, the War Department turned down his offer to raise a volunteer division. All four of his sons served, and the death of the youngest, Quentin, a pilot shot down over Germany, devastated him.6,1 Roosevelt died in his sleep at Sagamore Hill on January 6, 1919.6

The Miller Center sees Roosevelt’s 1912 platform as a forerunner of nearly every later liberal program, from his cousin Franklin’s New Deal to Lyndon Johnson’s Great Society.8 He was the first president to give his domestic program a name, a practice later presidents followed.4 A national park in the North Dakota Badlands, where his interest in conservation began, bears his name.12
