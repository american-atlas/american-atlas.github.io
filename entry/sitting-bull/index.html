Sitting Bull

Hunkpapa Lakota leader who refused the reservation

c. 1831–1890

Sitting Bull (Tatanka-Iyotanka) was a Hunkpapa Lakota war chief and holy man who led the Lakota who refused to accept life on a reservation.1 He rejected the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie and kept his people hunting buffalo on the open plains. In June 1876 his vision of soldiers “falling into camp” came before the Lakota and Cheyenne victory over George Armstrong Custer at the Little Bighorn.1,2 Pursued by the army, he led his followers into Canada and surrendered in 1881.1 He toured briefly with Buffalo Bill’s Wild West and lived at Standing Rock. In December 1890, amid fears of the Ghost Dance, he was killed by Lakota reservation police sent to arrest him.1,5

Why it matters

Sitting Bull became the best-known symbol of Native resistance to American conquest of the Great Plains. His refusal to sign away Lakota land or way of life, and the victory at the Little Bighorn associated with his name, made him famous among white Americans as well as his own people.1,3

Early life and context

Sitting Bull was born into a prominent Hunkpapa Lakota family sometime between 1831 and 1837, near where the Grand River meets the Missouri in present-day South Dakota. His father and two of his uncles were chiefs.1 He killed his first buffalo at ten and counted his first coup, an honor earned in immediate proximity to the enemy, at fourteen. After that he took his father’s name, Tatanka-Iyotanka, Sitting Bull.1 The National Park Service writes that he already showed the four cardinal Lakota virtues of bravery, fortitude, generosity, and wisdom.1 He joined the Kit Fox society and the elite Midnight Strong Heart society of warriors, became one of its two Sash Wearers, and then its leader.1

Sitting Bull was also a holy man. Through the Sun Dance and vision quests he sought guidance from Wakan-Tanka, and many Lakota believed his visions came true.1 In the 1850s, as the Lakota argued over how to deal with the expanding United States, he spoke for those who wanted to avoid trade and diplomacy with whites altogether. “I have seen nothing that the white man has,” he said, “which is as good as our right to roam and live on the open plains as we choose.”1 He stayed away from white settlements and forts almost entirely until the 1880s.1

Major actions and achievements

The non-treaty Lakota. In 1866 the Lakota leader Red Cloud waged a successful war against forts along the Bozeman Trail, and in 1868 he and other leaders signed the Treaty of Fort Laramie. The treaty created the Great Sioux Reservation in the western half of present-day South Dakota, including the Black Hills, which are sacred to the Lakota.1,4 Sitting Bull refused to take part and held that those who signed spoke only for themselves. The Lakota split between those who followed Red Cloud and Spotted Tail onto the reservation and the “non-treaty” bands who followed Sitting Bull and kept living as before.1,2 (The treaty’s list of signatures, as transcribed by the National Archives, includes a mark for a “Sitting Bull,” while the National Park Service states that Sitting Bull refused to join the negotiations.)4,1

The road to war. In 1874 an army expedition under Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer found gold in the Black Hills, and miners poured onto the reservation in violation of the treaty.2,4,3 The Lakota refused to sell the Black Hills. The government then ordered all Lakota onto the reservation by January 31, 1876, or be treated as “hostiles.”2,1 Sitting Bull ignored the deadline and sent word to the reservation Lakota that while they lived on scant rations, his people were feasting on buffalo.1 In mid-June 1876, at a Sun Dance, he gave fifty pieces of flesh from each arm and went without water for two days and nights. He then saw a vision of soldiers falling upside down into the Lakota camp, “like grasshoppers.” A voice told him, “I give these to you because they have no ears,” which the Lakota understood as a promise of victory.1,2

The Little Bighorn. On June 25, 1876, Custer’s 7th Cavalry attacked the great camp of Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho along the Little Bighorn River, which the Lakota called the Greasy Grass.2,3 The National Park Service estimates the village at about 7,000 people on its Sitting Bull page and about 8,000 on its battle page, with 1,500 to 1,800 warriors.1,2 Sitting Bull, past fighting age, did not fight himself but sent his nephews White Bull and One Bull into battle with his own protective medicine.1 The warriors drove Major Marcus Reno’s battalion back across the river and destroyed the five companies under Custer’s direct command.2,1 The Park Service estimates about 80 Lakota and Cheyenne deaths, and the American Yawp says Custer and 268 of his men were killed.1,3 The victory made Sitting Bull internationally famous as the “architect of Custer’s defeat,” whether or not that was accurate.1

Beliefs and ideas

Sitting Bull believed the Lakota should keep their freedom to hunt and live on the open plains, and that no treaty signed by others could bind those who did not consent to it.1 His authority rested as much on spiritual power as on military skill. He sought visions through the Sun Dance and vision quest, and his followers saw his prophecies fulfilled.1

Controversies, limits, and criticism

After the Little Bighorn, Americans demanded revenge, and the army under Generals George Crook and Nelson Miles pursued the non-treaty bands for a year and a half.3,1 The Black Hills were taken by the government without compensation in 1877. The National Archives notes that ownership of the Black Hills is still the subject of a legal dispute between the United States and the Sioux.2,4 In the fall of 1877 Sitting Bull led his followers into Canada, which the Lakota called the land of the White Grandmother. Buffalo were scarce there, and sickness and harsh winters wore his people down. One by one his followers drifted back to the reservations, and on July 20, 1881, he surrendered at Fort Buford in Dakota Territory.1,3 By then the buffalo herds had been destroyed: the American Yawp says the bison population fell from over ten million at midcentury to only a few hundred by the early 1880s.3

On the Standing Rock Reservation, Indian agents refused to recognize chiefs. Many Lakota, including Sitting Bull’s protégé Gall, took up farming.1 In 1885 Sitting Bull was allowed to tour with Buffalo Bill Cody’s Wild West show, where he was the star attraction and became Cody’s friend, but after one season the Standing Rock agent, James McLaughlin, refused to let him go again.1,3 The two men clashed for years.1

Legacy

In 1890 the Ghost Dance, a religious movement begun by the prophet Wovoka, spread to the Lakota. It promised that if Native people lived justly and danced, their ancestors would return, the buffalo would come back, and whites would vanish from the West.3,1 Agents and the army feared an uprising. McLaughlin believed Sitting Bull was involved, and at six in the morning on December 15, 1890, more than forty Lakota Indian police broke into his cabin to take him in for questioning. As they led him outside, a crowd gathered and shouting turned to gunfire. Sitting Bull was killed by a shot to the chest.1,5 Two weeks later the 7th Cavalry, Custer’s old regiment, surrounded Spotted Elk’s (Big Foot’s) band at Wounded Knee Creek and, after a shot was fired, massacred them. The American Yawp estimates that between 150 and 300 Lakota men, women, and children were killed. HISTORY puts the toll at almost 150, nearly half of them women and children, and notes that some historians put it twice as high.3,5 The American Yawp calls Wounded Knee the end of sustained armed Native resistance on the Plains.3

Sitting Bull is remembered as a defender of Lakota land and freedom who refused to give up his people’s way of life. The Little Bighorn Battlefield National Monument now presents the battle as a clash between the buffalo-and-horse culture of the northern Plains and the industrial culture of the United States.2,1
