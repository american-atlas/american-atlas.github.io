Dred Scott v. Sandford

The Supreme Court denies Black citizenship and opens the territories to slavery, 1857

1857 · Washington, D.C.

Dred Scott, an enslaved man, and his wife, Harriet, sued for their freedom in 1846 because their enslaver had held them for years at army posts in free territory.1 After an eleven-year legal struggle, the Supreme Court ruled against them on March 6, 1857.1 Chief Justice Roger B. Taney declared that people of African descent could not be citizens of the United States, and that Congress had no power to ban slavery from the federal territories, which meant the Missouri Compromise had been unconstitutional.1,2 Meant to settle the slavery question, the decision inflamed it and moved the nation closer to civil war.1,4 The National Archives notes that many legal scholars consider it the worst decision the Court has ever issued. The Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments overturned it.1

Why it matters

Dred Scott v. Sandford tried to settle the slavery question in the South’s favor by judicial decree. It denied Black Americans any claim to citizenship and declared the federal territories open to slavery. Its failure helped bring on the Civil War and the constitutional amendments that reversed it.1,4

Historical context

Dred Scott had been held by John Emerson, a U.S. Army surgeon. In the 1830s Emerson took him from Missouri to the army post at Rock Island in the free state of Illinois. From about 1836 to 1838 he held Scott at Fort Snelling, on the west bank of the Mississippi north of 36°30′, where the Missouri Compromise had banned slavery. Harriet was also held there.2,1 In 1846 the Scotts sued for their freedom in the St. Louis Circuit Court. They argued that residence in free territory had made them free, and as the National Archives notes, courts had ruled that way in the past.1

What began as a private lawsuit became an eleven-year struggle. Scott lost in the Missouri courts and then filed a new suit in federal court, which eventually reached the Supreme Court.1 Along the way slavery became the most explosive issue in American politics. The Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854, which critics exposed as a measure to overturn the Missouri Compromise and open western lands to slavery, had inflamed the question, and by the time the justices heard the case it carried enormous political stakes.1,4 Before his inauguration, President-elect James Buchanan learned from members of the Court how they were likely to rule. In his inaugural address on March 4, 1857, he said that the Court was about to decide the question and urged “all good citizens” to accept its ruling.3

The legal question

Could a Black man descended from enslaved Africans be a citizen of the United States entitled to sue in federal court? And did Scott’s residence in a free state and a free territory make him free? That raised the further question of whether Congress had the power to prohibit slavery in the territories.1,2

The ruling

By 7 to 2, the Court ruled against Scott. People of African descent, whether enslaved or free, were not and could not be citizens of the United States, so Scott could not sue in federal court. The Missouri Compromise’s ban on slavery north of 36°30′ was unconstitutional, so Scott had not become free by living at Fort Snelling.2,1

The Court’s reasoning

Chief Justice Taney read the majority opinion on March 6, 1857.1 He held that people of African descent “are not included, and were not intended to be included, under the word ‘citizens’ in the Constitution.”2 At the founding, he wrote, they “had for more than a century before been regarded as beings of an inferior order … and so far inferior, that they had no rights which the white man was bound to respect.”2 The words of the Declaration of Independence, he argued, could not have included “the enslaved African race,” or its signers would have been “utterly and flagrantly inconsistent with the principles they asserted.”2

Taney then went further. Treating enslaved people as property protected by the Constitution, he concluded that the act of Congress prohibiting slavery “north of the line therein mentioned, is not warranted by the Constitution, and is therefore void.” Neither Scott nor his family had been made free by being taken there.2 Justices John McLean and Benjamin Curtis dissented.2

Consequences

The decision was meant to end the dispute over slavery in the territories. Instead, as the American Yawp puts it, it “seemed to settle the sectional crisis by making slavery fully national, but in reality it just exacerbated sectional tensions further.”4 Southerners cited it to claim the right to take enslaved people into every territory, while Republicans recognized no such right.3 Abraham Lincoln answered with his “House Divided” speech in June 1858: “A house divided against itself cannot stand.”5 In his debates with Lincoln, Stephen Douglas argued that local communities could still keep slavery out by refusing to pass laws to protect it.5 The National Archives concludes that the ruling “moved the nation a step closer to the Civil War.”1 For Black Americans, the National Archives notes, the ruling meant that they could expect no protection from the federal government or the courts.1

Later interpretation

How later courts, scholars, and the public have judged the case. This is not part of the ruling itself.

The Civil War and Reconstruction undid the decision. The Thirteenth Amendment (1865) abolished slavery, and the Fourteenth (1868) declared that all persons born or naturalized in the United States are citizens.1,6,7 Dred Scott became a byword for judicial error. In 1896 Justice John Marshall Harlan warned in his Plessy v. Ferguson dissent that that decision would “prove to be quite as pernicious as the decision made by this tribunal in the Dred Scott Case.”8 The sources differ slightly on the scope of Taney’s ruling. The National Archives summarizes it as denying citizenship to enslaved people, while the opinion itself and the American Yawp make clear that it covered all Black Americans, whether enslaved or free.1,2,4
