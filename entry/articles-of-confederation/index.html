The Articles of Confederation

The first constitution of the United States, 1781–1789

November 15, 1777 – March 1, 1781

The Articles of Confederation were the first constitution of the United States.1 Drafted mainly by John Dickinson and adopted by the Continental Congress on November 15, 1777, they created “a firm league of friendship” among thirteen states that each kept their “sovereignty, freedom and independence.”1,2 They took effect only on March 1, 1781, when Maryland ratified.1 Congress had one house, and each state one vote.2 It could make war and treaties but could not tax, regulate commerce, or establish federal courts, weaknesses that, after the war, led James Madison, George Washington, and others to fear the union would collapse and to replace the Articles with the Constitution in 1787.1,3

Why it matters

The Articles carried the United States through the end of the Revolution and produced the Northwest Ordinance, but their weaknesses convinced many leaders that the union needed a stronger government. Their failures shaped nearly every major feature of the Constitution that replaced them, from the power to tax to the method of amendment.1,3,4

Historical circumstances

After Richard Henry Lee proposed independence in June 1776, the Second Continental Congress appointed three committees on June 11. One, with a member from each colony, was to decide what form a union should take; John Dickinson of Delaware was its principal writer.1 His draft named the confederation “the United States of America.”1 Americans had just rebelled against a distant government that taxed them without consent, and the plan reflected that suspicion of central power.3 After long debate, Congress adopted the Articles on November 15, 1777.1 Ratification required all thirteen states, and quarrels over representation, voting, and the western lands some states claimed held it up for more than three years, until Maryland ratified on March 1, 1781.1 The American Yawp says Congress “ratified” the Articles in 1781; more precisely, Congress had adopted them in 1777, and 1781 was when the last state approved.3,1

Principal ideas

  • A league of states. “The said States hereby severally enter into a firm league of friendship with each other, for their common defense, the security of their liberties, and their mutual and general welfare.”2
  • State sovereignty. Each state kept “its sovereignty, freedom and independence, and every Power, Jurisdiction and right, which is not by this confederation expressly delegated to the United States.”1
  • One state, one vote. “In determining questions in the United States in Congress assembled, each State shall have one vote.”2
  • Supermajorities. War, treaties, coining or borrowing money, and other major acts required the assent of nine states.2
  • No power to tax. Congress could request money from the states but had no power to levy or collect taxes, regulate foreign or interstate commerce, or create a federal judiciary.3,1
  • Hard to change. No alteration could be made unless agreed to by Congress “and be afterwards confirmed by the legislatures of every State.”2
  • Cooperation among states. States had to give “full faith and credit” to each other’s court records and hand over fugitives from justice.1

A government without money

The National Archives describes a Congress that “could not tax and was generally impotent in setting commercial policy,” with a depleted treasury and paper money flooding the country and causing extraordinary inflation.1 Because the national government could not tax, the burden of repaying war debts fell on the states. The Yawp notes that this helped push Massachusetts to side with its bondholders against debt-ridden farmers.4

Crisis and replacement

Disputes among the states over territory, war pensions, taxes, and trade threatened to tear the country apart.1 When farmers rose in Shays’ Rebellion in 1786–87, the Confederation government could not help put it down; Massachusetts relied on a militia paid for by private Boston businessmen.5 In May 1787 the Constitutional Convention met in Philadelphia, officially to revise the Articles. By mid-June the delegates had decided to redesign the government entirely, and the new Constitution took effect in 1789.1,4
