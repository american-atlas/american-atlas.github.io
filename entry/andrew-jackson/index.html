Andrew Jackson

Seventh president, champion of the “common man,” and architect of Indian removal

1767–1845

Andrew Jackson was the dominant figure in American politics between Thomas Jefferson and Abraham Lincoln. He was the first president from the West and the first “self-made man” to reach the White House.1 Orphaned by the Revolution, he rose as a Tennessee lawyer, planter, and enslaver, and his victory at New Orleans in 1815 made him a national hero.2,9 As president from 1829 to 1837 he cast himself as the people’s champion against privilege, destroyed the Second Bank of the United States, faced down South Carolina’s nullification of federal law, and founded the Democratic Party.1,4 He also signed the Indian Removal Act, which drove the Native nations of the Southeast from their homelands and led to the Cherokee Trail of Tears.4,10

Why it matters

Jackson turned the presidency into a direct link between the president and the voters and built the first modern mass party, while his opponents organized as the Whigs. The Miller Center calls the national two-party system his legacy.7 His record also includes the forced removal of Native nations and the defense of slavery, and historians remain sharply divided over him.7,4,10

Early life and context

Andrew Jackson was born on March 15, 1767, in the Waxhaw settlement, a community of Scotch-Irish immigrants on the border between North and South Carolina. His exact birthplace is disputed, but he considered himself a South Carolinian.2 His father died before he was born.2 The Revolution wiped out the rest of his family. His oldest brother, Hugh, died in patriot service. Andrew and his brother Robert, too young to be regular soldiers, fought with irregulars and were captured in 1781. Robert died of smallpox soon after their release, and their mother died of illness while trying to help nephews held on a British prison ship.2 At fifteen Jackson was an orphan and a hardened veteran.2

He read law in North Carolina, was admitted to the bar in 1787, and in 1788 went west to Nashville as public prosecutor. There he built a legal practice, traded, and began acquiring land and enslaved people.2,9 In 1791 he began living with Rachel Donelson Robards, who was separated from her husband. They married formally in 1794 after her divorce. His supporters later claimed the couple believed she was already divorced in 1791, but the Miller Center finds this unlikely, and charges of bigamy followed Jackson into his presidential campaigns.2 He served as a delegate to Tennessee’s constitutional convention in 1795, as the state’s first congressman, briefly as a senator, and as a judge of the superior court.2 In 1804 he bought The Hermitage, a cotton plantation near Nashville worked by enslaved people.2,1

Jackson’s temper led him into many quarrels. In an 1806 duel he let Charles Dickinson, a crack shot, fire first, took a bullet in the chest, and then killed him. He carried the bullet for the rest of his life.2 In 1802 he had won election as major general of the Tennessee militia. When the War of 1812 came, his willingness to share his men’s hardships on a march home from Natchez earned him the nickname “Old Hickory.”2 After Red Stick Creeks killed more than four hundred people at Fort Mims, he led Tennessee militia and Native allies into the Creek country and crushed the main Creek force at Horseshoe Bend in March 1814. Treaties he later negotiated took millions of acres from the Creeks, Choctaws, Chickasaws, and Cherokees, including nations that had fought on his side.2

As a U.S. major general, Jackson defended New Orleans with regulars, militia, free Black soldiers, Native Americans, and pirates. On January 8, 1815, his men shattered a frontal British assault, inflicting more than two thousand casualties.2,9 The Miller Center counts American losses in the main attack at thirteen dead and fifty-eight wounded and missing, while the American Yawp gives barely three hundred American casualties for the campaign’s defense of the city.2,9 The peace treaty had already been signed at Ghent two weeks earlier, but the victory made Jackson the nation’s greatest hero since Washington.2 In 1818, pursuing Seminoles, he invaded Spanish Florida on his own broad reading of his orders, seized its forts, and executed two British subjects. The episode caused an international uproar but helped persuade Spain to cede Florida in 1819.2

Major actions and achievements

The road to the White House. In 1824 Jackson led a four-man field with 43 percent of the popular vote and 99 electoral votes, short of a majority. When the House chose John Quincy Adams and Adams named Henry Clay secretary of state, Jackson denounced a “corrupt bargain.”3 In 1828 he swept Adams aside with 56 percent of the vote and 178 electoral votes to 83, after a campaign full of attacks on his marriage and violent past. Rachel died on December 22, before his inauguration.3 In 1832 he defeated Clay with 55 percent and 219 electoral votes to 49. That campaign also introduced national nominating conventions.3

The people’s tribune. Jackson saw himself as the people’s defender against special interests, and he took his case directly to the voters.7 He replaced officeholders through “rotation in office,” which critics called the “spoils system,” after a supporter’s phrase “to the victor belong the spoils.”4,1 He vetoed road and canal bills, beginning with the Maysville Road in 1830, and used the veto more than all his predecessors combined.1,7 He relied on informal advisers, the “Kitchen Cabinet,” and remade his official cabinet in 1831 after a bitter social feud over Peggy Eaton, the wife of his secretary of war, which also deepened his break with Vice President John C. Calhoun.4,7 He was proud of paying off the national debt.7

Nullification. In 1832 South Carolina declared the federal tariff null and void within its borders. Jackson, though he favored lower tariffs, issued a proclamation calling the Union indivisible and warning, “Disunion by armed force is treason.”1,4 At a dinner in 1830 he had already toasted, with Calhoun looking on, “Our federal Union: It must be preserved.”4 Early in 1833 Congress passed a compromise tariff together with a “force bill” authorizing him to enforce the revenue laws, and South Carolina rescinded its nullification of the tariff.4

The Bank War. Jackson, like Jefferson, believed the Second Bank of the United States was dangerous and unconstitutional.1 When its supporters pushed an early recharter through Congress in 1832, he vetoed it on July 10. His veto message declared that “the rich and powerful too often bend the acts of government to their selfish purposes.”4,11 In 1833 he moved federal deposits from the Bank to state banks, firing a Treasury secretary who refused to carry out the order. The Senate censured him in March 1834, and his allies had the censure expunged in 1837.4 His enemies, calling him “King Andrew the First,” organized as the Whig Party.9,7

Abroad. Foreign affairs mattered less. Jackson pressed France to pay old claims for damage to American shipping after a dispute that nearly led to war, and he tried clumsily to buy Texas from Mexico. The Miller Center argues that those efforts sowed distrust that helped bring on war with Mexico a decade later.8

Beliefs and ideas

Jackson believed that republican government should be simple, frugal, and close to the people, and that government favors bred inequality. Laissez-faire, in his view, best protected economic equality and political liberty.7 He held the Union to be perpetual and denounced nullification and secession.7 His democracy, however, was for white men. The Jacksonian era opened voting to many poor white men while Black men lost voting rights.9 Jackson treated the controversy over slavery as a threat to his party and to sectional peace. In 1835 he called for federal suppression of the abolitionists’ “incendiary publications” mailed to the South.4

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Indian removal. Jackson had long favored moving the eastern nations west of the Mississippi. As president he maintained that the federal government had no right to defend the Cherokees against Georgia’s encroachments.4 On May 28, 1830, he signed the Indian Removal Act, the only major law passed at his request during his presidency, authorizing removal treaties with nations east of the Mississippi.10,7 He described removal as a “benevolent policy” and asked, “What good man would prefer a country covered with forests and ranged by a few thousand savages to our extensive Republic, studded with cities, towns, and prosperous farms?”10 The Chickasaws and Choctaws submitted, the Creeks under duress, and a long war with the Seminoles began in 1835.4 The Supreme Court upheld Cherokee sovereignty against Georgia in Worcester v. Georgia (1832), but Jackson refused to protect the Cherokees’ treaty rights.1,4,12 In 1835 his government signed the Treaty of New Echota with a minority faction of the Cherokees.4,12 In 1838, under his successor Martin Van Buren, soldiers forced the remaining Cherokees west on what became known as the Trail of Tears. The National Archives says about 4,000 of some 16,000 Cherokees died; the American Yawp says that of 16,000 who set out, only 10,000 completed the journey.10,12

Slavery and violence. From his early years in Nashville Jackson bought and held enslaved people, and his cotton plantation, The Hermitage, ran on their labor.2,9,1 His duels and brawls marked him as a violent man, and his 1818 invasion of Florida raised charges that he defied civilian authority.2,3

Economic consequences. Jackson’s war on the Bank, his 1836 Specie Circular requiring payment in coin for public lands, and a wave of speculation were followed by the Panic of 1837, which broke just as he left office and deepened into a long depression.4,1 The American Yawp concludes that the Bank War worsened rather than solved the country’s economic problems.9

Legacy

Jackson retired to The Hermitage in 1837 but kept advising Van Buren. In 1844 he helped replace Van Buren with James K. Polk as the Democratic nominee because Van Buren opposed annexing Texas.5 He died at The Hermitage on June 8, 1845, and was buried in the garden beside Rachel.5 He and Rachel had no children of their own. They adopted Rachel’s nephew as Andrew Jackson Jr., and they raised Lyncoya, a Native American boy orphaned in 1813, who died in 1828.6

Jackson’s reputation has always divided Americans. To admirers he is a symbol of American achievement and democracy. To detractors, the Miller Center writes, he is “an incipient tyrant, the closest we have yet come to an American Caesar.”7 Today his presidency is remembered as much for the Trail of Tears as for the expansion of popular democracy.9,10
