Barack Obama

Forty-fourth president and the first African American president

born 1961

Barack Obama was inaugurated on January 20, 2009, as the forty-fourth president and the first African American to hold the office.1 The son of a white mother from Kansas and a Black Kenyan father, he grew up in Hawaii and Indonesia, worked as a community organizer in Chicago, and led the Harvard Law Review before entering politics.2 He took office in a deep recession, won an $800 billion stimulus, and signed the Affordable Care Act, which sharply reduced the number of uninsured Americans.1,4 Abroad he withdrew most American troops from Iraq, ordered the raid that killed Osama bin Laden, and pursued agreements on Iran’s nuclear program and climate change.5 Republican control of Congress after 2010 blocked much of his later agenda.1

Why it matters

Obama’s election was a landmark in the long history of race in American politics.1,8 His presidency also pulled the economy back from the edge of a second depression and changed the American health-care system. Several of his achievements, especially those resting on executive action, were later reversed or challenged.7,4

Early life and context

Barack Hussein Obama II was born in Hawaii on August 4, 1961. His parents, who had met as students at the University of Hawaii, were Ann Dunham, a white American from Kansas, and Barack Obama Sr., a Black Kenyan studying in the United States.2 His father left the family when Obama was two and later returned to Kenya. After his parents divorced, his mother married an Indonesian student, Lolo Soetoro, and from age six to ten Obama lived in Indonesia, attending Catholic and Muslim schools.2 “I was raised as an Indonesian child and a Hawaiian child and as a black child and as a white child,” he later said.2 He returned to Hawaii to live with his grandparents and attend the Punahou School. His mother, who admired the civil rights movement, taught him that being Black was “a great inheritance.”2

Obama went to Occidental College in Los Angeles and then to Columbia University in New York, graduating in political science in 1983.2 In Chicago he worked as a community organizer on the largely poor and Black South Side, organizing residents of the Altgeld Gardens public housing project.2 Concluding that “I just can’t get things done here without a law degree,” he entered Harvard Law School in 1988. He was elected president of the Harvard Law Review for 1990–91, the first African American to hold the post.2 The attention led to his memoir Dreams from My Father (1995).2 At a Chicago law firm he met Michelle Robinson, a South Side native and lawyer; they married in 1992 and had two daughters, Malia and Sasha.2,6

Back in Chicago, Obama directed a voter registration drive, practiced civil rights law, and taught at the University of Chicago Law School.2 He won a seat in the Illinois state senate in 1996, after challenging his predecessor’s nominating petitions, and eventually passed nearly 300 bills there.2 He lost a 2000 primary race for Congress by about 30 points.2 In October 2002 he spoke against the coming war in Iraq: “I don’t oppose all wars. … What I am opposed to is a dumb war.”2 In 2004 his keynote address at the Democratic National Convention, with its theme of “the audacity of hope,” made him a national figure, and he won his U.S. Senate race with 70 percent of the vote, the largest margin in Illinois history.2

Major actions and achievements

The 2008 election. Obama announced his candidacy in February 2007. Relying heavily on the Internet and a vast grassroots network, he won the Iowa caucuses and then defeated Hillary Clinton in a long contest for the Democratic nomination.3,1 When videos of sermons by his former pastor, Jeremiah Wright, threatened his campaign, he answered with a speech on race in Philadelphia in March 2008.3 He became the first presidential candidate to decline public funds, which allowed him to raise nearly three-quarters of a billion dollars.8 The financial collapse of September 2008 turned the race. On November 4 he defeated John McCain, 53 percent to 46 percent, and 365 electoral votes to 173.3 “Change has come to America,” he told supporters in Chicago that night.9

Rescuing the economy. When Obama took office, the economy was losing jobs and unemployment was nearing 10 percent.4 On February 17, 2009, he signed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, a stimulus of about $800 billion split among aid to the states, middle-class tax cuts, and infrastructure.4 He directed $60 billion to rescue General Motors and Chrysler. In 2010 he signed the Dodd-Frank Act to regulate Wall Street.4 By the time he left office, according to the Miller Center, the economy had added a net 11.3 million jobs, unemployment had fallen below 5 percent, and the annual deficit had dropped from more than $1.4 trillion to less than $600 billion.4

Health care. After a year of struggle and angry protests from the conservative Tea Party movement, Obama signed the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act on March 23, 2010.4,8 It survived two major Supreme Court challenges, in 2012 and 2015, although the Court made the expansion of Medicaid optional for the states.4 The share of adults without health insurance fell from 18 percent to 11 percent by the end of his presidency.4 He also appointed two Supreme Court justices, Sonia Sotomayor, the first Latina on the Court, and Elena Kagan.4

Foreign policy. Obama withdrew most American troops from Iraq. In Afghanistan he first increased the American force, by 17,000 troops in early 2009 according to the American Yawp, and then by a further 33,000 announced in December 2009, before beginning withdrawals.5,8 On May 2, 2011, a team of Navy SEALs killed Osama bin Laden at his hideout in Abbottabad, Pakistan.5 He joined NATO in backing the Libyan rebels against Muammar Qaddafi, and from 2014 he ordered air strikes against the so-called Islamic State in Iraq and Syria.5 In 2015, after two years of negotiations between Iran and the United States and five other nations, an agreement limited Iran’s nuclear program in exchange for lifting sanctions.5 He restored diplomatic relations with Cuba in December 2014 and visited Havana in 2016, and he helped bring about the 2015 Paris climate agreement.5

Beliefs and ideas

Obama described empathy as “at the heart of my moral code,” a value he learned from his mother, and he argued that government has an important role in opening opportunity to all.6 His politics combined liberal goals with a pragmatic, often cautious style. The Miller Center calls his foreign policy “pragmatic and piecemeal,” except for his long concern about climate change.5 He stressed national unity: “There’s not a black America and white America and Latino America and Asian America. There’s a United States of America.”2

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Obama’s presidency was marked by deep partisan division. After Republicans won the House in 2010, he spent much of his time in largely unsuccessful negotiations over taxes, budgets, and the deficit. After the 2012 massacre of schoolchildren in Newtown, Connecticut, he failed to win gun control legislation, and comprehensive immigration reform also failed.1,4 Once Republicans won the Senate in 2014, he turned increasingly to executive action, including the Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals program for undocumented immigrants brought to the country as children. Courts blocked a broader 2014 immigration order.1,4 The Affordable Care Act remained bitterly contested.4

His foreign policy drew criticism from both directions. He expanded drone strikes and special operations against suspected terrorists. The bombing campaign in Libya was followed by chaos, including the 2012 attack in Benghazi that killed four Americans.5 He called off planned strikes on Syria after the Assad government crossed his “red line” by using chemical weapons, and he later acknowledged that his administration had underestimated the rise of the Islamic State.5

Legacy

Obama left office on January 20, 2017, at fifty-five, with job approval of about 60 percent.7,1 A C-SPAN survey of historians that year ranked him twelfth among all presidents.7 The Miller Center suggests his greatest impact may lie in “the crises that did not happen”: the country avoided a second Great Depression and did not launch new large-scale wars.7 Some of his most notable achievements, among them the Affordable Care Act, the Paris agreement, and DACA, were soon overturned or under attack.7 After leaving office he and his family stayed in Washington while his younger daughter finished school, and he began work on the Obama Presidential Center on Chicago’s South Side.6,1
