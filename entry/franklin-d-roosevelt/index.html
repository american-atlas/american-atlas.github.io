Franklin D. Roosevelt

Thirty-second president, architect of the New Deal and wartime leader

1882–1945

Franklin D. Roosevelt led the United States through the two great crises of the twentieth century, the Great Depression and World War II, and was the only president elected four times.1,2 Born to wealth and paralyzed by polio at thirty-nine, he returned to politics and won the presidency in 1932 by promising “a new deal for the American people.”1,3,4 His New Deal transformed the federal government’s role in American life.7 As commander in chief after Pearl Harbor, he built the alliance with Britain, the Soviet Union, and China that defeated Germany and Japan.5,1 He died in April 1945, weeks before victory in Europe.6

Why it matters

Roosevelt changed what Americans expected from their government and their president. Under him the federal government took responsibility for the economic security of ordinary citizens, the Democratic Party became the nation’s majority party, and the United States emerged from World War II as a global power.7,4,5

Early life and context

Franklin Delano Roosevelt was born on January 30, 1882, at Hyde Park, New York, the only child of James Roosevelt, a wealthy landowner and businessman, and Sara Delano Roosevelt, who devoted herself to her son throughout her long life.1,3 He grew up on the family estate on the Hudson River, taught by tutors until he was fourteen, when he entered the Groton School. There he came to admire his distant cousin Theodore Roosevelt, the rising Republican star.3,1 He graduated from Harvard in three years, edited its student newspaper, the Crimson, and studied law at Columbia, passing the bar in 1907 without finishing a degree.1,3 In 1905 he married Anna Eleanor Roosevelt, a distant cousin and President Theodore Roosevelt’s niece.3,2 They had six children, five of whom survived infancy.1

In 1910 Roosevelt won a seat in the New York State Senate as a Democrat from Republican-dominated Dutchess County, and he became a determined opponent of the Tammany Hall machine that ran New York City’s Democratic Party.3,1 Having backed Woodrow Wilson in 1912, he became assistant secretary of the navy in 1913. He argued for a “big Navy” and, during the war in Europe, for improving American military readiness, and he was praised for helping prepare the country to enter the war.3 During those years he began an affair with Lucy Mercer, Eleanor’s social secretary. When Eleanor discovered it in 1918 and offered a divorce, he refused, in part because he knew a divorced man could not succeed in politics. They stayed married, but their marriage became more a political partnership than a romance, and Eleanor built an independent public life.3 In 1920 Roosevelt ran for vice president on the losing Democratic ticket with James Cox.1,3

In the summer of 1921, at his vacation home on Campobello Island in Canada, Roosevelt fell ill with polio. He never regained the use of his legs.1,3 Through years of painful exercise he learned to “walk” short distances with braces, canes, and aides, and he founded a center at Warm Springs, Georgia, for other polio patients.3,1 “I know that he had real fear when he was first taken ill,” Eleanor later said, “but he learned to surmount it. After that I never heard him say he was afraid of anything.”3 With Eleanor and his adviser Louis Howe keeping him connected to Democratic politics, he returned to public life. In 1928 he was narrowly elected governor of New York, winning by half a percentage point while Al Smith lost the presidency to Herbert Hoover.3,1 As the Depression deepened, Governor Roosevelt declared that “there is a duty on the part of government to do something about this,” and he created a state relief program for the unemployed.3

Major actions and achievements

Election and the New Deal. At the 1932 Democratic convention in Chicago, Roosevelt broke with tradition by flying there to accept the nomination in person. In part he went to show that polio had not weakened him, and he promised “a new deal for the American people.”4,1 He swept into office in a landslide, and Democrats won large majorities in both houses of Congress.4 When he took office on March 4, 1933, the banking system had collapsed and unemployment exceeded a quarter of the workforce.1,7 He closed the banks temporarily, explained his plans to the nation in radio “fireside chats,” and in his first hundred days won laws creating the Civilian Conservation Corps, the Agricultural Adjustment Administration, the Tennessee Valley Authority, and federal deposit insurance.1,7 In 1935 the Second New Deal added the Works Progress Administration, the Wagner Act protecting unions, and the Social Security Act.1,7 (See the New Deal entry for details.)

A new majority. In 1936 Roosevelt crushed the Republican Alf Landon, who carried only Maine and Vermont, declaring of the business and financial interests arrayed against him, “They are unanimous in their hate for me—and I welcome their hatred.”4 He had built the “New Deal Coalition” of Southern whites, urban Catholics and Jews, Black voters in Northern cities, union members, Midwestern farmers, and liberals, which would power the Democratic Party for thirty years.4 In 1940, with Europe at war, he broke George Washington’s two-term tradition and defeated Wendell Willkie with 55 percent of the vote. In 1944 he won a fourth term over Thomas Dewey, 432 electoral votes to 99.4

Toward war. For most of the 1930s Roosevelt’s internationalism was limited by the Depression and by isolationist feeling in Congress, which passed five Neutrality Acts between 1935 and 1939.5 In 1933 he established official relations with the Soviet Union and pursued a “Good Neighbor” policy toward Latin America.5 When war broke out in Europe in 1939, he declared the nation neutral but said he could not ask Americans to be “neutral in thought as well.”5 After France fell in 1940, he traded fifty old destroyers to Britain for bases. In March 1941 he won the Lend-Lease program, which made the United States the “arsenal of democracy.” After Germany invaded the Soviet Union that June, he extended Lend-Lease to the Soviets.1,5 In August 1941 he and Winston Churchill issued the Atlantic Charter, a statement of their war aims.5

Commander in chief. After Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, Roosevelt asked Congress for war, and Germany and Italy declared war on the United States four days later.1,5 On January 1, 1942, he formed the coalition of nations fighting the Axis that he called the “United Nations.”1,5 With Churchill and Stalin he planned the war at Tehran in 1943 and the peace at Yalta in early 1945. At Tehran he and Churchill promised Stalin the invasion of France that came on D-Day, June 6, 1944.5 He hoped the United Nations would outlast the war as an organization for world peace.5

Beliefs and ideas

Roosevelt believed in American capitalism but thought it had to be reformed to survive, and he approached problems as a pragmatist: try something, and if it fails, try something else.7 He believed government had a duty to help citizens in hard times. As governor he said relief was owed “not as a matter of charity, but as a matter of social duty.”8 In foreign policy he was an internationalist in the tradition of Theodore Roosevelt and Woodrow Wilson, convinced that the United States had a vital role to play in the world.5

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Roosevelt’s critics accused him of expanding federal power and the presidency beyond constitutional limits. His 1937 plan to add justices to the Supreme Court was denounced as “court-packing” and defeated, and his 1938 attempt to purge conservative Democrats in primaries largely failed.1,7 He did little to challenge segregation, fearing the loss of Southern Democratic votes.8 On February 19, 1942, he signed Executive Order 9066, under which some 122,000 people of Japanese descent, nearly 70,000 of them American citizens, were forcibly removed from the West Coast and confined in fenced, guarded camps in remote areas.9 (The American Yawp gives the number as more than 110,000.)10 In 1988 Congress acknowledged the injustice, apologized, and paid $20,000 to each survivor.9 His administration knew for much of the war that the Nazis were killing Jews, though officials probably could not conceive of the scale of the killing. Roosevelt’s policy was to win the war first, which would in turn stop the killing, and later critics argued that the United States could and should have done more to help Europe’s Jews.5

Roosevelt ran for a fourth term in 1944 although a medical examination early that year had found serious heart and circulatory problems.6 During the campaign his opponent discreetly raised the age issue.4

Legacy

By early 1944 a medical examination had revealed serious heart and circulatory problems, and the strain of the war, the 1944 campaign, and the Yalta Conference wore him down.6,1 On April 12, 1945, while sitting for a portrait at Warm Springs, Georgia, he collapsed and died of a cerebral hemorrhage at sixty-three. (The FDR Library describes it as a massive stroke; he died two and a half hours later without regaining consciousness.) Vice President Harry Truman took the oath of office that day.6 Churchill later compared the news to having “been struck a physical blow.”6 Hundreds of thousands of mourners lined the route of the funeral train from Georgia to Washington and on to Hyde Park, where he was buried on April 15.6,1 Victory in Europe came less than a month later.1

Roosevelt reshaped the presidency. His fireside chats created a direct bond with the public, and the 1939 reorganization law created the Executive Office of the President, changing the shape of the White House for good.7,11 In the Miller Center’s judgment, no president except Abraham Lincoln did more to change American society and politics.11
