The Triangle Shirtwaist Factory Fire

A disaster that changed American workplace safety, March 25, 1911

March 25, 1911 · Asch Building, Greenwich Village, New York City

Near closing time on Saturday, March 25, 1911, fire swept the Triangle Waist Company, a blouse factory on the top floors of the Asch Building in Manhattan. It killed 146 of its roughly 500 workers, most of them young Italian and Jewish immigrant women, some only fourteen.1 On the ninth floor workers found the stairway doors locked or blocked, the fire escape gave way, and the fire department’s ladders could not reach them. Dozens jumped to their deaths in front of crowds on the street.1,6,7 The owners were acquitted of manslaughter.3 But public anger led New York to create a Factory Investigating Commission, whose work rewrote the state’s labor laws and shaped the career of a future Secretary of Labor, Frances Perkins.6

Why it matters

The Triangle fire made the dangers of industrial work a public scandal. It convinced many Americans that government had to regulate the workplace, and it produced the most thorough investigation of working conditions that any state had yet attempted.7,6

Historical background

The Triangle Waist Company, a maker of women’s shirtwaists, filled the top three floors of the ten-story Asch Building near Washington Square.6 Cornell’s Kheel Center describes it as a typical sweated factory, marked by low wages, very long hours, and unsanitary and dangerous conditions.2 Its owners, Max Blanck and Isaac Harris, subcontracted much of the work to middlemen who hired the hands and paid whatever rates they chose. The owners said they never knew what the workers were paid, or even exactly how many people worked for them on a given day.2

Most of the workers were recent Italian and Jewish immigrants who had come with their families to find a better life. For people still learning a new language, complaining could mean losing a job they badly needed.1 Some had joined the International Ladies’ Garment Workers’ Union, but Triangle remained a non-union shop.1 In 1909 an incident at the factory set off a walkout of its 400 employees. The Women’s Trade Union League, an organization of middle-class reformers, helped the strikers picket against hired thugs and the police. At a mass meeting at Cooper Union, thousands of garment workers answered the young Clara Lemlich’s call for a general strike.2 The strikers demanded union recognition, higher wages, and better safety conditions. The American Yawp writes that the owners decided a workable fire escape and unlocked doors were too expensive, and they called in the police to break the strike.7 A 1910 agreement after a cloakmakers’ strike set up a grievance system in the garment trade, but many shops, Cornell notes, stayed in the hands of owners who ignored workers’ rights and safety.2

Causes and contributing factors

Fire inspection and fire precautions in New York were badly inadequate.1 On the day of the fire, waste bins full of cloth scraps from the day’s work fed the flames and let them spread fast.6 The owners had often locked exit doors in the past, saying that workers stole materials.1 At quitting time workers normally left through one exit on the Greene Street side, where every handbag was inspected.3 The building’s single fire escape led nowhere safe, and the elevator shaft had only one car.1,6 Firefighting equipment could not reach a fire so high up. The Labor Department’s history says the ladders reached only the seventh floor.6

What happened

Near closing time on March 25, 1911, fire broke out in one of the crowded, littered workrooms.6,1 Workers on the eighth and tenth floors escaped. Those on the ninth floor were trapped.6,7 They crowded against exits that were locked, doors blocked by machinery, and an elevator with a single car.6 Survivors said they could not open the ninth-floor doors to the Washington Place stairs, their only escape once the Greene Street stairs were engulfed.1,3

The sources describe the doors differently. Cornell’s Kheel Center says workers and many others believed the doors had been deliberately locked. The Labor Department calls the exits illegally locked. The American Yawp states that the doors had been chained shut to keep workers from taking breaks, and that the managers who held the keys saved themselves.1,6,7 Whether the owners knew the doors were locked became the question at their trial.3

The fire escape bent under the weight of the workers trying to use it, and the Yawp says it collapsed.1,7 Firefighters’ ladders were several stories too short, and the water from their hoses could not reach the top floors.1 Women crowded at the windows and, as the flames closed in, began to jump. Life nets held by firemen tore under the falling bodies.6,7 A United Press reporter wrote that he learned “a new sound, a more horrible sound than description can picture. It was the thud of a speeding, living body on a stone sidewalk.” About forty people died in the fall and more than a hundred inside the building.6 By the end, 146 were dead and 71 injured.1,7 The reporter Bill Shepherd, remembering the strike of the year before, wrote: “These dead bodies were the answer.”7

Immediate consequences

The city spent the following weeks identifying the dead and sorting their belongings. The ILGWU proposed an official day of mourning, and New Yorkers gathered in churches, synagogues, and the streets.4 On April 5, 1911, more than 100,000 people marched up Fifth Avenue in a funeral procession while another 400,000 watched.6 The reformer Martha Bruere watched it pass her window for six hours and wrote that “it is dawning on these thousands on thousands that such things do not have to be!”6 The labor organizer Rose Schneiderman, herself a former Triangle worker, said: “This is not the first time girls have been burned alive in this city… the life of men and women is so cheap and property is so sacred!”7

ILGWU Local 25 and the Women’s Trade Union League, the Workmen’s Circle, the Jewish Daily Forward, and the United Hebrew Trades formed a Joint Relief Committee. It paid pensions, cared for injured workers and children, and sent money to victims’ families in Russia and Italy. Working with the American Red Cross, it handed out about $30,000.5

Blanck and Harris insisted their building was fireproof and had just been approved by the Department of Buildings.3 On April 11, a grand jury indicted them on seven counts of second-degree manslaughter under section 80 of the Labor Code, which forbade locking doors during working hours.3 Worker after worker testified that the Washington Place door would not open. But the defense attorney Max Steuer raised enough doubt about whether the owners knew it was locked, and on December 27, 1911, the jury acquitted them.3 The Yawp writes that the jury took less than two hours.7 Grieving families cried out, “Where is justice?”3 Twenty-three civil suits followed. In March 1914 the owners settled them for $75 per life lost.3 Blanck kept running his business the same way. In 1913 he was charged with locking a door of his factory during working hours, and he was fined twenty dollars.3

Long-term significance

The deeper response came from Albany. A mass meeting at the Metropolitan Opera House, held days before the funeral march, created a Committee on Safety that lobbied the state for an investigation.6 The state senate majority leader Robert F. Wagner and Alfred E. Smith took up the cause, and a law of June 30, 1911, created the Factory Investigating Commission, with Wagner as chairman and Smith as vice-chairman. Samuel Gompers of the American Federation of Labor served on it.6 The commission could subpoena witnesses, and it looked into fire hazards, sanitation, occupational disease, and factory inspection. In 1911 and 1912 it held 59 public hearings, heard 472 witnesses, and inspected 3,385 workplaces, from bakeries to chemical plants.6

Between 1912 and 1914, 13 of the 17 bills it sent to the legislature became law. Together they made a new and much stricter factory code, covering fire safety, ventilation, sanitation, machine guards, and elevators. By the end, 20 laws in all came out of its work.6 New York also adopted workmen’s compensation.6 When business groups pushed “ripper” bills to weaken the new code, the commission’s supporters rallied, and the governor vetoed all but one of the bills that passed.6

Frances Perkins, a young social reformer, testified before the commission and led its members on factory visits. She said the commission turned the Triangle flames into “a torch that lighted up the industrial scene” and later called its work a “turning point” in American attitudes toward social responsibility.6 Perkins went on to serve as Secretary of Labor and carried its lessons into national policy.6 Wagner later served in the U.S. Senate.6 The Triangle fire is still remembered as a symbol of the extremes of industrialism, and its victims as martyrs of the labor movement.8
