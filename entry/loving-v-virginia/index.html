Loving v. Virginia

The Supreme Court strikes down bans on interracial marriage, 1967

1967 · Washington, D.C.; Caroline County, Virginia

Mildred Jeter, a woman of African American and Native descent, and Richard Loving, a white man, married in Washington, D.C., in 1958 and went home to Caroline County, Virginia. There they were arrested and convicted under the state’s ban on interracial marriage and ordered to leave Virginia for twenty-five years.1,2 In Loving v. Virginia (1967) a unanimous Supreme Court struck down the law, holding that it violated both the Equal Protection and Due Process Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment.2,3 “Under our Constitution, the freedom to marry, or not marry, a person of another race resides with the individual and cannot be infringed by the State,” Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote.2 The decision ended such bans in Virginia and the fifteen other states that still had them.2,1

Why it matters

Loving v. Virginia swept away the last legal bans on interracial marriage, which had deep roots in slavery, and showed how two people who simply wanted to live at home together could change constitutional law.1,2

Historical context

Virginia had regulated interracial marriage since 1691. In 1849 it declared marriages between whites and African Americans “absolutely void,” and in 1878 it made both partners liable to two to five years in the penitentiary.1 The laws existed because such marriages kept happening.1 In 1924, amid a eugenics movement that taught that races should not mix, the General Assembly passed the Act to Preserve Racial Integrity. It defined a white person as someone with “no trace whatsoever of any blood other than Caucasian,” with an exception for those who counted Pocahontas among their ancestors, and it barred white people from marrying anyone but white people.4,1 The Supreme Court had refused to hear earlier challenges. In 1883, in Pace v. Alabama, it held that a law that punished both races equally did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment.1

Richard Loving, born in 1933, was a white construction worker from Central Point in Caroline County. Mildred Jeter, born in 1939 in the same community, was of African American and Native ancestry, and later in life identified herself as Indian.1 They married in Washington, D.C., on June 2, 1958, and moved in with her parents.1 In July the county prosecutor obtained warrants. A few days later, in the early morning, the sheriff and two deputies broke into the couple’s bedroom. “They asked Richard who was that woman he was sleeping with,” Mildred Loving recalled. “I said, ‘I’m his wife,’ and the sheriff said, ‘Not here you’re not.’”1

They were indicted in October 1958. On January 6, 1959, Judge Leon M. Bazile sentenced each of them to a year in jail, suspended on condition that they leave Virginia and not return together for twenty-five years.1,2 The Supreme Court’s opinion says they pleaded guilty; Encyclopedia Virginia adds that they first pleaded not guilty and changed their pleas at the end of the arguments.2,1 The Lovings moved to Washington. Mildred came back alone to Central Point to give birth to their children.1 In 1963 she wrote to Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy, who referred her to the American Civil Liberties Union.1 “I wasn’t in anything concerning civil rights,” she said later. “We were trying to get back to Virginia. That was our goal, to get back home.”1

The legal question

Does a state law that forbids and punishes marriages between people solely because of their race violate the Equal Protection and Due Process Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment?2

The ruling

The ACLU lawyers Bernard Cohen and Philip Hirschkop asked Judge Bazile to set aside the conviction in November 1963. He refused on January 22, 1965, in an opinion declaring that “Almighty God created the races white, black, yellow, malay and red, and he placed them on separate continents… The fact that he separated the races shows that he did not intend for the races to mix.”1,2 Hirschkop later said the judge “couldn’t have done us a bigger favor.”1 On March 7, 1966, the Supreme Court of Appeals of Virginia upheld the law, though it found the order to leave the state unreasonable.1 The U.S. Supreme Court heard argument on April 10, 1967. Virginia argued that the law protected the state from the “sociological [and] psychological evils” of interracial marriage. Hirschkop called the statutes “slavery laws, pure and simple.”1 Cohen told the Court what Richard Loving had told him: “Mr. Cohen, tell the Court I love my wife and it is just unfair that I can’t live with her in Virginia.”1

On June 12, 1967, the Court ruled unanimously for the Lovings. Chief Justice Warren wrote that the statutes “cannot stand consistently with the Fourteenth Amendment.”2 The Court reversed the convictions on two separate grounds: equal protection and due process.2,3

The Court’s reasoning

Equal protection. Virginia argued that its law was not discriminatory because it punished the white and the Black partner equally. The Court rejected this “equal application” theory. Racial classifications, especially in criminal laws, demanded “the most rigid scrutiny.” The Court found “patently no legitimate overriding purpose independent of invidious racial discrimination.” The fact that Virginia banned only marriages involving white people showed that the law was designed “to maintain White Supremacy.”2 The Court also noted that the Virginia court’s earlier defense of the law, in Naim v. Naim (1955), spoke of preventing “a mongrel breed of citizens,” which Warren called “obviously an endorsement of the doctrine of White Supremacy.”2

Due process. The Court also held that marriage “is one of the ‘basic civil rights of man,’ fundamental to our very existence and survival.” To deny it “on so unsupportable a basis as the racial classifications embodied in these statutes” was “surely to deprive all the State’s citizens of liberty without due process of law.”2 The National Constitution Center describes the decision as an expansion of the Court’s reading of the Equal Protection Clause.3

Consequences

The ruling ended laws against interracial marriage in Virginia and the fifteen other states that still had them; Maryland had repealed its law while the case was pending.1,2 In August 1967 the Associated Press reported the first known interracial wedding in Virginia since the decision, in Norfolk.1 On the day of the ruling Mildred Loving told reporters, “I feel free now.” The couple, who had avoided publicity throughout, built a house in Central Point.1 Richard Loving was killed by a drunk driver in 1975. Mildred Loving died in 2008.1

Later interpretation

How later courts, scholars, and the public have judged the case. This is not part of the ruling itself.

Encyclopedia Virginia writes that the case laid the legal basis for a cultural redefinition of marriage, and that over time marriages between white and Black Americans became both more common and more accepted.1 The National Constitution Center notes that the holding expanded the Court’s reading of the Equal Protection Clause, and its description of marriage as a fundamental freedom protected by due process became an important precedent.3,2
