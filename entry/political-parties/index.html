Political Parties

How Americans organized to win elections, from Federalists and Republicans to today’s two-party system

1792

Political parties appear nowhere in the Constitution, and many founders hoped to avoid them.1 George Washington warned against “the baneful effects of the spirit of party.”2 Yet parties formed within a few years and have organized American politics ever since.3,4 Hamilton’s Federalists and Jefferson’s Democratic-Republicans gave way to Andrew Jackson’s Democrats and their Whig opponents. In the 1850s the antislavery Republican Party was born, and since then Democrats and Republicans have dominated a two-party system, even as their coalitions and ideas have changed dramatically.5,6,7,8

Why it matters

Parties are the machinery of American democracy: they turn public opinion into governing coalitions. Their realignments mark the great turning points in American political history, from 1800 to the Civil War, the New Deal and beyond.1,6,7

Origins

The founders distrusted parties. In Federalist No. 10 James Madison defined a faction as citizens “united and actuated by some common impulse of passion, or of interest, adversed to the rights of other citizens,” and argued that a large republic would control “the mischiefs of faction.”9 In his Farewell Address of 1796 Washington warned Americans “in the most solemn manner against the baneful effects of the spirit of party generally.”2

But divisions appeared inside Washington’s own administration. Alexander Hamilton wanted a strong national government that promoted commerce and industry; Thomas Jefferson wanted to restrict federal power and preserve an agricultural economy.1 From these quarrels grew the Federalists and the Republicans, or Democratic-Republicans. By the mid-1790s Jefferson was the informal leader of the nation’s first opposition party.1,10 The House historians record that the First and Second Congresses divided into pro- and anti-administration members, and that by the Fourth Congress the labels were Federalists and Democratic-Republicans.3

Powers and limits

Parties have no formal powers under the Constitution, but they do much of the work of democracy: they nominate candidates, organize voters, run campaigns and organize Congress.3,5 They also adapted the constitutional machinery. The Twelfth Amendment, passed after the election of 1800, was designed, the American Yawp notes, “to accommodate the way political parties worked.”1

How it changed over time

The first transfer of power. Jefferson’s victory in the election of 1800 brought the first peaceful transfer of power between parties. Republicans called it a “bloodless revolution,” partly because the Constitution “made no provision for political parties” and they thought they had rescued the country from an aristocratic takeover.1 During the War of 1812, New England Federalists met at the Hartford Convention in 1814 to curb the power of the Democratic-Republicans and even contemplated secession, a stance that, the Yawp notes, “posed the possibility of disaster” for the young country.4

Democrats and Whigs. Andrew Jackson’s supporters began calling themselves Democrats. Under Martin Van Buren they built “the first modern party in the United States,” with a centralized leadership and a consistent program at every level of government.5 Jackson’s enemies, mocking him as “King Andrew the First,” took the name Whigs from the patriots of the Revolution and formally organized in 1834; they held their first national convention in 1839 and won the presidency in 1840 with William Henry Harrison.5

The Republican Party. Slavery broke the Whigs apart. The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 set off protests across the North, and the antislavery movements that began that year “coalesced with the formation of a new political party,” which its organizers named the Republican Party, after the founders. It nominated John C. Frémont for president in 1856 and elected Abraham Lincoln in 1860.6

Realignments. Franklin D. Roosevelt’s New Deal built a Democratic coalition, in the Yawp’s words “the uneasy alliance of national liberals, urban laborers, farm workers, and southern whites.”7 The civil rights laws of the 1960s, passed under Democratic leadership, pushed white southerners toward the Republican Party, and inflation and slow wage growth eroded the coalition’s working-class base. Ronald Reagan won the presidency in 1980 with 51 percent of the popular vote.8

Party names that changed sides

American party names can confuse readers. Jefferson’s party called itself Republican, and historians often call it Democratic-Republican; it is the ancestor of today’s Democratic Party, not the Republican Party.1,5 The modern Republican Party, founded in 1854, chose its name to recall the founders.6 The parties’ regional bases also shifted. Southern whites were a pillar of the Democratic New Deal coalition, but the civil rights laws of the 1960s, passed under Democratic leadership, pushed them toward the Republicans.7,8

Third parties

The two-party system has always had challengers. In the 1850s the anti-immigrant Know-Nothing, or American, Party made impressive gains in 1854 and 1855 before the antislavery cause overtook it.6 Earlier, the Anti-Masons were among the groups that fed into the Whig coalition.5
