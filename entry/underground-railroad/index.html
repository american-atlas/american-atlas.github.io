The Underground Railroad

Escape from slavery, and the networks that helped freedom seekers reach liberty

The Underground Railroad was not a railroad and not underground. The National Park Service defines it as “the efforts of enslaved African Americans to gain their freedom by escaping bondage,” and the help others gave them.1 Those helpers included free and enslaved Black people, white abolitionists, American Indians and others.1 Freedom seekers fled to the North, Canada, Mexico, Spanish Florida, Indian territory, the West, the Caribbean and Europe, sometimes guided by “conductors” such as Harriet Tubman.1,2 After the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 made helping them more dangerous, the networks grew more organized, and the Underground Railroad became, in the Park Service’s words, a “widespread form of civil disobedience.”1

Key facts

What it was
Escape from slavery through flight, and help with that escape, from slavery’s beginning to the end of the Civil War
Destinations
The free states, Canada, Mexico, Spanish Florida, Indian territory, the West, the Caribbean and Europe
Famous conductor
Harriet Tubman, who led about 70 people to freedom in the 1850s
Philadelphia
The Black abolitionist William Still helped freedom seekers and recorded their stories
1850 law
Federal commissioners were paid $10 for ruling a captive enslaved, $5 for ruling a captive free
Preferred term
“Freedom seeker,” not “fugitive” or “runaway”

Why it matters

The Park Service calls the Underground Railroad “one of the earliest grass roots movements in the United States in which people united across racial, gender, religious, and class lines in hopes of promoting social change.”2 At its center were the freedom seekers themselves.

Goals

The goal was freedom. Enslaved people sought to escape bondage and reach places beyond the reach of recapture. Those who helped them, sometimes on the spur of the moment and sometimes as part of organized networks, aimed to get them there safely in defiance of the law.1 The Park Service stresses that “there is no Underground Railroad without freedom seekers,” who are at the center of the story.2

Strategies

  • Escape began at home. The Underground Railroad “started at the place of enslavement.” Freedom seekers followed rivers, canals, bays, the Atlantic coast, ferries, roads and trails, and many escapes began near ports, free territory or international borders.1
  • Ingenuity. They used “disguises, forgeries and other strategies” to slip past slave catchers who watched the expected routes for advertised rewards.1
  • Conductors and stations. A conductor escorted or directed freedom seekers between “stations,” or safe houses; a conductor did not need to belong to any organized network.2
  • The sea. The maritime industry spread information and offered jobs and passage; ties to whaling even made the Pacific coast a destination.1
  • War. During the Civil War many freedom seekers escaped to the lines of the Union army.1

Opposition

Slavery was legal, and so was hunting those who fled it. Enslavers advertised rewards, and slave catchers watched the routes.1 A fugitive slave clause in the Constitution and a federal law of 1793 already provided for the return of escapees, and in Prigg v. Pennsylvania (1842) the Supreme Court ruled that the federal law overrode a Pennsylvania law against carrying Black people out of the state to enslave them.4,2,3

The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, part of the Compromise of 1850, was far harsher. It created federal commissioners who decided cases without a jury trial, barred Northern authorities from interfering with captures, and required citizens to help in arrests when called upon. Commissioners were paid $10 if they ruled a captive enslaved and only $5 if they ruled the person free, which, the American Yawp writes, “fostered corruption and the enslavement of free Black northerners.”3,4

Achievements

Many reached freedom. Harriet Tubman escaped from Maryland to Philadelphia, learned about the networks from William Still in Philadelphia, and returned again and again; between 1850 and 1860 she brought about 70 people, including her parents, to freedom.5 “I never ran my train off the track and I never lost a passenger,” she said.1 William Still, the Yawp notes, “frequently risked his life” helping freedom seekers and preserved their letters and stories.3 After 1850 many fled all the way to Canada.3,5

The Underground Railroad also changed Northern opinion. The Yawp writes that no part of the Compromise of 1850 “proved more troubling to antislavery Americans than the Fugitive Slave Act,” and Northern resistance to it helped bring on the sectional crisis.3

Unresolved questions

Historians and the National Park Service now debate even the words used to tell the story. Terms such as “fugitive,” “runaway” and “escapee,” the Park Service writes, were “constructs of slave-holding society and patronizing abolitionists” that take away individual agency, so it prefers “freedom seeker.” But it also notes that “there is not universal consensus”: some historians keep “fugitive” to stress that people who freed themselves were breaking the law.2 Research continues, and new routes are still being discovered.1

How many trips did Tubman make?

Even a single source can count differently. The National Women’s History Museum says Tubman made eleven trips from Maryland to St. Catharines, Ontario, between 1850 and 1860, “19 in total” over the years, and that she brought about 70 people to freedom; the same biography later speaks of “the hundreds of enslaved that she freed over the course of her lifetime.”5 The museum also credits her with helping coordinate the 1863 Combahee River Raid in South Carolina, which freed about 750 people.5
