Anti-Federalists

The opponents of the Constitution whose objections produced the Bill of Rights

1787–1791

The Anti-Federalists were the Americans who opposed ratifying the Constitution in 1787 and 1788. They feared that the new national government would swallow the states and threaten liberty, and they rallied above all around its lack of a bill of rights.1,2 Leaders included George Mason and Patrick Henry of Virginia, and essayists such as “Brutus” in New York.1,3 They lost the fight over ratification, but their pressure led several states to ratify only with proposed amendments, and those proposals became the Bill of Rights in 1791.1,2

Why it matters

The Anti-Federalists lost the ratification fight but shaped the result. Their insistence on written protections for liberty gave the Constitution its Bill of Rights, and their warnings about centralized power began a debate over federalism that has never ended.1,2

Goals

Anti-Federalists did not agree on everything, but most shared a few aims. They wanted power kept close to the people in the states, and they distrusted a strong central government far away.1,3 Most of all, they wanted a written guarantee of individual rights. The Philadelphia convention had voted down George Mason’s proposal for a national bill of rights, and the American Yawp writes that this omission “became a rallying point for opponents of the document.”1

Strategies

Opponents argued their case in pamphlets and newspapers, in the same public debate that produced the Federalist Papers.1 Mason, one of three delegates present on the convention’s last day who refused to sign the Constitution, wrote a pamphlet that the National Archives says persuaded many Americans to oppose it.2 In New York a writer calling himself “Brutus” published essays beginning on October 18, 1787.3 In the state ratifying conventions Anti-Federalists either voted no or demanded amendments as the price of approval.1

Opposition

The Federalists, led by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay, answered in the Federalist Papers.1 They argued that a bill of rights was “not only redundant but dangerous,” in the Yawp’s summary, because listing some rights might seem to limit others; the government, they said, could only use the powers the Constitution gave it.1,2 At the Virginia convention in June 1788 Madison, Edmund Randolph and John Marshall faced Patrick Henry and George Mason.1

Achievements

  • Amendments attached to ratification. In Massachusetts, where the Anti-Federalists at first probably had the upper hand, the convention narrowly ratified the Constitution in early 1788 but proposed amendments for the first Congress to consider. Other states followed this pattern.1 The National Archives calls this the Massachusetts Compromise and says it “paved the way for the passage of the Bill of Rights.”2
  • The Bill of Rights. Madison, once the most vocal opponent of a bill of rights, introduced amendments in the First Congress on June 8, 1789. Ten of them were ratified by December 15, 1791.2 The Yawp notes that he had won election to the House only by promising his Virginia voters such a list.1

Unresolved questions

The Anti-Federalists’ deeper fear, that the national government would keep growing at the states’ expense, was not settled by the Bill of Rights. Brutus warned that the “necessary and proper” clause gave Congress a power so broad that it might be used “entirely to abolish the state legislatures.”3 Arguments about the reach of that clause and about the balance between the states and the nation continued long after the Anti-Federalists themselves had disappeared as a group.1,3

Brutus No. 1

The first Brutus essay, dated October 18, 1787, asked whether the thirteen states should be reduced to “one great republic.” Its author argued that the proposed government, though not “a perfect and entire consolidation” of the states, came so close that “it must, if executed, certainly and infallibly terminate in it.”3 The author signed only with the pen name Brutus.3

The close votes

After nearly a month of debate Virginia, the most populous state, ratified 89 to 79. Congress announced on July 2, 1788, that enough states had ratified for the Constitution to take effect, but New York ratified by just three votes, and Rhode Island by two, a full year after Washington took office.1

Accepting defeat

Once Washington was elected, his authority helped settle the matter. By 1793, the Yawp writes, “the term Anti-Federalist would be essentially meaningless,” though many former opponents went on to join the Democratic-Republicans who challenged Hamilton’s policies.1
