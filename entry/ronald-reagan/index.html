Ronald Reagan

Fortieth president and leader of the conservative revival

1911–2004

Ronald Reagan, a former radio announcer, film actor, and governor of California, was elected president in 1980 at a time of economic trouble and setbacks abroad.1 He cut income taxes, broke an air traffic controllers’ strike, and built up the military as deficits and the national debt soared.4,9 After denouncing the Soviet Union, he worked with Mikhail Gorbachev on the 1987 INF Treaty, the first Cold War agreement to reduce nuclear arsenals.5,1 Despite the Iran-Contra affair, he left office in 1989 highly popular, having reshaped American conservatism.1,8

Why it matters

Reagan made conservatism, with its calls for lower taxes, less regulation, a strong military, and firm anticommunism, the dominant force in the Republican Party and a powerful one in American politics.8,9 His partnership with Gorbachev helped bring the Cold War toward a peaceful end, while his economic record, with long growth, low inflation, and record deficits, is still debated.8

Early life and context

Ronald Wilson Reagan was born on February 6, 1911, in an apartment above a general store in Tampico, Illinois. He was the younger son of Jack Reagan, a shoe salesman, and Nelle Wilson Reagan.2 The family moved from town to town as Jack looked for work and settled in Dixon in 1920.2 Jack was warm and talkative but an alcoholic. Nelle, a devout member of the Disciples of Christ, taught her sons that alcoholism was a disease and spent much of her time visiting prisoners and hospital patients. Reagan felt he took after her.2 Jack, a Democrat, refused to let his sons see The Birth of a Nation because it glorified the Ku Klux Klan. During the Depression the New Deal’s Works Progress Administration employed both Jack and Ronald’s brother, Neil.2

“Dutch” Reagan was a lifeguard for six summers on the Rock River and, according to newspaper reports and later research, saved 77 people from drowning.2 At Eureka College in Illinois, from 1928 to 1932, he majored in economics with a C average but threw himself into football, drama, and student government. He washed dishes to pay his way.2,1 After graduation, with a quarter of Americans out of work, he found a job as a radio sportscaster in Davenport, Iowa, and then at WHO in Des Moines, where he became known for his re-creations of Chicago Cubs games.1,2 On a trip to the Cubs’ spring training in California in 1937, he took a screen test at Warner Brothers and won a $200-a-week contract.2

Over the next twenty years Reagan made about fifty films. The Miller Center gives the total as 52 in one essay and “more than 50” in another.1,2 He played the dying football star George Gipp in Knute Rockne—All American (1940), which earned him the nickname “the Gipper,” and his own favorite performance came in Kings Row (1942), whose line “Where’s the rest of me?” became the title of his 1965 autobiography.2 Nearsightedness kept him out of combat in World War II, and he spent most of the war narrating training films for the Army Air Corps’ First Motion Picture Unit.2 He married the actress Jane Wyman in 1940; they had a daughter, Maureen, adopted a son, Michael, and divorced in 1948. In 1952 he married the actress Nancy Davis, with whom he had two children, Patti and Ron.2,7

Reagan joined the Screen Actors Guild in 1937 and became its president in 1947.2 (The Miller Center says he served until 1954 and again from 1959; the American Yawp gives 1947 to 1952.)2,9 A liberal Democrat and admirer of Franklin Roosevelt, he fought Communist influence in Hollywood. He became a secret FBI informant in 1953 and, as SAG president, helped carry out the studios’ blacklist, though he also worked to clear actors he thought wrongly accused.2 From 1954 to 1962 he hosted General Electric Theater and toured GE’s 135 plants, giving talks that grew into a standard speech against high taxes and big government.2 Rising income and high taxes, battles with leftist union leaders, and his GE experiences moved him to the right. He campaigned for Richard Nixon in 1960 and changed his registration to Republican in 1962.2,1

Major actions and achievements

A Time for Choosing. On October 27, 1964, Reagan gave a nationally televised speech for the Republican presidential candidate Barry Goldwater. He warned that government was eroding individual freedom and that the country was too weak against the Soviet Union, and told viewers, “You and I have a rendezvous with destiny.” The speech raised $1 million and made him the new hero of the conservative movement.1,2,11

Governor of California. In 1966 Reagan defeated Governor Pat Brown by nearly a million votes, carrying 53 of the state’s 58 counties, and he was reelected in 1970.2 He proved more pragmatic than expected. To close an inherited deficit he agreed to a record $1 billion tax increase, and he worked with Democrats on welfare reform and property-tax relief.2 He took a hard line against campus unrest and, at the request of administrators, sent the National Guard to Berkeley in 1969 and Santa Barbara in 1970.2 He also accepted a Redwood National Park and protected the wild rivers of the north coast.2 After a half-hearted bid in 1968, he came within a narrow margin of taking the 1976 Republican nomination from President Gerald Ford.1,2

The 1980 victory. Running against Carter amid high inflation, unemployment, and the Iranian hostage crisis, Reagan asked voters whether they were better off than four years earlier.3,9 He won 51 percent of the vote to Carter’s 41 percent and 489 electoral votes to 49, and Republicans won control of the Senate for the first time since 1954.3 The hostages in Iran were released as he took the oath on January 20, 1981.3,9 In his inaugural address he declared, “In this present crisis, government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem.”10

Reaganomics. On March 30, 1981, Reagan was shot and seriously wounded by a would-be assassin. His good humor (“I hope you’re all Republicans,” he told his surgeons) brought a wave of public support.1 He used it to win a 25 percent cut in income-tax rates over three years, less than the 30 percent he had requested, along with modest spending cuts. His advisers predicted, on supply-side theory, that growth would make up the lost revenue.4 In 1981 he also fired more than 11,000 striking air traffic controllers, a blow that left organized labor reeling.4,9 A severe recession followed as the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker raised interest rates to break inflation. Unemployment reached 11 percent, and in January 1983 Reagan’s approval fell to 35 percent. Reagan stood by Volcker, inflation fell, and a long expansion began in 1983.4,1 He agreed to tax increases in 1982 and 1984, worked with Speaker Tip O’Neill on a 1983 compromise that kept Social Security solvent for a generation, and in his second term won the bipartisan Tax Reform Act of 1986.4 By the time he left office the top marginal tax rate had fallen from 70 to 28 percent.4,8

Reelection. In 1984, with the economy booming and his campaign promising “Morning Again in America,” Reagan defeated Walter Mondale, who had pledged to raise taxes, carrying every state except Minnesota and winning 525 electoral votes to 13.3 When Mondale raised the issue of his age, Reagan, then 73, joked in their second debate that he would not exploit “my opponent’s youth and inexperience.”3

Ending the Cold War’s arms race. In his first term Reagan pressed a huge military buildup, called the Soviet Union an “evil empire” in March 1983, and proposed the Strategic Defense Initiative, a space-based missile defense that critics called “Star Wars.”5,9 The arrival of Mikhail Gorbachev in 1985 changed the relationship. The two leaders met in Geneva in November 1985 and at Reykjavik in October 1986, where they discussed abolishing nuclear weapons altogether before the talks broke down over SDI.5 In Berlin on June 12, 1987, Reagan called out, “Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!”12,9 In December 1987 the two signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty, which called for destroying more than 2,600 Soviet and American nuclear weapons, In June 1988 Reagan visited Moscow, met Soviet citizens in Red Square, and spoke to students at Gorbachev’s alma mater.5,1

Beliefs and ideas

Reagan believed that high taxes and an overgrown federal government stifled economic growth and individual freedom, and that the United States must confront communism and negotiate with the Soviet Union only from strength.2,1 He also feared that the doctrine of “mutual assured destruction” could lead to nuclear war, and he pursued the vision of a world without nuclear weapons.1,5,8 He was a pragmatist in practice. As governor he said he preferred partial victories to “going off the cliff with all flags flying,” and many of his main achievements were bipartisan compromises.2,8

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Reagan’s economic program cut taxes but never balanced the budget. The deficit reached more than $220 billion in 1986, and the national debt roughly tripled, from $914 billion to $2.6 trillion.4 The Miller Center’s figures on the smallest annual deficit vary slightly, at least $153 billion for 1983–89 in one passage and $149 billion for 1985–89 in another.4 The gains of the 1980s were unequal. During his first term the incomes of the richest fifth of families rose by 9 percent while those of the poorest fifth fell by 8 percent.4

Civil rights groups criticized his record. In the summer of 1980, after the Republican convention, he spoke at the Neshoba County Fair in Mississippi, near where three civil rights workers had been murdered in 1964, and spoke in favor of “states’ rights.”3,9 The American Yawp argues that his attacks on welfare used thinly veiled racial stereotypes. His administration also backed Bob Jones University’s challenge to the policy of denying tax exemptions to segregated schools, before retreating.9,4

Abroad, Reagan supported anticommunist forces from Afghanistan to Central America, a policy called the Reagan Doctrine.5 His backing of the Contra rebels against Nicaragua’s Sandinista government divided Congress, which restricted aid with the Boland Amendments.5 In October 1983 a truck bomb killed 241 American servicemen, most of them Marines, at their barracks in Beirut, and the force was withdrawn early in 1984. That same month the United States invaded Grenada.5 The gravest scandal was the Iran-Contra affair, revealed in late 1986. National Security Council staff had secretly sold arms to Iran to win the release of American hostages in Lebanon and diverted some of the profits to the Contras despite the congressional ban.1,5 Reagan at first denied trading arms for hostages. In a speech on March 4, 1987, he took “full responsibility” and admitted that “the facts and the evidence tell me it is not” true.5 The independent counsel, Lawrence Walsh, concluded in 1993 that there was no credible evidence Reagan knew of the diversion. The Miller Center adds that the inquiries showed “lax management and enormous detachment” on his part.5

Legacy

Reagan left office in January 1989 with the highest approval rating of any departing president since Franklin Roosevelt and handed power to his vice president, George H. W. Bush.1 In a letter of November 5, 1994, he told the nation he had Alzheimer’s disease: “I now begin the journey that will lead me into the sunset of my life. I know that for America there will always be a bright dawn ahead.”6 He withdrew from public view, and he died on June 5, 2004. After a state funeral he was buried at his presidential library in Simi Valley, California.6

Historians still debate why the Cold War ended as it did. Some credit Reagan’s military buildup and SDI, others the unrest in Eastern Europe, the war in Afghanistan, or the collapse of the Soviet economy. The Miller Center concludes that there is no consensus, but that Reagan’s policies clearly contributed.8 Reagan and Gorbachev themselves said that the whole world had won.8 At home, his landslides pushed the Democrats toward the center, and Republicans for decades presented themselves as his heirs. The Miller Center calls him a transformational president who “casts a long shadow.”8
