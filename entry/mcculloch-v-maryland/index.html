Supreme Court Cases

Court Case·The Early Republic

McCulloch v. Maryland

The Supreme Court upholds broad federal power and bars states from taxing the national bank

1819 · Washington, D.C.

When Maryland taxed the Baltimore branch of the Second Bank of the United States, the bank’s cashier, James W. McCulloch, refused to pay.1 On March 6, 1819, the Supreme Court, speaking through Chief Justice John Marshall, ruled that Congress had the implied power to charter a bank under the Constitution’s “necessary and proper” clause.1,2 It also ruled that states could not tax the federal government, because “the power to tax involves the power to destroy.”1 The decision became a cornerstone of national power and of American federalism.2

Why it matters

McCulloch v. Maryland established that the federal government has powers implied by, but not listed in, the Constitution, and that federal law prevails over conflicting state action. Those principles lie at the heart of American federalism.1,2

Historical context

Whether Congress could create a national bank had divided the founders. In 1791 Alexander Hamilton’s plan for a Bank of the United States won congressional approval over strong opposition: Jefferson and other Republicans argued that “the Constitution did not authorize Congress to create a bank,” while Hamilton argued it was both constitutional and important for the country’s prosperity.5 The Constitution Center’s commentary adds that Madison and Jefferson feared a bank would serve Northern merchants and create “a financial aristocracy.”2

The First Bank’s twenty-year charter expired in 1811. In 1816 Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States to help control the unregulated currency issued by state banks.1,6 Many states doubted its constitutionality, and in 1818 Maryland passed a law taxing all banks not chartered by the state, which in practice meant the national bank’s Baltimore branch. Its cashier refused to pay, and Maryland sued him.1

The legal question

  • Does the Constitution give Congress the power to create a bank?
  • If so, may a state tax that bank?

Beneath both, the National Archives notes, lay a larger question: “Does the Federal Government hold sovereign power over states?”1

The ruling

The Court held that Congress had the power to incorporate the bank, and that Maryland could not tax an instrument the national government used to carry out its constitutional powers. Maryland’s tax was unconstitutional.1,3,4

The Court’s reasoning

Implied powers. Marshall admitted that among the enumerated powers “we do not find that of establishing a bank or creating a corporation.” But unlike the Articles of Confederation, the Constitution contains no phrase excluding “incidental or implied powers.”2 A constitution that tried to list every means of government “would partake of the prolixity of a legal code, and could scarcely be embraced by the human mind.” Hence his most quoted line: “we must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding.”2 It was “intended to endure for ages to come, and consequently, to be adapted to the various crises of human affairs.”2

Necessary and proper. The clause lets Congress choose its means: “Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional.”2

Supremacy. The government of the Union, “though limited in its powers, is supreme within its sphere of action.”2 So a state could not tax it: “the power to tax involves the power to destroy.” If states could tax one federal instrument, Marshall warned, “they may tax the mail; they may tax the mint,” and the people “did not design to make their government dependent on the states.”2,1

Consequences

The decision secured the Second Bank, but not for long. Andrew Jackson, who blamed the bank for the Panic of 1819, vetoed its recharter in 1832, calling it unconstitutional and “dangerous to the liberties of the people.”6 In that veto he rejected the idea that the Court had settled the matter: “The Congress, the Executive, and the Court must each for itself be guided by its own opinion of the Constitution,” and “the opinion of the judges has no more authority over Congress than the opinion of Congress has over the judges.”7

Later interpretation

How later courts, scholars, and the public have judged the case. This is not part of the ruling itself.

McCulloch is regarded as one of the most important decisions in American constitutional history. Its broad reading of implied powers gave Congress wide latitude to choose how to exercise its powers and confirmed that states could not interfere with legitimate federal operations.1,2 The balance between national authority and states’ rights that it addressed, which Marshall himself predicted “will probably continue to arise, so long as our system shall exist,” has remained contested ever since.2

The lawyers

The case was argued for nine days, from February 22 to March 3, 1819, by some of the most famous lawyers of the age. Daniel Webster, the attorney general and William Pinkney argued for the bank; Joseph Hopkinson, Walter Jones and Maryland’s attorney general, Luther Martin, for the state.3
