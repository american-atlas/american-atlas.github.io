Plessy v. Ferguson

The Supreme Court upholds “separate but equal,” 1896

1896 · Washington, D.C.

In 1892 Homer Plessy, a New Orleans man who was seven-eighths white, deliberately sat in a whites-only railroad car to challenge Louisiana’s law requiring segregated trains.1 On May 18, 1896, the Supreme Court upheld the law by 7 to 1, ruling that “equal but separate” accommodations did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment.1 Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented alone, declaring that “our constitution is color-blind.”1 The decision gave constitutional approval to the Jim Crow system of segregation for more than half a century, until Brown v. Board of Education rejected it in 1954.1,2

Why it matters

Historical context

During Reconstruction, three constitutional amendments and a series of federal laws affirmed Black Americans’ rights. The Civil Rights Act of 1875 made it a crime to deny anyone “the full and equal enjoyment” of inns, public conveyances, and theaters.1 In 1883 the Supreme Court struck down that act, ruling that the Fourteenth Amendment did not give Congress power to stop discrimination by private individuals and leaving Black citizens to seek relief from the states.1 At the same time, Southern states were writing inequality into law. Separate schools came first, and segregation soon spread to most public facilities.1 From the early 1890s these states also used literacy tests and “grandfather clauses” to take the vote from Black men.4

Railroads were a key battleground. Beginning with an 1887 Florida law, states required separate cars for each race.1 When a bill was proposed in Louisiana in 1890, the Black community of New Orleans protested, but the law passed despite the presence of sixteen Black legislators. It required separate or partitioned coaches and punished passengers who sat in the wrong section with a $25 fine or twenty days in jail. Black nurses caring for white children, however, could ride in white compartments.1 The railroads themselves disliked the law because of the cost of adding cars.1

In 1891 young Black men in New Orleans formed the “Citizens’ Committee to Test the Constitutionality of the Separate Car Law” and hired Albion W. Tourgée, a Radical Republican writer and politician, as their lawyer.1 After the Louisiana Supreme Court ruled in May 1892 that the law could not be applied to interstate travel, the committee arranged a test case on a trip within the state. With the cooperation of the East Louisiana Railroad, on June 7, 1892, Homer Plessy took a seat in a white compartment, was challenged by the conductor, and was arrested.1 Judge John H. Ferguson of the Orleans Parish criminal court ruled against him, and the Louisiana Supreme Court upheld the law but allowed an appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court.1 (The American Yawp describes Plessy’s challenge as aimed at the segregation of streetcars. The National Archives’ account describes railroad coaches.)3,1

The legal question

Does a state law requiring separate railway accommodations for white and Black passengers violate the Thirteenth Amendment’s ban on slavery or the Fourteenth Amendment’s guarantee of the equal protection of the laws?1

The ruling

By a vote of 7 to 1, the Court upheld the Louisiana law. As long as the separate accommodations were equal, segregation did not violate the Constitution.1

The Court’s reasoning

Justice Henry Brown of Michigan wrote for the majority. He argued that “social prejudice” could not be overcome by legislation and that, if the civil and political rights of both races were equal, neither could be inferior to the other civilly or politically. He rejected the argument that segregation was a mark of inferiority: “We consider the underlying fallacy of the plaintiff’s argument to consist in the assumption that the enforced separation of the two races stamps the colored race with a badge of inferiority. If this be so, it is not by reason of anything found in the act, but solely because the colored race chooses to put that construction upon it.”1 “If one race be inferior to the other socially,” he concluded, “the Constitution of the United States cannot put them upon the same plane.”1

Justice John Marshall Harlan of Kentucky dissented alone.1 “In the eye of the law, there is in this country no superior, dominant, ruling class of citizens. There is no caste here. Our constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens.”1 He warned that the law put “the brand of servitude and degradation upon a large class of our fellow citizens,” that “the thin disguise of ‘equal’ accommodations for passengers in railroad coaches will not mislead any one,” and that the decision would “prove to be quite as pernicious as the decision made by this tribunal in the Dred Scott Case.”1

Consequences

Plessy gave constitutional approval to the Jim Crow system. Segregation, already spreading, now extended to schools, transportation, and most public and semi-public places across the South, and for more than fifty years the great majority of African Americans lived under what the National Archives calls “second-class citizenship.”1,4 Just one year before the decision, Booker T. Washington had told white Southerners in his Atlanta speech that “in all things that are purely social we can be as separate as the fingers.”3

Later interpretation

How later courts, scholars, and the public have judged the case. This is not part of the ruling itself.

The NAACP spent decades attacking “separate but equal.” In 1930 it commissioned the Margold Report, which became the basis for its legal campaign against the doctrine.5 On May 17, 1954, in Brown v. Board of Education, a unanimous Court under Chief Justice Earl Warren held that segregated public schools violated the Fourteenth Amendment. “We cannot turn the clock back to 1868, when the Amendment was adopted, or even to 1896, when Plessy v. Ferguson was written,” the Court said, and “any language in Plessy v. Ferguson contrary to this finding is rejected.”2 After Brown and the civil rights laws of the 1950s and 1960s, many states rewrote their constitutions to conform with the spirit of the Fourteenth Amendment. “For Homer Plessy,” the National Archives notes, “the remedies came too late.”1
