Abraham Lincoln

Sixteenth president, who preserved the Union and ended slavery

1809–1865

Abraham Lincoln rose from a one-room log cabin on the Kentucky frontier to become the sixteenth president of the United States.1 Elected in 1860 as the first Republican president, he led the Union through the Civil War, issued the Emancipation Proclamation, and pushed the Thirteenth Amendment abolishing slavery through Congress.3,4,5 He was assassinated by John Wilkes Booth in April 1865, days after the war’s end.6 Historians and the public alike rank him as the nation’s greatest president.7

Why it matters

Lincoln saved the Union and destroyed slavery, and he did both by appealing to the nation’s founding ideals, above all the Declaration’s promise that all men are created equal.2,7 His wartime presidency also transformed the office, setting precedents for presidential power in a national emergency that historians still debate.7

Early life and context

Abraham Lincoln was born on February 12, 1809, in a one-room log cabin with a dirt floor in Hardin County, Kentucky.2 His father, Thomas, a farmer and carpenter who could barely sign his name, moved the family to the Indiana frontier when Abraham was seven. His mother, Nancy Hanks, died when he was nine; his stepmother, Sarah Bush Johnston, encouraged his education and took his side in his frequent arguments with his father.2 Abraham grew up with farm chores and little schooling, reading by firelight. At nineteen he built a flatboat and took a load of produce down the Mississippi to New Orleans, then dutifully, if resentfully, handed his earnings to his father.2

In 1831, at twenty-two, he struck out on his own in the village of New Salem, Illinois. Six feet four inches tall and strong enough to win a wrestling match against the town bully, he clerked in a store, served as postmaster, worked as a surveyor, and split rails, and his wit and his ability to read and write made him a popular figure.2 He volunteered in the Black Hawk War of 1832, and his company elected him captain, an honor he later said meant more to him than his nomination for president; he saw no fighting.2 He lost his first race for the state legislature that year, though he won 277 of the 300 votes cast in New Salem, and then won four terms from 1834 to 1840 as a Whig who favored roads, canals, and a state bank.2 He taught himself law, received his license in 1837, and moved to Springfield, where he became one of the most successful lawyers in Illinois.2

Major actions and achievements

As a young legislator Lincoln was one of only six members, out of eighty-three, who voted against a 1837 resolution condemning abolitionists.2 Elected to Congress in 1846, he challenged President James Polk’s claim that Mexico had started the Mexican-American War on American soil, an unpopular stand at home, and served only a single term.2 After several years practicing law, he returned to politics in 1854, and in 1856 he joined the new Republican Party, which was dedicated to repealing the Kansas-Nebraska Act and stopping the spread of slavery into the West.2

In 1858 the Republicans nominated Lincoln for the Senate against Stephen A. Douglas, the Democratic “Little Giant.” He opened with his “House Divided” speech: “A house divided against itself cannot stand,” he warned; the nation could not remain permanently half slave and half free.2 In seven debates across Illinois, followed by reporters from around the country, Lincoln insisted that slavery was a moral wrong that violated the Declaration of Independence and must not spread, while Douglas defended letting each territory decide. The state legislature, which then chose senators, returned Douglas by 54 votes to 46, but the contest made Lincoln a national leader of his party.2

At the Republican convention in Chicago in May 1860, Lincoln trailed the favorite, Senator William H. Seward of New York, on the first ballot but won on the third.8 The Democrats split, with Northern Democrats nominating Douglas and Southern Democrats John C. Breckinridge, and a new Constitutional Union Party nominated John Bell.8 Lincoln, who was not on the ballot in any Southern state, carried all but one free state and won 180 electoral votes, a clear majority, against 72 for Breckinridge, 39 for Bell, and 12 for Douglas.8 Before he took office, seven states of the Lower South seceded and formed the Confederate States of America.3 In his inaugural address of March 4, 1861, Lincoln promised not to interfere with slavery where it existed but vowed to hold federal property, and pleaded, “We must not be enemies.”3 When he sent unarmed ships to resupply Fort Sumter, Jefferson Davis ordered the fort attacked; it surrendered after thirty-three hours, and the war began.3

As commander in chief, Lincoln adopted General Winfield Scott’s Anaconda strategy of blockade and encirclement and kept the four slaveholding border states in the Union.3 He replaced general after general, frustrated by commanders like George McClellan and George Meade who let Lee’s army escape after victories at Antietam and Gettysburg, until he found in Ulysses S. Grant a general who would press the enemy relentlessly.3 His administration also built the modern American state: it financed the war with greenbacks, the first federal income tax, national banks, and $2.6 billion in loans, and it passed the Homestead Act and the Morrill Act creating land-grant colleges.3

Lincoln moved step by step toward emancipation. At first he insisted that his purpose was to save the Union, not to free the slaves, and he proposed compensated, gradual emancipation in the border states, which they refused.3 But thousands of enslaved people were escaping to Union lines, Black Northerners and Radical Republicans demanded action, and emancipation would make it hard for Britain or France to recognize the Confederacy.3 On July 22, 1862, he told his cabinet he would issue an emancipation proclamation as a war measure, and after the Union victory at Antietam he announced it; on January 1, 1863, he declared all enslaved people in the rebellious areas “forever free” and opened the army to Black men.3,4 By the war’s end more than 180,000 Black soldiers had served.3 At Gettysburg in November 1863 he redefined the war in a few short paragraphs as a struggle for “a new birth of freedom.”9 Reelected in 1864 with 55 percent of the vote, helped by Sherman’s capture of Atlanta and the votes of Union soldiers, he used every power of his office to push the Thirteenth Amendment through the House, which passed it on January 31, 1865.8,5

Beliefs and ideas

Lincoln hated slavery, which he believed mocked the Declaration of Independence and threatened the survival of the Union.3 Yet he was also a careful politician who compromised on the issue for most of his career. He identified with antislavery forces that opposed slavery’s expansion rather than with abolitionists who demanded its immediate end, and he believed the Constitution gave the federal government no power to touch slavery where it already existed.3 He was a believer in “free labor”: every man, Black or white, had the right to the fruits of his own work.2 He held that the Union was permanent and secession legally void, and that the right of revolution existed only against real oppression, which the South could not claim.3

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Lincoln’s views on race reflected many of the prejudices of his time. In the 1858 debates, under attack from Douglas, he said that he opposed giving Black Americans the right to vote, serve on juries, or hold office, and for years he supported the voluntary colonization of freed people abroad, even funding failed projects in Central America and Haiti.2,3 Only near the end of his life, on April 11, 1865, did he publicly endorse voting rights for some Black men.6,3

His use of power was also hotly contested. He called up troops, spent money without congressional approval, and suspended the writ of habeas corpus; between fifteen and twenty thousand civilians, mostly in the border states, were arrested on suspicion of disloyalty.3,7 The antiwar Democrat Clement Vallandigham was convicted by a military court for his speeches, and after the war the Supreme Court ruled that military trials of civilians where civilian courts were open were unconstitutional.3 Lincoln defended his actions as emergency measures, asking whether it made sense “to lose the nation and yet preserve the Constitution.”7

Legacy

On April 3, 1865, Lincoln walked through the streets of Richmond, the fallen Confederate capital, cheered by crowds of freed people. Six days later Lee surrendered at Appomattox.6 On the evening of April 14, while Lincoln watched the comedy Our American Cousin at Ford’s Theatre in Washington, the actor and Confederate sympathizer John Wilkes Booth shot him in the back of the head; Lincoln died the next morning.6 Booth, who had decided to kill the president after hearing him endorse Black suffrage, was cornered in a Virginia barn, where he either shot himself or was killed, and four of his co-conspirators were hanged.6 Lincoln’s funeral train carried his body some 1,700 miles back to Springfield, as crowds gathered along the route to mourn him.6

Within days of his death, Americans began to see Lincoln as a martyr and the “Great Emancipator.”7 Historians, too, have ranked him first among the presidents: in a 1982 survey of forty-nine historians and political scientists he led in every category.7 His greatest achievements, in their judgment, were the preservation of the Union, the vindication of democracy, and the death of slavery.7 His home in Springfield is preserved today as the Lincoln Home National Historic Site.10
