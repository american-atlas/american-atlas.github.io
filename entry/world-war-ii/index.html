World War II

The United States in the global war, 1941–1945

December 7, 1941 – September 2, 1945 · Europe, North Africa, the Pacific and Asia, the Atlantic; the home front

World War II was the deadliest and most destructive war in human history: perhaps eighty million people died.1 The United States entered after Japan’s attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, and fought alongside Britain, the Soviet Union, China, and other Allies against Germany, Italy, and Japan.1,2 The Soviet Union bore the heaviest burden of the war against Germany and broke Hitler’s army, while American factories armed the Allies and American forces fought in North Africa, Italy, and France and across the Pacific.1 The war ended with Germany’s surrender in May 1945 and Japan’s in August, after the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.1,9,10 It left the United States the world’s leading economic and military power.1

Why it matters

World War II was the most destructive conflict in history. It defeated fascism and Nazism through the combined efforts of the Allies; the Soviet Union, at a cost of some 24 to 25 million lives, broke the German army, while Britain and the United States fought in the west and the Pacific. It made the United States the world’s leading military and economic power and set the stage for the Cold War, the civil rights movement, and the postwar boom.1,8

Who fought

The Allies were led by the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, and China, the nations Roosevelt called the “Four Policemen.” They fought the Axis powers of Germany, Italy, and Japan, which had linked themselves in the Tripartite Pact of September 1940.1,2 Franklin D. Roosevelt, Winston Churchill, and Joseph Stalin, the “Big Three,” coordinated strategy at Tehran in 1943 and Yalta in February 1945; Harry S. Truman, Stalin, and Churchill (replaced partway through by Clement Attlee) met at Potsdam in July 1945.1,6,7

Almost eighteen million Americans served, more than ten million of them draftees.1 They included about 900,000 African Americans by the National WWII Museum’s count (the American Yawp says more than a million), mostly in segregated units; 33,000 Japanese Americans, many of whose families were incarcerated at home; and more than 350,000 women in the women’s branches and nurse corps.5,1

Political background

For Japan, the war began a decade before Pearl Harbor. In September 1931 an explosion on a Japanese-owned railway in Manchuria, most likely staged by the Japanese themselves, gave Japan’s army a pretext to seize the region. On July 7, 1937, Japan launched a full-scale invasion of China, and when its army took the Chinese capital, Nanjing, in December, it killed between 250,000 and 300,000 people.1 In Europe, Adolf Hitler became chancellor of Germany in 1933. His Nazi regime crushed democracy, rearmed in defiance of the Treaty of Versailles, and sought Lebensraum, “living space,” in Eastern Europe.1 Germany annexed Austria in 1938 and, after Britain and France agreed to let it take Czechoslovakia’s Sudetenland, seized the rest of Czechoslovakia in March 1939. On September 1, 1939, Germany invaded Poland, and Britain and France declared war two days later.1 Germany conquered France in the spring of 1940 and bombed British cities in the Blitz, but the Royal Air Force won the Battle of Britain.1

Disillusioned by the carnage of World War I, many Americans wanted to stay out.1,3 Roosevelt declared that the nation would be neutral in law but could not be “neutral in thought.”3 In September 1940 he traded more than fifty old destroyers to Britain for leases on bases in Newfoundland and the Caribbean. In 1941 Congress approved Lend-Lease, which let the United States “lend” war supplies to nations fighting the Axis; by the war’s end more than thirty countries had received some $50 billion in aid.3 On June 22, 1941, Germany invaded the Soviet Union in the largest land invasion in history.1

In the Pacific, the United States had tried to pressure Japan to leave China. It gave credits to China, and after Japan moved into French Indochina in 1941 it imposed a full embargo on exports to Japan and froze Japanese assets.2 Japan’s leaders regarded the oil embargo as an act of war.1 On the morning of December 7, 1941, Japanese planes attacked the U.S. Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii. In less than two hours more than 3,500 Americans were killed or wounded, about 2,400 of them killed.4,1 “Yesterday, December 7, 1941—a date which will live in infamy—the United States of America was suddenly and deliberately attacked,” Roosevelt told Congress the next day. The Senate voted for war unanimously, with a single dissenting vote in the House, that of Jeannette Rankin of Montana.4 Days later Germany declared war on the United States, and the separate wars in Asia and Europe became one world war.2,1

Major phases

The Atlantic and North Africa (1942–1943). German U-boats sank Allied shipping in the Atlantic, but code-breaking, convoys, sonar, and depth charges won the Battle of the Atlantic for the Allies.1 In November 1942 American troops landed in French Morocco, and with the British advancing from El Alamein they drove Axis forces out of Africa by 1943.1 Meeting at Casablanca in January 1943, Roosevelt and Churchill agreed to invade Italy next and announced that they would accept nothing less than unconditional surrender.1

The air war and Italy (1943–1945). American bombers struck German factories, rail yards, and oil fields by day while the British bombed cities at night. Unescorted American bombers suffered terrible losses until long-range fighters could accompany them.1 Allied bombing killed perhaps 400,000 German civilians.1 The campaign in Italy, which Churchill had called Europe’s “soft underbelly,” proved slow and bloody in mountainous terrain. Mussolini’s government fell, and a new Italian government made peace.1

The Eastern Front. The decisive land war in Europe was fought between Germany and the Soviet Union. After Germany’s invasion in June 1941, the Soviets moved their factories east of the Urals and defended Moscow, Leningrad, and Stalingrad. Leningrad endured a long siege by starvation, and at Stalingrad German forces and their allies suffered some 850,000 casualties.1 “It was the Soviet Union that broke Hitler’s army,” the American Yawp concludes. About 80 percent of all German casualties came on the Eastern Front, and roughly 25 million Soviet soldiers and civilians died in what Russians call the Great Patriotic War; the National WWII Museum estimates 24 million.1,8 At Tehran in November 1943, Stalin pressed Roosevelt and Churchill to open a second front in France to relieve the pressure on the Red Army, and Roosevelt agreed.1

Normandy to the Elbe (1944–1945). On June 6, 1944, American, British, and Canadian troops landed in Normandy in Operation Overlord, the largest amphibious assault in history. The night before, General Dwight D. Eisenhower drafted two statements, one for success and one for failure.1 Paris was liberated about two months later. Germany’s last offensive in the west, the Battle of the Bulge, failed that winter.1 Soviet forces reached Germany in January 1945, and the Americans crossed the Rhine in March. In late April American and Soviet troops met at the Elbe River, and Soviet forces took Berlin in early May, days after Hitler’s suicide. Germany surrendered on May 8, 1945.1,7

The Pacific (1941–1945). After Pearl Harbor, Japan took the Philippines. American and Filipino prisoners were forced on a march of some eighty miles without food or water, and about ten thousand died on the Bataan Death March.1 In 1942 the naval battles of the Coral Sea and Midway crippled Japan’s navy, and American forces began “island hopping,” seizing islands that could hold airfields and bypassing others.1 The fighting was savage. At Iwo Jima, seventeen thousand Japanese defenders inflicted almost thirty thousand casualties on seventy thousand Marines before the island fell; at Okinawa, captured in June 1945 after eighty days, tens of thousands were killed or wounded.1 American bombers firebombed more than sixty Japanese cities, killing about 100,000 civilians in a single raid on Tokyo in March 1945.1

Turning points

Midway (June 1942). The carrier battle, with the earlier fight in the Coral Sea, crippled Japan’s naval operations in the Pacific.1

Stalingrad (1942–1943). At Stalingrad, where German forces and their allies suffered some 850,000 casualties, the Soviet victory was followed by a Soviet advance west that German counterattacks could not stop.1

D-Day (June 6, 1944). The Normandy landings opened the second front in Western Europe that Stalin had long demanded.1

The atomic bomb (August 1945). Fearing that Germany might build an atomic bomb, the United States had launched the secret Manhattan Project. The first test, Trinity, took place in New Mexico in July 1945.1 American officials estimated that invading Japan could cost half a million American casualties and perhaps millions of Japanese lives, numbers they later cited to justify the decision. Historians still debate the motives behind the decision, and many American officials criticized it.1 On August 6 a B-29, the Enola Gay, dropped a uranium bomb on Hiroshima, a city of almost 300,000. Some 70,000 people probably died from the blast, heat, and radiation, and the toll passed 100,000 by the end of 1945.9 On August 9 a plutonium bomb struck Nagasaki, killing about 40,000 people at once and perhaps 70,000 by January 1946 (the American Yawp gives about 80,000).10,1 The day after Nagasaki, Emperor Hirohito overruled Japan’s military leaders and offered to surrender.10 He announced Japan’s surrender on August 15, and on September 2, 1945, Japanese delegates signed the formal surrender aboard the USS Missouri.1

Outcome

The Allies won. Germany was disarmed and divided into four occupation zones run by the United States, Britain, France, and the Soviet Union, and the Allies agreed at Potsdam to try German war criminals and remake German society along democratic lines.7 Poland’s borders were moved westward, and several million Germans were expelled from the territories transferred to Poland and from other countries.7 At Yalta, Stalin had agreed to enter the war against Japan in return for southern Sakhalin, the Kurile Islands, and rights in Manchuria.6

As the Allies advanced, they uncovered the full scale of the Holocaust, the systematic murder of some eleven million civilians, including six million Jews.1 Americans had done little to help Jewish refugees before the war. In 1939 the ship St. Louis, carrying more than nine hundred Jewish refugees, was turned away, and hundreds of its passengers later died in the Holocaust. In 1944 Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau formed the War Refugee Board, which may have saved some 200,000 Jews.1 On June 26, 1945, fifty nations signed the charter of the United Nations in San Francisco, with a Security Council of five permanent members, each holding a veto.1,6

Consequences for American society

The home front. “Economies win wars no less than militaries,” the American Yawp observes.1 Government spending during the war doubled all federal spending in American history up to that time, and unemployment, a quarter of the workforce a decade earlier, virtually disappeared.1 Americans lived with rationing of gasoline, meat, sugar, and coffee, bought war bonds, and paid income taxes that reached a top rate of 94 percent.1 Millions of women took factory and office jobs. “Rosie the Riveter” became their symbol, and more than 350,000 women served in uniform, though most lost or left their jobs when the men came home.1 Under the Bracero Program, begun in 1942, Mexican workers came north on contracts to work on farms and railroads.1

Race and rights. In 1941 A. Philip Randolph threatened a march on Washington until Roosevelt issued Executive Order 8802, which banned discrimination in defense industries.1 About a million African Americans served, mostly in segregated units. The Tuskegee Airmen flew more than 1,500 missions. Black newspapers called for a “Double V,” victory over fascism abroad and racism at home, and NAACP membership grew tenfold, to 500,000.1 On February 19, 1942, Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, under which more than 110,000 people of Japanese descent, 70,000 of them American citizens, were removed from their homes and confined in camps. In 1982 a congressional commission blamed “race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership,” and in 1988 the government apologized and paid reparations.1

A new world. The war pulled the United States out of the Depression and into an unprecedented boom.1 The G.I. Bill of 1944 paid for veterans’ education and helped close to four million of them buy homes, though women and Black veterans received far fewer of its benefits.1 The United States emerged as the world’s strongest economic and military power. But the wartime alliance with the Soviet Union soon broke down, and disputes over Eastern Europe began at Yalta and Potsdam and grew into the Cold War.1,6,7
