The New Deal

Franklin D. Roosevelt’s programs of relief, recovery, and reform, 1933–1939

March 4, 1933 – 1938 · Washington, D.C., and nationwide

The New Deal was President Franklin D. Roosevelt’s response to the Great Depression: a burst of federal programs to stabilize the banks, put the unemployed to work, raise farm prices, protect workers’ right to organize, and create a safety net for the elderly and the jobless.1,2,7 It did not end the Depression, and it left racial and gender inequalities largely in place.1,11 But it transformed the role of the federal government and the presidency, and it built a Democratic coalition that dominated politics for a generation.1,11

Why it matters

The New Deal did not end the Great Depression, but it made the federal government responsible for the economic security of ordinary Americans. It created institutions, from Social Security to the FDIC, that still shape American life.1,2,11

Historical background

By the time Roosevelt took office on March 4, 1933, the economy had collapsed. More than twelve million Americans were out of work, a quarter of the workforce, and a new wave of bank failures had hit in February.2,7 Two of every five banks open in 1929 had closed.1 Herbert Hoover had relied on voluntary action and loans to business, and voters had rejected him in a landslide.1

Roosevelt, the Democratic governor of New York, had been born to a wealthy Hudson Valley family, served as assistant secretary of the navy in World War I, and been paralyzed by polio in 1921.1 As governor he had told the legislature that relief was owed “not as a matter of charity, but as a matter of social duty,” and his Temporary Emergency Relief Administration came to support a tenth of the state’s families.1 Accepting the Democratic nomination on July 1, 1932, he declared, “I pledge you, I pledge myself, to a new deal for the American people.” Newspaper editors seized on the phrase.1 Hoover warned that such a program meant “the total abandonment of every principle upon which this government and the American system is founded.”1

Causes and contributing factors

The New Deal had no single blueprint. Roosevelt believed in American capitalism but thought it had to be reformed to survive, and he was a pragmatist: he would try something, and if it failed, try something else.2 Even the New Deal economist Alvin Hansen admitted in 1940, “I really do not know what the basic principle of the New Deal is.”2 Its programs rested on different, sometimes contradictory, theories of the Depression’s causes. Some blamed overproduction, some cutthroat competition, some a lack of purchasing power; John Maynard Keynes’s theory of deficit spending was not even published until 1936.7,2 Roosevelt surrounded himself with a “Brain Trust” of academic advisers and appointees such as Harry Hopkins and Frances Perkins, the first woman to serve in a cabinet. He often gave overlapping tasks to aides who disagreed with one another.2,1

What happened

The Hundred Days. “The only thing we have to fear is fear itself,” Roosevelt said in his inaugural address.8 Two days later he declared a national bank holiday, and Congress passed his Emergency Banking Act almost sight unseen. Federal officials would inspect every bank, reopen the sound ones, and close the rest.2,1 On March 12 he explained the plan in the first of his radio “fireside chats,” and in the following weeks Americans returned nearly $1 billion to the banks.2 Between March and June 1933, Congress passed a flood of laws.2 The Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC) put 300,000 young men to work in 1,200 camps planting trees, building bridges, and cleaning beaches. The Federal Emergency Relief Administration (FERA) sent cash to state relief agencies. The Securities Act required honest information about stocks, and the Glass-Steagall Act created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) and separated commercial from investment banking.1,2 Roosevelt also took the country off the gold standard.2

Planning for recovery. The heart of the early New Deal was two experiments in economic planning. The Agricultural Adjustment Act (May 1933) paid farmers to cut production in order to raise prices; more than three million farmers joined, and farm income rose by more than half between 1932 and 1935.1,2 But in the South, landowners often kept the payments, evicted their tenants and sharecroppers, and bought machinery instead, pushing the poorest farmworkers off the land.1 The National Industrial Recovery Act (June 1933) suspended the antitrust laws so that businesses could write more than 500 “codes of fair competition” fixing prices, wages, and production. Section 7a promised workers the right to bargain collectively, and cooperating businesses displayed the National Recovery Administration’s Blue Eagle.3,2 The codes, the National Archives concludes, “did little to help recovery, and by raising prices, they actually made the economic situation worse.”3 The same law created the Public Works Administration, which spent more than $3 billion on projects such as the Triborough Bridge in New York.2

The Tennessee Valley. On May 18, 1933, Roosevelt signed the act creating the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA), a federal corporation charged with building dams to control floods, improve navigation, and generate cheap electricity in one of the poorest regions in the country.4,2 It was the first time a federal agency was directed to develop the resources of an entire region. Its dams brought electric lights, appliances, jobs, and industry to the valley, but they also displaced thousands of families, sometimes whole towns.4,2 The Rural Electrification Administration later brought electricity to millions of farm homes.2

Critics and the courts. Many Americans thought the New Deal did too little. Senator Huey Long of Louisiana won a mass following for his Share Our Wealth plan to redistribute the fortunes of the rich; more than 27,000 Share Our Wealth clubs sprang up before he was assassinated in 1935.1 Dr. Francis Townsend campaigned for old-age pensions, and the radio priest Charles Coughlin attacked Roosevelt in broadcasts that, the American Yawp notes, were vitriolic and anti-Semitic.1,2 From the right, conservative businessmen and dissident Democrats formed the American Liberty League in 1934 to denounce the New Deal as an un-American attack on free enterprise.2 The Supreme Court struck hardest. On May 27, 1935, in Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, it unanimously ruled the National Industrial Recovery Act unconstitutional, and in 1936 it struck down the AAA.1,3,2

The Second New Deal. Roosevelt answered by moving left. In 1935 Congress created the Works Progress Administration (WPA) with a nearly $5 billion appropriation. By 1937 it employed three million people building schools, hospitals, airports, and more than half a million miles of roads, and it hired artists, writers, and musicians as well.1,2 The National Labor Relations Act of July 5, 1935, known as the Wagner Act after its sponsor, Senator Robert Wagner of New York, guaranteed workers “the right to self-organization” and created the National Labor Relations Board to enforce it.5 Union membership surged: in 1937 sit-down strikers in Flint, Michigan, won recognition for the United Auto Workers from General Motors.1,5 On August 14, 1935, Roosevelt signed the Social Security Act, which created old-age pensions, unemployment insurance, and aid to dependent children and the blind. (The National Archives’ own page gives both August 14 and August 15 as the date of signing.)6,2 Roosevelt insisted that the pensions be paid from workers’ and employers’ contributions rather than general taxes. “No dole,” he said. “Mustn’t have a dole.”1,6

The second term. In 1936 Roosevelt won reelection in a landslide, losing only Maine and Vermont, and in his second inaugural vowed to help the third of the nation that was ill-housed, ill-clad, and ill-nourished.1,2 In February 1937, fearing the Court would strike down Social Security, he proposed adding a new justice for every sitting justice over a certain age, up to six in all. The American Yawp gives the age as seventy and the Miller Center as seventy-five.1,2 Critics called it “court-packing,” and the plan failed in Congress. But that spring the Court began upholding New Deal laws, including the Wagner Act in NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel (1937), and it has not struck down a major New Deal law since.2,5 That same year, believing the economy had recovered, Roosevelt cut federal spending, and the economy plunged into the “Roosevelt recession.” In April 1938 he reversed course and asked Congress for a $5 billion relief and public works program.2,9 The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 set a national minimum wage of 25 cents an hour and limited working hours.1,11

Immediate consequences

The New Deal stopped the collapse and brought partial recovery. The banking panic ended, farm incomes rose, and the WPA, CCC, and other agencies gave work to millions.1,2 But unemployment stayed high. Still above 19 percent in 1939, it remained in double digits in 1940, and stock prices had not recovered from the crash.2,11 Only the vast spending of World War II brought full employment.2,7

Politically, the tide turned. The court-packing fight and the 1937 recession helped a conservative coalition of Southern Democrats and Republicans gain ground in the 1938 elections. Roosevelt tried to purge conservative Democrats in that year’s primaries; of the ten he targeted, only one lost.1,2 By 1939 he struggled to pass new reforms, and the coming war in Europe increasingly drew his attention.1 Congress did pass the Reorganization Act of 1939, which created the Executive Office of the President.2

Long-term significance

The New Deal changed what Americans expected from Washington. Before World War I the federal government had been, in the American Yawp’s phrase, a “government out of sight”; after the New Deal, Americans came to see it as an ally in finding work, earning a decent wage, getting a fair price for crops, or organizing a union.1 Social Security became the centerpiece of the American welfare state, and the floor under wages and ceiling on hours set by the Fair Labor Standards Act continue to this day.1,11 Roosevelt also reshaped the presidency: his fireside chats created a direct bond with the public, and the president became chief legislator as well as chief executive.11 Voter turnout jumped in 1932 and 1936, and most of the new voters stayed loyal to a Democratic coalition of liberals, urban workers, farm workers, and white Southerners well into the 1960s.1

The New Deal’s benefits were unequally shared. To keep the votes of Southern Democrats, Roosevelt refused to back a federal anti-lynching law or the abolition of the poll tax (“I just can’t take that risk,” he told the NAACP’s secretary), and he accepted the exclusion of farm workers and domestic servants from Social Security pensions, a decision that left out many Black and Mexican American workers.1,2 NRA codes kept lower wages for Black workers, and AAA payments helped push Black tenants and sharecroppers off the land.1 Many programs assumed that men were breadwinners and steered women into means-tested relief, although Roosevelt appointed Frances Perkins and the African American adviser Mary McLeod Bethune to prominent posts, and Eleanor Roosevelt became a major voice for economic and racial justice.1 Historians still debate when the New Deal ended. Some point to the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938; others see its spirit in wartime controls, the G.I. Bill, and a “New Deal order” that lasted until Lyndon Johnson’s Great Society.1
