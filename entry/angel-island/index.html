Angel Island Immigration Station

The West Coast station built to enforce exclusion, 1910–1940

January 21, 1910 – 1940 · San Francisco Bay, California

The immigration station on Angel Island in San Francisco Bay opened in 1910 and, in the words of the foundation that preserves it, “was designed to detain, rather than welcome, newcomers.”1 Under the Chinese Exclusion Act and later laws, Chinese and other Asian immigrants faced medical inspections, long detentions, and grueling interrogations to prove their right to enter.1,2,5 Some waited weeks, others years.1 Detainees carved poems into the barracks walls, and their rediscovery in 1970 led to the station’s preservation as a National Historic Landmark and museum.1,4

Why it matters

Angel Island shows the exclusionary side of American immigration history. Its foundation contrasts it with Ellis Island: on the West Coast, entry “was mediated through racial exclusion, restrictive legislation, and the US government’s attempt to control Pacific migration.” The poems on its walls preserve the voices of people the law tried to keep out.1,4

About the place

The station stands on Angel Island, in San Francisco Bay. The Bureau of Immigration chose the site for its isolation, believing it would keep detainees from escaping or communicating with friends and relatives in San Francisco.1 Visitors today can see the restored barracks, where Chinese detainees carved poems into the wooden walls, and the Angel Island Immigration Museum, opened in 2022 in the former hospital building.1

History

Exclusion. Immigrants from Guangdong Province in southern China began arriving in California in the mid-nineteenth century, driven by famine, rebellion, and economic collapse. Welcomed at first as laborers during the Gold Rush and railroad boom, they became scapegoats when the economy faltered in the 1870s.1 In 1882 Congress passed the Chinese Exclusion Act. The National Archives calls it “the first significant law restricting immigration into the United States”; it imposed a ten-year ban on Chinese laborers and barred courts from granting citizenship to Chinese immigrants.5 The ban was extended by the Geary Act in 1892 and made permanent in 1902.5

Paper sons and daughters. The law still allowed a few classes to enter: merchants, teachers, students, diplomats, clergy, and the children of those already here.1 After the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and fire destroyed the city’s birth and immigration records, Chinese residents could claim citizenship and report children born in China. Some sold these places to young people who wanted to come, often at about $100 per year of the buyer’s age.1,2 Each “paper child” memorized a detailed history of a family and village they might never have seen.1,2

The station. Ships from Asia took three weeks or more to cross the Pacific.1 On arrival passengers were separated by race, gender, and class. European and first-class travelers were usually processed on board, while Asian and Pacific Islander immigrants, especially those in steerage, were ferried to the island for medical inspections and interrogations.1 Before a Board of Special Inquiry, Chinese applicants answered long lists of questions about their supposed families, neighbors, and the layout of their villages, and any difference between their answers and a witness’s could mean deportation.1 The station’s foundation counts over 300,000 people detained there out of more than 550,000 who arrived at San Francisco between 1910 and 1940.3 They came from more than 80 countries, among them India, Japan, Russia, the Philippines, and Mexico; in the late 1930s they also included several hundred Jewish refugees fleeing persecution in Europe.1

The poems. Most of the poets were Cantonese-speaking men from villages of the Pearl River Delta, often with little formal schooling, writing in traditional Chinese forms about disappointment, daily hardship, and anger at their confinement.4 Most poems are unsigned and undated, probably out of fear of punishment, and none identified so far appear to have been written by women.4

Closing and rediscovery. In August 1940 a fire destroyed the administration building and many records, and the station closed that October. During World War II the site briefly held Japanese internees and prisoners of war.1 Congress repealed the exclusion acts in 1943, when China was a wartime ally, though a quota of 105 Chinese immigrants a year remained.5 In 1970 the state park ranger Alexander Weiss found hundreds of poems carved into the walls of the abandoned barracks. Chinese American activists organized to save the site, the state funded restoration in 1976, and it became a National Historic Landmark in 1997.1 In 2011 and 2012 both houses of Congress passed resolutions condemning the Chinese Exclusion Act.5
