Harpers Ferry

Armory town where John Brown struck at slavery

Harpers Ferry, West Virginia, where the Potomac and Shenandoah Rivers meet

Harpers Ferry, a small town where the Potomac and Shenandoah Rivers cut through the Blue Ridge, packs an unusual amount of American history into a few streets.1 A federal armory built there in the 1790s became a center of early industry, where John H. Hall made rifles with interchangeable parts.2,3 In October 1859 the abolitionist John Brown seized the armory in a failed attempt to start a war against slavery, a raid that pushed the country closer to civil war.4,10 In 1862 Confederate troops captured the Union garrison there, the largest surrender of Federal troops in the war.1,5 After the war Storer College educated formerly enslaved people, and in 1906 W. E. B. Du Bois and the Niagara Movement met on its campus.6,7

Why it matters

Harpers Ferry concentrates several stories of the nineteenth century in one small town: the rise of machine-made manufacturing, the violent struggle over slavery that John Brown’s raid sharpened, the Civil War, and the long fight for Black education and civil rights that carried on at Storer College and the Niagara Movement.1,4,6,7

About the place

The town stands on steep ground at the point where the Potomac and Shenandoah Rivers meet and break through the Blue Ridge. Thomas Jefferson, who stood on a rock above the rivers on October 25, 1783, called the passage “one of the most stupendous scenes in Nature” and wrote that it was “worth a voyage across the Atlantic.”9 The National Park Service, which preserves much of the town, describes its history as “multi-layered,” and its archeological record goes back millennia.1

History

The armory. In 1794 Congress approved a bill “for the erecting and repairing of Arsenals and Magazines.” After Springfield, Massachusetts, was chosen for the first national armory, President George Washington picked Harpers Ferry for the second.2 The government bought 125 acres between the two rivers in 1796, construction began in 1799, and full-scale production of muskets, rifles, and later pistols began three years later. By 1810 the armory made about 10,000 arms a year.2 It struggled with weak waterpower and charges of mismanagement, and it was rebuilt between 1845 and 1854. Its workforce grew from 25 men in 1802 to about 400 by 1859.2

Interchangeable parts. In 1819 the New England gunmaker John H. Hall contracted to make 1,000 of his breechloading rifles at Harpers Ferry. Over the next two decades he built machines to make rifles with interchangeable parts, a “boldly ambitious goal” in an industry based on skilled handwork.3 In 1822 he reported that he had found “methods for fabricating arms exactly alike, & with economy, by the hands of common workmen.”3 The Park Service credits him with “laying a solid foundation for America’s developing factory system.”3 The town was also reached by one of the first successful American railroads.1

John Brown’s raid. In the summer of 1859 John Brown rented a farm in Maryland about five miles away, where his men hid. They numbered twenty-one at the time of the raid.4 The American Yawp notes that the group included Black radicals from Oberlin, Ohio, and from Canada.10 On the night of October 16, the raiders seized the bridges, the armory and arsenal, and Hall’s rifle works, took local enslavers hostage, and freed people they enslaved.4 The first person mortally wounded was Heyward Shepherd, a free African American railroad worker.4 Townspeople and militia soon surrounded the raiders, and Brown retreated into the armory’s brick fire-engine house with his remaining men and hostages.4,8 On the morning of October 18, U.S. Marines under Colonel Robert E. Lee broke in and captured him.4 Brown was tried for murder, conspiring with enslaved people to rebel, and treason against Virginia, convicted after a five-day trial, and hanged on December 2, 1859.4,11 The Yawp writes that his execution “made him a martyr in abolitionist circles and a confirmed traitor in southern crowds.”10

The Civil War. When Virginia passed its ordinance of secession in April 1861, the U.S. officer guarding the armory set fire to it and retreated, and Confederate forces later carried off its machinery and burned more buildings.2 By 1862 the armory lay in ruins, and only about 100 of the town’s prewar population of 3,000 remained.5 In September 1862, as Lee invaded Maryland, Stonewall Jackson surrounded the Union garrison on the heights around the town. On September 15 the Union commanders surrendered.5 The Park Service counts more than 12,700 Union troops captured, the largest single capture of Federal forces in the war, though another Park Service page describes a garrison of 12,500.5,1

Storer College. After the war, the Freewill Baptists of New England, the Freedmen’s Bureau, and the Maine businessman John Storer founded a school for the many formerly enslaved people of the Shenandoah Valley.6 Park Service pages date its start differently: one says the school began in 1865, another that the Freewill Baptists opened it in 1867.6,7 Teachers faced mob violence and stoning, but for twenty-five years Storer was the only school in West Virginia where a Black student could get an education beyond the primary level.6,7 It closed in 1955, after the state ended its funding, having taught more than 7,000 students.6

The Niagara Movement. From August 15 to 19, 1906, W. E. B. Du Bois’s Niagara Movement held its first public meeting in the United States at Storer College. Its members made a barefoot dawn pilgrimage to John Brown’s Fort.7 The meeting ended with Du Bois’s “Address to the Country.” The movement met yearly in other cities afterward. The Library of Congress says it disbanded in 1910, while the Park Service says it lasted until 1911, when almost all its members became the backbone of the new NAACP.7,12

John Brown’s Fort. The engine house where Brown made his last stand had a strange afterlife. It was taken apart and shown in Chicago in 1891, rebuilt on a farm outside town in 1895, moved to the Storer campus in 1909, and returned to Lower Town by the Park Service in 1968, about 150 feet from its original site.8
