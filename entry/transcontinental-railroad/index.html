The Transcontinental Railroad

Rails across the continent, 1863–1869

1863 – May 10, 1869 · Omaha, Nebraska, to Sacramento, California; joined at Promontory Summit, Utah

The first transcontinental railroad joined the eastern rail network to the Pacific coast on May 10, 1869, shortening a journey of several months to about one week.1 It was built by two companies under the Pacific Railway Act of 1862. The Union Pacific built west from Omaha and the Central Pacific built east from Sacramento, both paid with federal bonds and land.1,2 Irish, German, and Italian immigrants laid track on the Union Pacific, and some 10,000 Chinese workers did much of the hardest work on the Central Pacific, for lower pay and with their deaths left uncounted.1,6,7 The rails crossed the homelands of at least fifteen Native nations and opened the Plains to settlers, hide hunters, and soldiers.8,2

Why it matters

The transcontinental railroad tied the nation together economically and made the settlement of the West possible. It was built by immigrant labor and enormous federal subsidies, and at great cost to the Native nations whose lands it crossed.1,2,8

Historical background

Congress argued through the nineteenth century about whether the federal government should pay for “internal improvements” such as canals and railroads, and a railroad across the continent had long been a dream.1 In the 1850s it commissioned surveys of the possible routes. Private companies refused to take on the job without federal help, and fights over where the line should run stalled the project.1 The American Yawp notes that government aid to railroads had been part of the Republican Party platform since 1856.2

Causes and contributing factors

On July 1, 1862, Congress passed the Pacific Railway Act. It provided government bonds and large grants of land for rights of way to build a railroad and telegraph line from the Missouri River to the Pacific, and it secured the line’s use for the government.1 The act authorized two companies, the Union Pacific and the Central Pacific.1 The American Yawp says the bonds ranged from $16,000 to $48,000 per mile of track. Railroad companies received more than 175 million acres of public land between 1850 and 1871, more than the area of Texas.2 The National Archives counts 174 million acres for the four transcontinental lines eventually authorized.1 Because subsidies were paid by the mile, the two companies kept grading past each other for about 250 miles between Echo, Utah, and Wells, Nevada, until Congress forced them to agree on a meeting point.4

What happened

The Central Pacific. The company was chartered in 1862 to build the western section. Its first two years went slowly, held up by long supply lines and a shortage of workers, who often quit when news of a gold strike arrived.6 Its manager Charles Crocker turned to Chinese workers in 1864, against the doubts of some managers who feared that white workers would refuse to work with them.6 After small first crews proved themselves, the company recruited thousands more, many directly from Guangdong province in southern China.7,6 By July 1865 the Chinese workforce was nearly 4,000, and in February 1867 about 8,000 Chinese were working on tunnels and 3,000 laying track, roughly ninety percent of the workforce, according to the Stanford project. Historians estimate that at any one time 10,000 to 15,000 Chinese were working on the line.7 The National Archives and the National Park Service both put the total at over 10,000.1,6 Their jobs ranged from grading and laying track to blasting, tunneling, cooking, carpentry, and masonry. Almost all of it was done by hand.7,6

The Sierra Nevada was the hardest part. Chinese crews cut Bloomer Cut near Auburn, hacked a three-mile ledge into the slope at Cape Horn, and in 1865 began fifteen tunnels, the longest of them the Summit Tunnel through solid granite.7 They worked through two of the harshest winters on record, with forty-four storms between November 1866 and May 1867. Avalanches swept away camps, and one killed twenty workers.7 The company built 37 miles of snow sheds in 1868 and 1869.7 Historians disagree about the story that workers were lowered over cliffs in baskets to set explosives at Cape Horn. Some say it began in a 1927 publicity article. The Stanford project says recent research has found earlier accounts of workers using baskets and ropes in the Sierra.7

The Union Pacific. From 1863 the Union Pacific built west from Omaha with more than 8,000 Irish, German, and Italian immigrants, according to the Archives.1 The National Park Service says between 8,000 and 10,000 of the workers were Irish. They were often segregated, housed in cramped railroad cars, and paid less than native-born workers.9 Historians have found evidence that African American workers also helped build the line, although few records of them survive.10 Both companies, the Archives notes, faced severe weather, punishing terrain, and conflict with American Indians whose ancestral lands the tracks crossed.1

The race to Promontory. On April 28, 1869, the bet of $10,000 between Crocker and Thomas Durant of the Union Pacific produced a record. Eight Irish rail handlers and a large crew of Chinese workers laid 10 miles and 56 feet of track in a single day.7,6 The sources differ on the Chinese crew size. The National Park Service says no fewer than 400 Chinese laborers worked behind the eight track layers, while the Stanford project says about 5,000 workers, mostly Chinese, took part. The railroad recorded the names of the eight Irish workers. None of the Chinese workers' names was recorded.6,7

The last spike. On May 10, 1869, the two lines met at Promontory Summit. The National Park Service says eight Chinese men moved the final rail into position.6 A polished laurel tie and four ceremonial spikes were presented, among them a gold spike cast by the San Francisco contractor David Hewes. Leland Stanford swung at the final spike and hit the tie, and Durant, nursing a hangover, barely swung at all. A regular rail worker drove the last iron spike, and the telegrapher sent a single word: “D-O-N-E.” It was 12:47 p.m.5 Estimates of the crowd range from 300 to 1,500 in one account from the park and from 800 to 3,000 in another.4,11

Immediate consequences

The journey across the country shrank from months to about a week.1 From May to December 1869 Promontory was the meeting point of the two lines, a rowdy town of gambling and saloons, until the junction moved to Ogden in 1870.4 Many Chinese workers stayed with the railroad or took jobs on other lines. They helped build and maintain at least 70 others, and some also built the Canadian transcontinental line.7 Others went back to China, or took up farm work, mining, logging, levee building, and city jobs in Chinatowns.7 President Grant, a strong supporter of the transcontinental railroads, oversaw the completion in the first year of his presidency.3

Long-term significance

No other business drew such huge capital or such lavish government subsidies. The business historian Alfred Chandler called the railroads the “first modern business enterprise.” By 1900 the country had 190,000 miles of track, up from 9,000 in 1850.2 Railroads made ranching and the cattle drives possible and then ended them. They made Chicago the great hub of the Midwest. By 1880 about 400,000 men, nearly 2.5 percent of the workforce, worked in the industry.2 A congressional critic of the subsidies said in the 1870s that “if there be profit, the corporations may take it; if there be loss, the Government must bear it.”2 The railroads defined much of the Gilded Age, including its fortunes, its corruption, and its labor conflict.2

For Native nations the cost was heavy. The railroad crossed the homelands of at least fifteen of them, and in some cases construction went ahead before any formal agreement was in place. The line brought settlers and development, which limited access to hunting grounds, water, and wildlife.8 Federal policy encouraged widespread bison hunting, and the American Yawp says the bison fell from more than ten million at midcentury to only a few hundred by the early 1880s.8,2

Chinese workers' history was largely written out of the story. Twentieth-century accounts often left them out or played down their role. The Central Pacific kept no record of the deaths. Estimates run from 50 to over 1,000, and the National Park Service says over a thousand.7,6 A newspaper reported that the Chinese Six Companies organized a search for the remains of 300 along the line to send them home to China for burial.7 On June 24, 1867, about 3,000 Chinese workers between Cisco and Truckee struck for higher pay, a ten-hour day, and shorter tunnel shifts. The Park Service calls it the largest organized labor strike in the United States up to then.6,7 After about a week the company cut off their food and Crocker confronted them with a sheriff and a posse of deputized white men, and the strike ended. The company appears to have quietly raised pay for skilled workers afterward.7 Chinese workers were paid less than white workers, and had to pay for their own food. The Park Service puts the gap at about 30 percent, and the Stanford project says they were paid less than half as much per month.6,7 Within a decade nativist violence and discriminatory laws followed, the Page Act of 1875 and the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, which was made permanent in 1902 and repealed only in 1943.6 See also the Chinese Exclusion Act.
