The Continental Congress

The colonies’ revolutionary assembly, and the first national government

September 5, 1774 – March 1, 1781 · Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, and other towns during the war

The Continental Congress began as a meeting of colonial delegates coordinating protest against British policy and became, almost by accident, the government of a new nation.1 The First Continental Congress, which met in Philadelphia on September 5, 1774, issued a declaration of colonial rights and organized a boycott of British trade through the Continental Association.1,2 The Second Continental Congress, which met in May 1775 after fighting had begun, created the Continental Army under George Washington, voted for independence on July 2, 1776, adopted the Declaration of Independence, won French support, and wrote the Articles of Confederation.1,4,5

Why it matters

The Continental Congress turned thirteen separate colonies into a single political community that could declare independence, raise an army, make foreign alliances, and win a war. Its weaknesses, especially its dependence on the states for money, also taught the lessons that shaped the Constitution.1,5

Origins

After the Boston Tea Party, Parliament’s Coercive Acts of 1774 closed Boston’s port and put Massachusetts under royal control. Instead of isolating Massachusetts, they led Committees of Correspondence across the colonies to agree to send delegates to a Continental Congress “to coordinate an intercolonial response.”1 The First Continental Congress convened on September 5, 1774, with delegates from every colony but Georgia.1 Over six weeks it issued a declaration of rights that repeated arguments colonists had made since 1765: that they kept all the rights of Britons, including being taxed only by their own representatives and trial by jury.1

Powers and limits

Its first great act was the Continental Association of October 20, 1774, a continental agreement not to import, consume, or export British goods and to “wholly discontinue the slave trade.”1,2 It recommended that every county, city, and town choose a committee to watch for violators and publish their names so they would be “universally condemned as the enemies of American liberty.” The American Yawp calls it “perhaps the most radical document of the period,” because it gave ordinary colonists unprecedented local political power.1

In wartime the Second Congress acted as a national government. It adopted the Massachusetts militia as a Continental Army, named George Washington commander in chief, and sent diplomats abroad, but without the power to tax it struggled to pay for the war.1,5 It met in the Assembly Room of the Pennsylvania State House, now Independence Hall, though British forces occupied Philadelphia in 1777.3,1

How it changed over time

War and reconciliation. When the Second Continental Congress met in May 1775, war had already begun at Lexington and Concord.1 Radical delegates such as John Adams, Samuel Adams, and Richard Henry Lee wanted to support the Massachusetts militia, while moderates from the Middle Colonies feared that would mean war. The compromise was to create a Continental Army under Washington, explain the decision in a Declaration of the Causes and Necessity of Taking Up Arms, and at the same time send the king the Olive Branch Petition.1 The Declaration of Causes proclaimed: “Our cause is just. Our union is perfect.”4 King George III answered with a proclamation for suppressing rebellion.1

Independence. In May 1776 Congress urged the colonies to form new governments. On June 7 Richard Henry Lee moved that “these United Colonies are, and of right ought to be, Free and Independent States.” On July 2 the resolution passed 12–0, with New York abstaining, and Congress then approved Jefferson’s Declaration of Independence.1,3

War government. In June 1776 Congress also appointed a committee to plan a confederation; it adopted the Articles of Confederation in November 1777.5 Benjamin Franklin, sent to Paris, won a Treaty of Amity and Commerce with France after the American victory at Saratoga, signed on February 6, 1778; the Yawp writes that it “effectively turned a colonial rebellion into a global war.”1

Into the Confederation. When Maryland ratified the Articles on March 1, 1781, the Continental Congress became the Congress of the Confederation, which governed until the Constitution took effect in 1789.5
