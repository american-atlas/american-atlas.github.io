The End of the Cold War

The fall of communism in Eastern Europe and the collapse of the Soviet Union, 1989–1991

1989 – December 25, 1991 · Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union

Between 1989 and 1991, the communist regimes of Eastern Europe collapsed one after another, the Berlin Wall fell, and the Soviet Union itself dissolved. Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev’s reforms, perestroika (restructuring) and glasnost (openness), and his decision not to use force to prop up allied regimes made peaceful change possible. Presidents Ronald Reagan and George H. W. Bush combined pressure with negotiation. On December 25, 1991, Gorbachev resigned and the Soviet flag came down over the Kremlin, leaving the United States the world’s only superpower.

Why it matters

The peaceful end of the Cold War closed a four-decade conflict that had shaped American life and lifted the threat of superpower nuclear war.

Historical background

By the 1980s the Soviet economy was stagnating, and a costly war in Afghanistan drained its resources. Reagan built up American forces and denounced the Soviet Union, while backing anticommunist movements worldwide.

Causes and contributing factors

Gorbachev, who came to power in 1985, believed the Soviet system needed reform and sought to end the ruinous arms race. He met Reagan at Geneva in 1985 and Reykjavik in 1986, and the two signed the Intermediate Nuclear Forces Treaty in 1987. Gorbachev also abandoned the policy of using Soviet troops to support communist governments in Eastern Europe. Historians emphasize that the most significant causes of the collapse lay within the Soviet empire itself.

What happened

In Poland, round-table talks with the banned union Solidarity led to elections on June 4, 1989, which Solidarity won overwhelmingly; by August the country had Eastern Europe’s first non-communist prime minister. Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Romania, Bulgaria, and East Germany followed. On November 9, 1989, jubilant crowds brought down the Berlin Wall. Gorbachev accepted German reunification, and a reunited Germany joined NATO. Inside the Soviet Union, reforms unraveled the system. A failed hard-line coup in August 1991 fatally weakened Gorbachev; republics declared independence, and in December the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus formed the Commonwealth of Independent States.

Immediate consequences

On December 25, 1991, Gorbachev resigned as president of the Soviet Union, and the Soviet flag was lowered over the Kremlin for the last time, replaced by the Russian tricolor. The Bush administration focused on stability and security in Russia and the other former Soviet states.

Long-term significance

The end of the Cold War left the United States as the sole superpower and seemed to many to signal the global triumph of democracy and capitalism. It reshaped American defense priorities, alliances, and politics for the decades that followed.
