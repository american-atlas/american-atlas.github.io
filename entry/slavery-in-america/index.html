Slavery in America

The institution that shaped the nation’s economy, politics, and conflicts for nearly 250 years

1619–1865

Slavery, the ownership of human beings as property, inherited by their children and enforced by law and violence, was legal in every one of the thirteen colonies and lasted in the United States until 1865.2,12 Enslaved Africans and their descendants grew the tobacco, rice, and above all the cotton that made the South rich and drove the nation’s exports.2,4 By 1860 the census counted 3,953,760 enslaved people, about one in eight Americans.9 The struggle over slavery shaped the Constitution, split the nation in the 1850s, and ended only with the Civil War and the Thirteenth Amendment.10,5,12

Why it matters

Slavery was not a regional exception to American history but one of its central forces. It shaped the Constitution, built much of the nation’s wealth and its leading export, drove westward expansion and party politics, and its abolition required the nation’s bloodiest war.10,4,5 Historians also link the slave trade to the birth of the modern idea of race as an inherited physical difference used to justify oppression.1

Definition

Chattel slavery treated enslaved people as movable property that could be bought, sold, mortgaged, and inherited. In the English colonies and the United States, it became tied to race. A Virginia law of 1662 declared that a child inherited the “condition” of its mother, so that every child born to an enslaved woman was enslaved for life, whatever the status of the father, and other colonies soon passed similar laws.1 By the early 1700s, colonial slave codes had made slavery lifelong, hereditary, and racial: conversion to Christianity did not bring freedom, and skin color became the marker of a sharp legal line between Black and white.1,2

Historical context

Origins. Slavery in the Americas grew out of a much older Atlantic trade. Beginning in the 1440s, Portuguese sailors carried enslaved Africans to Portugal, and as Europeans colonized the Americas, with plenty of land and few workers, Portuguese, Dutch, and English ships became the conduits for Africans forced across the ocean.1 Colonists also enslaved Native Americans, above all as prisoners of war: historians estimate that between 24,000 and 51,000 Native people were forced into slavery in the southern colonies between 1670 and 1715.1 In late August 1619 the English privateer White Lion landed at Point Comfort, Virginia, and traded “20. and odd Negroes” for food. They had been seized from a Portuguese slave ship bound for Mexico and had most likely been captured in the African kingdom of Ndongo, in present-day Angola.6 They were the first documented Africans in English Virginia. The colony’s secretary, John Rolfe, called the ship a “Dutch man of Warr” because it sailed under Dutch papers, which is why many accounts, including the American Yawp, speak of Dutch traders.6,4 Their legal status was at first unsettled: the word “servant” appears in the early records because English and Virginia law had not yet established race-based slavery. Some, like Anthony and Mary Johnson, won their freedom and owned land on Virginia’s Eastern Shore.6

Making slavery racial. Over the seventeenth century that openness closed. A 1643 Virginia law made African women taxable as field workers, while white women were not; the 1662 law made slavery hereditary through the mother; and in 1705 the House of Burgesses passed its first comprehensive slave code.1,2 Under Virginia law, enslavers could not be convicted of murder for killing an enslaved person, while any Black Virginian who struck a white colonist would be severely whipped.2 After Bacon’s Rebellion in 1676, as more Africans became available through the Atlantic trade, Chesapeake planters turned on a large scale from indentured servants to enslaved labor.1 Yet the modern idea of race was still being made. A slave-ship captain, Thomas Phillips, wrote in 1694 that he could not see “any intrinsic value in one color more than another”; for him, profit was justification enough.1

The Middle Passage. Between 1517 and 1867 about 12.5 million Africans were forced onto ships for the Middle Passage, and about 10.7 million survived the voyage. About 40 percent went to Brazil and about 35 percent to the non-Spanish Caribbean, more than 20 percent to Spanish colonies, and only about 3.5 percent reached British North America and the United States, which lay north of the sugar-growing regions at the heart of the Atlantic economy.7 By the most widely used estimate, from the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database, about 388,000 Africans landed alive in North America; the American Yawp gives a figure of about 450,000.7,8,1 More than half came through Charleston, South Carolina.7 Death rates aboard ship averaged above 20 percent in the early decades of the trade and fell to about 5 percent by its last decade.7 Olaudah Equiano and the ship’s surgeon Alexander Falconbridge described captives chained in the hold amid filth and disease.1 Most of the North American share of the trade was carried on by Rhode Island merchants, who traded rum for captives; Newport alone had at least 150 ships in the trade by 1740.7,2 Because enslaved women in North America bore more children than those in the Caribbean or South America, the enslaved population there grew by natural increase, and generations born in America soon outnumbered those born in Africa.1,4

Slavery in the colonies. By 1750 slavery was legal in every North American colony, but it took different forms.2 In Virginia about one hundred thousand enslaved people, at least 40 percent of the colony’s population, grew tobacco, mostly in closely supervised gangs from dawn to dusk.2 South Carolina, whose founding constitution legalized slavery from the start, was by 1750 the only mainland colony with an enslaved majority. Its rice economy depended on the skill of West Africans who already knew how to grow rice, and under the “task system” some enslaved people had time to grow their own crops and preserve African practices in the Gullah and Geechee languages.2 In the North, enslaved people worked on farms and in the ports as domestic servants and in the maritime trades; in Massachusetts only about 2 percent of the population was enslaved in the 1760s, but the slave trade was central to New England’s economy.2

Revolution and Constitution. The Revolution’s language of liberty shook the institution. In 1775 Virginia’s royal governor, Lord Dunmore, offered freedom to enslaved people who joined the British, the first mass emancipation in American history, and thousands fled to British lines during the war.3 Quakers in Pennsylvania had already turned against slavery, and after the war most Northern states passed gradual emancipation laws.2,3 New York’s law of 1799 freed people so slowly that the last enslaved New Yorkers were not freed until July 4, 1827.13 But the Constitution of 1787 protected slavery. The framers counted three-fifths of “all other persons,” meaning enslaved people, toward each state’s representation in Congress, which gave Southern white men extra power.10,5 In what George Mason called “a bargain,” delegates from South Carolina and Georgia won a guarantee that Congress would not interfere with the slave trade for twenty years, and the Constitution also allowed Congress to pass fugitive slave laws.10,5 Congress banned the import of enslaved people from January 1, 1808. Many Virginia planters supported the ban, not because they opposed slavery, but because their own enslaved populations were growing and they could sell people to the new cotton lands.7

The Cotton Kingdom. In the nineteenth century, cotton made slavery more profitable than ever. Eli Whitney’s cotton gin (1794), a new strain of cotton developed in Mississippi in 1833, steamboats, and the land opened by Indian removal turned the Southwest into a Cotton Belt.4 By 1835 the five main cotton states produced more than five hundred million pounds a year, nearly 55 percent of all American exports; the two billion pounds grown in 1860 made up more than 60 percent.4 The enslaved population of the South grew from 654,121 in 1790 to more than 1.1 million in 1810 and to nearly four million by 1860.4 Prices rose with cotton: a “prime field hand” who sold for about $700 in 1838 cost around $1,600 by 1850, and planters borrowed money using enslaved people as collateral.4 Output per worker in Mississippi rose 600 percent between 1820 and 1860, extracted through heavier workloads, longer hours, and harsher punishment.4 “To sell cotton in order to buy negroes—to make more cotton to buy more negroes,” wrote one observer in 1834, was “the aim and direct tendency of all the operations of the thorough going cotton planter.”4

The internal slave trade. To supply the cotton frontier, more than one million enslaved men, women, and children were moved from the Upper South to the Deep South between 1790 and 1860, many of them sold “downriver.”7,4 The trade tore families apart: during the cotton boom, between one-fifth and one-third of all marriages between enslaved people were broken up by sale or forced migration.4 Yet by the Civil War about two-thirds of enslaved people lived in households with a mother, a father, and children, and families kept names, memories, and traditions alive across plantations.4 Enslaved women faced the constant threat of sexual violence, which the law and white society rarely treated as a crime. In Missouri in 1855, a nineteen-year-old woman named Celia, raped repeatedly by her enslaver for five years, killed him and was hanged.4

Resistance. Enslaved people resisted every day by breaking tools, running wagons off the road, delaying work, and running away.4 Many built their own forms of Christianity, blending in African traditions, even as anti-literacy laws kept most of them from reading the Bible for themselves.4 Some rose in arms. In the Stono Rebellion of September 1739, about eighty enslaved people in South Carolina marched toward Spanish Florida, which offered freedom to people who escaped the English, under a banner reading “Liberty!”, burning plantations and killing at least twenty white settlers before the militia defeated them.2 New York City saw a revolt in 1712 and a feared conspiracy in 1741, after which thirty-two Black men and five poor white men were executed.2 On August 22, 1831, Nat Turner led the deadliest slave rebellion in the antebellum South, in Southampton County, Virginia, killing fifty-seven white men, women, and children; in the terror that followed, white mobs killed hundreds of enslaved people, most of them unconnected to the revolt, and states passed harsher laws.4 Many more people simply escaped. These freedom seekers, often helped by networks known as the Underground Railroad, went to the North, Canada, Mexico, Spanish Florida, and beyond. They were helped by enslaved and free Black people, American Indians, and white allies of many faiths.11 Escaped people such as Frederick Douglass and Sojourner Truth became leading voices of the abolitionist movement, and Harriet Tubman returned again and again to guide others out.5,13,11

Crisis and abolition. As the nation expanded, every new territory raised the question of slavery’s spread, from the Missouri Compromise to the Compromise of 1850.5 The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 forced Northern citizens to help capture escaped people and let federal commissioners decide their fate without a jury. A commissioner was paid $10 if he ruled that a person was enslaved but only $5 if he set the person free.5 In 1854 the federal government spent over $40,000 to return a single man, Anthony Burns, from Boston to slavery in Virginia.5 The Dred Scott decision of 1857 declared that Black Americans could never be citizens.5 When Abraham Lincoln, whose party opposed slavery’s expansion, won the presidency in 1860, Southern states began to secede, and South Carolina’s declaration of secession complained above all that the North had failed to enforce the Fugitive Slave Act.5 During the war, many enslaved people escaped to the lines of the Union army, the Emancipation Proclamation of January 1, 1863, declared enslaved people in the rebelling states free, and the Thirteenth Amendment, passed by Congress on January 31, 1865, and ratified on December 6, 1865, abolished slavery throughout the United States.11,14,12

Examples

  • Virginia’s tobacco plantations, which turned to enslaved labor on a large scale in the late 1600s and held about one hundred thousand enslaved people by 1750.1,2
  • South Carolina’s rice coast, the only mainland colony with an enslaved majority by 1750, where the Stono Rebellion broke out in 1739.2
  • New York’s gradual emancipation, under which Sojourner Truth escaped in 1826 and the last enslaved New Yorkers were freed only on July 4, 1827.13
  • The slave markets of New Orleans, such as the rotunda of the St. Louis Hotel, where Harriet Beecher Stowe set the sale of Uncle Tom in Uncle Tom’s Cabin.4
  • The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, which required free states to help capture people who had escaped and led to the return of Anthony Burns from Boston in 1854.5
  • The slaveholding presidents of the early republic, including Washington, Jefferson, Madison, and Jackson.
