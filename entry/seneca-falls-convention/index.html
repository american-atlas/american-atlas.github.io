The Seneca Falls Convention

The first women’s rights convention, July 1848

July 19–20, 1848 · Wesleyan Chapel, Seneca Falls, New York

On July 19 and 20, 1848, about three hundred women and men met at the Wesleyan Chapel in Seneca Falls, New York, for the first women’s rights convention in the United States.1 Organized by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott, it adopted a Declaration of Sentiments modeled on the Declaration of Independence. It proclaimed that “all men and women are created equal” and listed the wrongs done to women, beginning with the denial of the vote.2,4 One hundred people signed it, 68 women and 32 men. Frederick Douglass was among those who attended.1,3 The demand for the vote was the most controversial of its resolutions, and it launched the movement that won the Nineteenth Amendment seventy-two years later.6,5,8

Why it matters

The Seneca Falls Convention turned scattered complaints into an organized demand for women’s equality and gave the movement its founding document.4,6

Historical background

Women in the 1840s could not vote, and married women had few property rights. The Declaration of Sentiments complained that the law made a married woman “civilly dead.”4,2 Many of the first women’s rights advocates came to the cause through the fight against slavery. Angelina and Sarah Grimké, Lucretia Mott, Sojourner Truth, Elizabeth Cady Stanton, and Susan B. Anthony all began as abolitionists.6 When the Grimké sisters were attacked for speaking in public against slavery, they concluded that they would have to fight for women’s rights in order to fight for the rights of the enslaved.6

Lucretia Mott was a Philadelphia Quaker, raised in a faith that stressed the equality of all people under God, and a founder of the Philadelphia Female Anti-Slavery Society in 1833.5 In 1840 she was a delegate to the World Anti-Slavery Convention in London. The organizers refused to seat the women delegates.6,5 Elizabeth Cady Stanton, on her honeymoon with the abolitionist Henry Stanton, who was also a delegate, met Mott at a tea party in London. Angry at the women’s exclusion, the two promised each other to hold a women’s rights convention when they returned home.4,5,6

Causes and contributing factors

Eight years passed. In 1848 the vow became a reality, when Stanton and Mott organized a convention at Seneca Falls, in upstate New York.4,5 Stanton, working with the M’Clintock family, drafted the document that the meeting would consider.1,4 She deliberately modeled it on the Declaration of Independence to make plain the connection between women’s liberty and the language of the nation’s founding.6

What happened

About three hundred people came to the Wesleyan Chapel on July 19 and 20, 1848, including Mott and Frederick Douglass, the formerly enslaved abolitionist and editor.1,7 The Declaration of Sentiments began: “When, in the course of human events, it becomes necessary for one portion of the family of man to assume among the people of the earth a position different from that which they have hitherto occupied…” Then it changed the most famous line in American history: “We hold these truths to be self-evident; that all men and women are created equal.”2

In place of the Declaration of Independence’s charges against King George, it listed charges against men: “The history of mankind is a history of repeated injuries and usurpations on the part of man toward woman, having in direct object the establishment of an absolute tyranny over her.”2 The first was that man “has never permitted her to exercise her inalienable right to the elective franchise.” Others were that married women were bound to promise obedience, that divorce and custody laws ignored women’s happiness, that single women who owned property were taxed without representation, that women were shut out of profitable work, the professions, and colleges, and that men had claimed the right “to assign for her a sphere of action, when that belongs to her conscience and her God.”2 The sources count these grievances differently. The American Yawp counts fifteen grievances and eleven resolutions, while the National Women’s History Museum describes eighteen grievances.6,4,5

The resolutions demanded property rights, access to the professions, and, most controversially, the vote.6 On the morning of July 20, sixty-eight women signed the Declaration under the words “Firmly relying upon the final triumph of the Right and the True, we do this day affix our signatures to this declaration.” Thirty-two men also signed.3,1 The signers expected trouble: “we anticipate no small amount of misconception, misrepresentation, and ridicule; but we shall use every instrumentality within our power to effect our object.”2

Immediate consequences

Seneca Falls was the first of many such meetings, held almost entirely in the Northern states.6 Douglass went on to support women’s suffrage actively.7 That same year Stanton circulated petitions in support of New York’s Married Women’s Property Act, which let married women keep their own property.4 The movement grew slowly, however. Few states reformed married women’s property laws before the Civil War, and none was ready to give women the vote.6 Stanton soon found a partner in Susan B. Anthony, whom she met in 1851, and together they led the movement for half a century.4

Long-term significance

The Declaration of Sentiments set out the agenda of the American women’s rights movement for decades, and the demand for the vote, the most radical part of it, launched the long fight for suffrage.5,4 Mott later recalled that she had grown up “so thoroughly imbued with women’s rights that it was the most important question” of her life.5 Women won a constitutional guarantee of the vote only with the Nineteenth Amendment, ratified on August 18, 1920. Few of the movement’s early supporters lived to see it.8 The convention’s site is preserved today as Women’s Rights National Historical Park.1
