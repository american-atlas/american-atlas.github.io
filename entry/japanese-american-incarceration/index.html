Japanese American Incarceration

The forced removal and imprisonment of Japanese Americans during World War II

February 19, 1942 · West Coast exclusion zones; ten War Relocation Centers in California, Arizona, Utah, Idaho, Wyoming, Colorado, and Arkansas

After Japan attacked Pearl Harbor, the United States government forced about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry from their homes on the West Coast and held them in camps for most of World War II.1,2 Nearly 70,000 of them were American citizens. None was charged with a crime, and none could appeal.1 The removal rested on Executive Order 9066, signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on February 19, 1942, and the Supreme Court upheld it in Korematsu v. United States (1944).1,3 In 1988 Congress acknowledged that the incarceration was a “grave injustice” caused by “racial prejudice, wartime hysteria, and a failure of political leadership.” It apologized and paid $20,000 to each survivor.4,1

Why it matters

The incarceration showed how quickly fear and racism could override constitutional rights in wartime, and the decades-long fight for redress showed how a democracy can admit a wrong.1,4

Historical background

Between 1861 and 1940 about 275,000 Japanese immigrants came to Hawaii and the mainland United States, most of them between 1898 and 1924, when new quotas ended Asian immigration.1 On the West Coast they farmed marginal land, fished, and ran small businesses. In 1940 Japanese Americans controlled less than 4 percent of California’s farmland but produced more than 10 percent of the value of its farm output.1 They faced discrimination at every turn. Real estate agents kept them in ethnic enclaves, a 1913 California law barred immigrants who could not become citizens from owning land, and in 1922 the Supreme Court ruled in Ozawa v. United States that Japanese immigrants could be denied citizenship.1 The immigrant generation, the Issei, therefore remained aliens, while their American-born children, the Nisei, were citizens by birth.1

Causes and contributing factors

Japan’s attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, set off fears for national security, especially on the West Coast. The National Archives notes that these fears combined with economic competition, distrust of cultural separateness, and long-standing anti-Asian racism.1 Lobbyists from Western states, many of them representing competing farm interests or nativist groups, pressed Congress and the president to remove all people of Japanese descent, Issei and Nisei alike.1 Justice Department officials raised constitutional and ethical objections, so the army carried out the plan instead.1 German and Italian nationals were also interned, more than ten thousand of them, but individually and after reviews. People of Japanese descent were removed as a group.8

On February 19, 1942, Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066. It authorized the secretary of war and military commanders to “prescribe military areas … from which any or all persons may be excluded.” It named no ethnic group.1 Lieutenant General John L. DeWitt of the Western Defense Command then imposed curfews on Japanese Americans only.1 On March 21 Congress made violating the military orders a crime punishable by up to a year in prison.1

What happened

DeWitt first asked Japanese Americans to leave the coastal zones voluntarily. Only about 7 percent complied, and officials in inland states refused to accept Japanese Americans except under guard.1,6 On March 29, 1942, he ordered the forced removal of the remaining West Coast residents of Japanese ancestry on 48 hours’ notice.1 The speed of the removal forced many families to sell homes, farms, and businesses in a hurry.1 Over the next six months about 122,000 men, women, and children were moved first to temporary “assembly centers” and then to ten fenced and guarded “relocation centers” in remote parts of six Western states and Arkansas: Manzanar and Tule Lake in California, Poston and Gila River in Arizona, Topaz in Utah, Minidoka in Idaho, Heart Mountain in Wyoming, Granada in Colorado, and Jerome and Rohwer in Arkansas.1,9 The federal courts’ educational page counts some 26 sites in all.2

A civilian agency, the War Relocation Authority (WRA), created on March 18, 1942, ran the camps. Its first director, Milton Eisenhower, had hoped for voluntary resettlement, but when Western governors refused to accept Japanese Americans except under guard, he reluctantly planned a network of permanent camps. Dillon Myer replaced him in June 1942.6

In early 1943 the government gave every adult in the camps a “loyalty questionnaire.” Question 27 asked men whether they would serve in combat wherever ordered, and question 28 asked everyone to swear allegiance to the United States and renounce any allegiance to Japan’s emperor.7 Many resented being asked to renounce an allegiance they had never held, and Issei, barred from American citizenship, feared being left stateless.7 Those judged “disloyal” were segregated at Tule Lake, where guards beat inmates and a strike in November 1943 was distorted by West Coast newspapers into a “riot.”6

Immediate consequences

Some Japanese Americans fought the orders in court. Fred Korematsu, a young shipyard welder from the Oakland area, refused to report, changed his name and appearance, and was arrested on May 30, 1942. “I was an American citizen,” he later said, “and I had as many rights as anyone else.”3 On December 18, 1944, the Supreme Court upheld his conviction 6 to 3.3,2 Justice Hugo Black wrote that racial restrictions were “immediately suspect” but that “pressing public necessity” could justify them. Korematsu, he said, was excluded “because we are at war with the Japanese Empire.”2,3 Justice Frank Murphy called the order the “legalization of racism.” Justice Robert Jackson likened the principle of military necessity the Court had accepted to “a loaded weapon.”3,2 The same day the Court decided a companion case, the Endo case, in the prisoner’s favor, but it sidestepped the constitutional question. The day before, the War Department had announced that the exclusion would end on January 2, 1945.3,6

Others served. More than 30,000 Japanese Americans fought for the United States in segregated units, while their families remained behind barbed wire.1,8 The 442nd Regimental Combat Team, formed on March 23, 1943, with the 100th Infantry Battalion from Hawaii, became the most decorated unit in American military history for its size and length of service. It suffered about 800 killed or missing, and its men won more than 4,000 Purple Hearts and 29 Distinguished Service Crosses.5 In France in 1944 the 442nd rescued the “Lost Battalion” of Texas soldiers at a cost of casualties several times the number of men saved.5 “You fought the enemy abroad and prejudice at home and you won,” President Harry Truman told the unit in July 1946.5

The WRA closed every camp except Tule Lake by the end of 1945, pushing inmates to resettle. Most had lost their homes and property.6,1 A congressional commission later estimated their property losses at $1.3 billion and lost income at $2.7 billion, in 1983 dollars.1 The Japanese American Evacuation Claims Act of 1948 paid only token amounts, because it required documents that few families still had.1,4

Long-term significance

Encouraged by the civil rights movement, Japanese American activists began calling for redress in 1970.4 In 1980 Congress created the Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians, which heard more than 750 witnesses in twenty cities. Its 1983 report, Personal Justice Denied, gave factual and moral support for reparations.4 Meanwhile documents uncovered by the legal historian Peter Irons showed that officials had suppressed FBI and Federal Communications Commission reports refuting claims of Japanese American espionage.3 On November 10, 1983, Judge Marilyn Hall Patel overturned Korematsu’s conviction. His case, she wrote, “stands as a caution that in times of distress the shield of military necessity and national security must not be used to protect governmental actions from close scrutiny and accountability.”3,2 President Ronald Reagan signed the Civil Liberties Act on August 10, 1988, and the first redress checks, each with a signed presidential apology, were presented in October 1990.4 In all, 82,219 people received payments.4
