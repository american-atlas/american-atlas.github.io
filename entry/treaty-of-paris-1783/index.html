Treaty of Paris (1783)

The peace that ended the Revolutionary War and recognized American independence

September 3, 1783 · Paris, France

The Treaty of Paris, signed on September 3, 1783, ended the American Revolution. In it Great Britain recognized the thirteen states as “free sovereign and Independent States” and accepted boundaries that ran west to the Mississippi River.1,2 The American negotiators, John Adams, Benjamin Franklin and John Jay, had already agreed preliminary articles with Britain in November 1782.2 The treaty also dealt with fishing rights, debts, prisoners and the property of Loyalists, and some of its promises were broken almost at once.1,3

Why it matters

The treaty made the United States an independent nation in international law and gave it a territory reaching to the Mississippi, which opened the way west.1 Its unkept promises, on Loyalist property and on enslaved people, shaped the politics of the next decades.3

Historical circumstances

The National Archives calls the War for Independence “actually a world conflict,” involving France, Spain and the Netherlands as well as Britain and the United States.1 In 1782 Britain offered the states some autonomy inside the empire. Franklin refused: he insisted on recognition of independence and would not make a peace separate from France.2 Formal talks began on September 27, 1782, with Adams and Jay joining Franklin. Franklin even asked for Canada, though he knew the British would not agree.2

After two months of hard bargaining the two sides signed preliminary articles on November 30, 1782. They could only take effect once Britain and France also made peace; France signed its own preliminary articles on January 20, 1783, and the definitive treaties followed on September 3.2,1

Principal ideas

  • Independence (Article 1). The king “acknowledges the said United States … to be free sovereign and Independent States” and gives up all claims to govern them. The thirteen states are named one by one.1
  • Boundaries (Article 2). A line ran from Nova Scotia through the Great Lakes to the Lake of the Woods, then down the Mississippi to the thirty-first parallel and east to the Atlantic.1 The National Archives notes that these boundaries “would allow for American western expansion.”1
  • Fishing (Article 3). Americans kept the right to fish on the Grand Bank and other banks off Newfoundland.1
  • Debts (Article 4). Creditors on both sides were to meet “no lawful Impediment” in collecting prewar debts.1
  • Loyalists (Articles 5 and 6). Congress would “earnestly recommend” that the states restore confiscated Loyalist property, and there would be no further confiscations or prosecutions for the part anyone had taken in the war.1
  • Withdrawal (Article 7). British forces were to leave “without … carrying away any Negroes or other Property of the American inhabitants.”1
  • The Mississippi (Article 8). Navigation of the river was to remain “free and open” to both British subjects and American citizens.1

Broken promises

Both sides failed to keep parts of the bargain. The American Yawp writes that the treaty’s Loyalist clause was supposed to protect their property, but “the Americans … reneged on this promise,” and states kept seizing Loyalist property through the 1780s.3 Britain, for its part, did not obey Article 7: its commanders kept earlier promises of freedom and evacuated thousands of formerly enslaved people to Canada, the Caribbean and Britain. Some later settled in Nova Scotia, and some went on to Sierra Leone.3,1

A note on what the treaty promised

Sources describe the Loyalist clause differently. The State Department’s Office of the Historian says the British “promised restitution of property lost during the war by Americans loyal to the British cause,” and the American Yawp says the clause was supposed to “require the Americans to compensate Loyalists.”2,3 The treaty’s own text is weaker: Congress was only to “earnestly recommend” restitution to the state legislatures, which kept the real power to decide.1

The unfinished portrait

Benjamin West’s studio began a painting of the peace negotiators, but it was never finished, because the British negotiators would not sit for their half. The Office of the Historian calls it “a powerful symbol of the division between Great Britain and its former American colonies.”2
