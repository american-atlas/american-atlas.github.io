The Supreme Court

The nation’s highest court and interpreter of the Constitution

September 24, 1789–present · Washington, D.C. (first met in New York City)

The Supreme Court is “the highest tribunal in the Nation for all cases and controversies arising under the Constitution or the laws of the United States.”2 The Constitution created it but said little about how it should work, so Congress and the justices themselves built the federal judiciary over time.1,3 It began as a weak body whose first justices complained of its “limited stature.”1 Beginning with Chief Justice John Marshall, it became a co-equal branch that can strike down laws as unconstitutional, and its rulings have shaped every era of American history, for better and for worse.1,4

Why it matters

The Supreme Court is where Americans have brought their deepest disputes about rights and power, from slavery and segregation to the limits of government, and its interpretations of the Constitution have shaped every era of American history.1,6,9

Origins

Article III of the Constitution vests “the judicial Power of the United States” in “one supreme Court” and in such lower courts as Congress creates, and provides that judges hold office during good behavior.3 Beyond that, the Supreme Court’s own history notes, the Constitution “elaborated neither the exact powers and prerogatives of the Supreme Court nor the organization of the Judicial Branch as a whole.”1

Establishing a federal judiciary was a high priority. The first bill introduced in the Senate became the Judiciary Act of 1789, which divided the country into thirteen judicial districts and three circuits and created a Supreme Court of a Chief Justice and five associate justices.1 The Court first assembled in New York City on February 1, 1790, but Chief Justice John Jay had to postpone the meeting a day because some justices could not get there in time.1 For most of its first century the justices also had to “ride circuit,” holding court twice a year in each district, a burden they complained about along with the Court’s low prestige. Jay resigned in 1795 to become governor of New York and refused to return as chief justice in 1800.1

Powers and limits

The Court hears a few cases directly, such as disputes between states, and many more on appeal on questions of federal law.3 Its most important power, judicial review, the authority to declare laws unconstitutional, is not written in the Constitution. In Marbury v. Madison (1803) Marshall ruled that the Court could decide whether an act of Congress violated the Constitution; after 1810 the Court extended judicial review to state laws.4

The Court’s power has limits. Justices are nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate; Congress fixes the number of associate justices, currently eight, by statute; and constitutional amendments can override the Court’s interpretations.2,3 To protect its independence, judges serve during good behavior and their salaries cannot be reduced while in office.1

How it changed over time

The Marshall Court. Shortly before leaving office, President John Adams made John Marshall of Virginia the fourth chief justice. Marshall served for more than thirty-four years, and his leadership, the Court’s own history says, “was central to the development of its prominent role in American government.”1 Besides Marbury, his Court upheld broad federal power in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819).5 Not everyone accepted the Court’s authority: Jefferson complained that judicial review “would make the Judiciary a despotic branch,” and Andrew Jackson declared in 1832 that “the opinion of the judges has no more authority over Congress than the opinion of Congress has over the judges.”4,10

Slavery and segregation. In Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857), which the National Archives calls “one of the most notorious decisions ever issued,” the Court held that enslaved people were not citizens and that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories.6 After Reconstruction the Court struck down the Civil Rights Act of 1875 in 1883, and in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) it upheld a Louisiana law requiring “equal but separate” railway cars.7

The New Deal. In the 1930s the Court struck down much of Franklin Roosevelt’s program. In 1937 Roosevelt proposed adding a new justice for every sitting justice over seventy. The “court-packing scheme” failed in Congress, but, the American Yawp writes, “the chastened Court thereafter upheld social security and other pieces of New Deal legislation.”8

Civil rights. On May 17, 1954, Chief Justice Earl Warren delivered the unanimous ruling in Brown v. Board of Education, holding that state-sanctioned school segregation violated the Fourteenth Amendment and overturning Plessy’s “separate but equal” rule.9

Continuity and change

The number of justices changed six times before settling at nine in 1869. Since 1790 there have been only seventeen chief justices and 104 associate justices, who have served an average of sixteen years. On average a new justice joins the Court almost every two years. George Washington appointed the six original justices and four more; Franklin Roosevelt came close to that record with eight appointments and the elevation of Harlan Fiske Stone to chief justice.1

Equal Justice Under Law

The words “Equal Justice Under Law” are carved above the main entrance of the Supreme Court Building. The Court describes them as expressing its “ultimate responsibility.”2
