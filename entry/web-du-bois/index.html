W. E. B. Du Bois

Scholar, editor, and co-founder of the NAACP

1868 (disputed)–1963

W. E. B. Du Bois was the foremost Black intellectual of the early twentieth century and one of its most uncompromising voices for civil rights.1,4 The first African American to earn a PhD at Harvard, he pioneered the sociological study of Black life and wrote The Souls of Black Folk (1903), which challenged Booker T. Washington’s accommodation to segregation.1,7 He founded the Niagara Movement in 1905, helped found the NAACP in 1909, and for nearly a quarter century edited its magazine, The Crisis.4,5 He was also a leader of the Pan-African movement. He died in Ghana on the eve of the 1963 March on Washington.4

Why it matters

Du Bois insisted that Black Americans demand full civil and political equality without compromise, and he used scholarship, journalism, and organizing to press that demand. His idea of “double consciousness” and his warning that “the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color-line” remain among the most quoted ideas in American thought.7,1,4

Early life and context

William Edward Burghardt Du Bois was born in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, in 1868, three years after the end of the Civil War. The American Yawp and the Library of Congress give 1868. The Academy of American Poets heads its biography “1868–1963” but gives his birth date in the text as February 23, 1869.1,4,3 He was raised mainly by his mother. His early years were spent in a small farmhouse near the Housatonic River on land his mother’s family had held for more than 150 years.3,2 He graduated at the top of his high school class, and as a teenager he reported on the town’s politics as a correspondent for a Philadelphia newspaper.1,9

Du Bois went south to Fisk University in Nashville. His years in the South in the 1880s shaped his life’s work, the study of what was then called the “Negro problem,” the system of racial and economic discrimination that he predicted would be the problem of the twentieth century.1 He then studied at Harvard, did graduate work in Germany, and in 1895 became the first Black American to earn a PhD at Harvard.1 He taught at Wilberforce University in Ohio and at Atlanta University in Georgia, and his early studies of the Atlantic slave trade and of Black life in Philadelphia, published as The Philadelphia Negro, helped establish sociology as an academic discipline.1,9

Major actions and achievements

The Souls of Black Folk. In 1903 Du Bois published The Souls of Black Folk, a collection of essays that combined history, sociology, and literature.1,7 It opened with a prophecy: “The problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color-line.”7 It described the “double-consciousness” of Black Americans, “this sense of always looking at one’s self through the eyes of others,” and the feeling of being “an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings.”7 In the chapter “Of Mr. Booker T. Washington and Others,” Du Bois criticized Washington’s accommodationism, which, he said later, had “implicitly abandoned all political and social rights.”1,7 Du Bois argued instead that a “Talented Tenth” of educated Black leaders should guide the race and press for civil rights immediately and without compromise.4

The Niagara Movement and the NAACP. In July 1905 Du Bois and William Monroe Trotter gathered twenty-nine Black leaders at Niagara Falls, on the Canadian side because hotels on the American side barred Black guests. Their platform demanded free speech, a free press, manhood suffrage, and the abolition of all distinctions based on race.4 Washington’s allies, the “Tuskegee Machine,” worked to undermine the movement.4 In 1909 Du Bois joined white and Black reformers, including veterans of Niagara, in founding the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People. In 1910 he left academia to become its director of publicity and research, at first its only Black officer.4,5

The Crisis. In November 1910 the NAACP launched The Crisis, with Du Bois as editor.5 For its largely Black readers he personified the NAACP. The magazine carried news, editorials, fiction, poetry, art, and reports on the achievements of people of color around the world. In the 1920s, with a circulation of more than 100,000, it became the premier outlet for writers of the Harlem Renaissance.5,6 In 1919 he went to France to report on the Paris Peace Conference, investigate the treatment of Black soldiers, and organize a Pan-African Congress.5,6 He also wrote novels, poetry, and a historical pageant, The Star of Ethiopia, seen by nearly 30,000 people.6,5 Books such as The Souls of Black Folk, The Philadelphia Negro, and Black Reconstruction in America (1935) helped transform the study of African American history.9,3

Beliefs and ideas

Du Bois believed that Black Americans should demand full equality at once, refusing the compromises Washington accepted. In the Yawp’s words, he urged them “to concede to nothing.”1 He trusted in the power of social science to expose and defeat white supremacy, and he saw American racism as part of a worldwide color line linked to colonialism.1,7,10 His thinking changed over time. An early socialist, he joined the Communist Party in 1961 and moved to Ghana at the invitation of President Kwame Nkrumah.4

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Du Bois’s rivalry with Booker T. Washington defined Black politics for a generation. Yet he had congratulated Washington on his 1895 Atlanta speech, and he later admitted, “I admired much about him.”4,1 Four years after attacking Washington in print, the two men spoke from the same lectern at Philadelphia Divinity School.1 In the 1920s he and other NAACP leaders traded accusations with the Black nationalist Marcus Garvey, and he criticized Harlem Renaissance writers whose portraits of the “Negro underworld” he feared would harm the civil rights cause.6

In January 1934 Du Bois published an editorial in The Crisis proposing voluntary segregation as an economic strategy for Black workers and farmers. The NAACP board, led by Walter White, rejected the idea and insisted on the primacy of integration. Amid that conflict, White’s personal rivalry with Du Bois, and the magazine’s financial troubles, Du Bois resigned as editor and from the board in July 1934.8 He later returned to the NAACP as director of special research, and in 1947 he submitted to the United Nations An Appeal to the World, linking racism in the United States to colonialism abroad.10

Legacy

In his last years Du Bois moved to Ghana, where he died on August 27, 1963, on the eve of the March on Washington.4,3 The Housatonic Heritage organization calls him the father of both the modern civil rights movement and Pan-Africanism.9 His boyhood homesite in Great Barrington, purchased in 1967 and dedicated as a memorial in 1969, became a National Historic Landmark in 1979.2,9 Washington, he once joked, “died in 1915. A lot of people think I died at the same time.” He lived nearly half a century longer.1
