Neil Armstrong

Test pilot and astronaut who became the first person to walk on the Moon, on July 20, 1969

1930–2012

Neil Armstrong was an Ohio-born engineer and test pilot who flew the X-15 rocket plane to the edge of space, saved his crew during NASA’s first life-threatening emergency in orbit, and on July 20, 1969, as commander of Apollo 11, became the first person to set foot on the Moon. An estimated 650 million people watched him step onto the lunar surface. He left NASA in 1971 to teach engineering.

Why it matters

Armstrong’s first step on the Moon was the climax of the Space Race and fulfilled the national goal set by President John F. Kennedy. His career as a pilot and engineer, from the X-15 to the Moon, spanned the leap from the jet age to the space age.

Early life and context

Armstrong was born on August 5, 1930, in Wapakoneta, Ohio, and was drawn to flight and engineering. He studied aeronautical engineering at Purdue University, but his studies were interrupted by service as a naval aviator from 1949 to 1952; during the Korean War he flew 78 combat missions in F9F-2 jet fighters and earned the Air Medal. He completed his Purdue degree in 1955, later adding a master’s degree in aerospace engineering from the University of Southern California.

Major actions and achievements

In 1955 Armstrong joined the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA), NASA’s predecessor, and became a research test pilot at its flight station at Edwards, California. He flew more than 200 types of aircraft in his career and was one of only twelve pilots to fly the X-15 rocket plane, making seven flights between 1960 and 1962, reaching 207,500 feet and a speed of 3,989 miles per hour.

In 1962 NASA chose him for its second group of astronauts. On March 16, 1966, as command pilot of Gemini VIII with David Scott, he performed the first docking of two spacecraft in orbit, linking with an uncrewed Agena target. About half an hour later the joined vehicles began to tumble violently. When Armstrong undocked, the capsule spun even faster, approaching one revolution per second, fast enough for the crew to black out. He regained control by shutting down the maneuvering thrusters and using the reentry thrusters to stop the spin; a thruster had stuck firing. The mission was cut short with an emergency landing in the Pacific, but his cool handling saved both men.

On July 16, 1969, Armstrong, Michael Collins, and Edwin “Buzz” Aldrin launched on Apollo 11. On July 20 Armstrong and Aldrin descended in the lunar module Eagle, which Armstrong partly flew by hand to a landing in the Sea of Tranquility. Armstrong then climbed down the ladder and stepped onto the Moon, saying, “That’s one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind.” Armstrong spent about two hours and fourteen minutes walking on the surface, collecting samples with Aldrin, before the two rejoined Collins for the trip home.

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Armstrong’s famous words became the subject of a long debate. He said he had intended to say “one small step for a man”, the version that makes clear sense, and believed he had, but the “a” cannot be clearly heard in the recordings. After decades of audio analysis, the Smithsonian’s National Air and Space Museum quotes the words as it believes he spoke them: “That’s one small step for man.”

Legacy

After Apollo 11 Armstrong left the astronaut corps and served two years as a NASA aeronautics manager at headquarters before leaving the agency in 1971 to become a professor of engineering at the University of Cincinnati, a post he held until 1979. He later served on corporate boards and on a presidential commission on the future of the space program, and in 1986 he was vice chairman of the commission investigating the Challenger disaster. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1969 and the Congressional Space Medal of Honor in 1978. He died on August 25, 2012, at 82. In 2014 NASA renamed its flight research center at Edwards, where he had flown the X-15, the Neil A. Armstrong Flight Research Center.
