The Kansas-Nebraska Act

The law that repealed the Missouri Compromise and set Kansas bleeding, 1854

May 30, 1854

The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854, sponsored by Senator Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois, created the territories of Kansas and Nebraska and let their settlers decide whether to allow slavery.1,2 In doing so it declared “inoperative and void” the Missouri Compromise of 1820, which had banned slavery in that region for more than thirty years.1 Settlers for and against slavery rushed into Kansas, and the fighting there became known as “Bleeding Kansas.”1,4 The law broke apart the Democratic Party in the North, led opponents of slavery’s spread to found the Republican Party, and moved the country closer to civil war.1,2,3

Why it matters

The Kansas-Nebraska Act destroyed the compromise that had kept the slavery question in the territories settled for a generation. In the words of the National Archives, it “aggravated the split between North and South on the issue of slavery until reconciliation seemed virtually impossible.”1

Historical circumstances

Nebraska was the last unorganized land of the Louisiana Purchase. In 1853 it stretched from the northern edge of Texas to Canada, taking in what are now Nebraska, Wyoming, the Dakotas, Colorado, and Montana.3 Under the Missouri Compromise of 1820 slavery was banned there, because it lay north of 36°30′. Southerners wanted that ban removed.2,1

Stephen A. Douglas, a Democratic senator from Illinois who chaired the Senate committee on territories, had personal and political reasons to organize the region. He hoped a transcontinental railroad would follow a northern route that began in his home city of Chicago.2,3 In January 1854 he introduced a bill that divided the land west of Missouri into two territories, Kansas and Nebraska, and left the question of slavery to their settlers, an idea called popular sovereignty.1,2 President Franklin Pierce first urged Douglas to leave the question to the Supreme Court. Douglas refused, saying that only an explicit repeal of the Missouri Compromise would keep the South in the Union, and at a meeting with Douglas, Jefferson Davis, and other senators Pierce agreed.2 Opposition began even before the bill was fully introduced. Senator Salmon P. Chase published a reply in Northern newspapers exposing it as a plan to overturn the Missouri Compromise and open the West to slavery.3 After months of bitter debate the bill became law on May 30, 1854.1,2

Principal ideas

The heart of the act was a single long clause, repeated for each territory. It declared the slavery section of the 1820 Missouri act “inoperative and void,” because it was “inconsistent with the principle of non-intervention by Congress with slavery in the States and Territories, as recognized by the legislation of eighteen hundred and fifty, commonly called the Compromise Measures.”1 It then announced: “it being the true intent and meaning of this act not to legislate slavery into any Territory or State, nor to exclude it therefrom, but to leave the people thereof perfectly free to form and regulate their domestic institutions in their own way, subject only to the Constitution of the United States.”1

Other sections set up territorial governments with appointed governors and elected legislatures. They gave the vote at the first election to “every free white male inhabitant above the age of twenty-one years,” and they extended the federal fugitive slave laws of 1793 and 1850 into the new territories.1 Each territory could later enter the Union “with or without slavery, as their constitution may prescribe.”1 The act also promised not to impair the rights of the Native peoples living there as long as those rights remained “unextinguished by treaty.”1

Bleeding Kansas

Almost immediately, settlers for and against slavery poured into Kansas, each side hoping to win the first territorial election.1 The New England Emigrant Aid Company supplied guns and goods to antislavery settlers, and proslavery men crossed over from Missouri to vote and fight.3,2 Rival territorial governments, election fraud, and quarrels over land claims led to violence.4 In May 1856 proslavery “border ruffians” from Missouri looted and burned the free-state town of Lawrence.2 Days later John Brown and his sons killed five proslavery settlers at Pottawatomie Creek.4 That same month, after Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts gave a fierce antislavery speech, Representative Preston Brooks of South Carolina beat him nearly to death with a cane on the Senate floor.3 The violence went on in eastern Kansas and western Missouri until 1861. In May 1858 proslavery men took eleven free-state settlers to a ravine and shot them in the Marais des Cygnes Massacre.4 Kansas finally entered the Union as a free state on January 29, 1861.4

A new party

The act shattered party lines in the North. Northern Democrats split over it, and both Douglas and Pierce were badly weakened.2 Protest meetings met across the North in 1854, especially in Wisconsin and Michigan, and the antislavery movements that began that year came together in a new party that took the name Republican.3,1 The Republicans were dedicated to repealing the act and stopping the spread of slavery into the West.5 Abraham Lincoln warned that popular sovereignty would bring a dozen more episodes like Bleeding Kansas.5 Douglas, meanwhile, broke with President James Buchanan and Southern Democrats when he opposed admitting Kansas under the proslavery Lecompton constitution.5
