The Louisiana Purchase

The United States doubles in size, 1803

April 30, 1803 · The Mississippi River valley to the Rocky Mountains

In 1803 the United States bought the Louisiana Territory from France for $15 million. The land stretched from the Mississippi River to the Rocky Mountains, covered 828,000 square miles, and doubled the size of the country.1,2 President Thomas Jefferson had only wanted to buy New Orleans and the Floridas, but Napoleon, who had just lost an army to yellow fever and revolt in Haiti and expected war with Britain, offered the whole territory.2,4 The purchase secured the Mississippi for American farmers and opened the way west. It also raised questions that would trouble the country for decades: whether the Constitution allowed it, whether slavery would spread into the new land, and what would become of the many Native nations who lived there.1,2,9

Why it matters

The Louisiana Purchase made the United States a continental power and secured the Mississippi, but it also planted the conflicts over slavery and Native land that would define the next half-century.1,2,9

Historical background

As settlers crossed the Appalachians, the Mississippi River became the main route by which western farmers shipped their produce, and New Orleans was the port where goods were loaded onto ocean-going ships.2 Spain had owned Louisiana since 1762. The Pinckney Treaty of 1795 gave Americans the right to navigate the river and to move their goods at New Orleans, and with a weak Spain in charge, the Americans expected to expand west without trouble.2

Then Napoleon Bonaparte revived French plans for an empire in the Americas. France got Louisiana back from Spain by a secret treaty in 1800 and took possession in 1802. Napoleon meant to recapture the sugar colony of Saint-Domingue, today Haiti, from the enslaved people in revolt there, and to use Louisiana as the granary of his empire.2 In 1802, before the French arrived, Spanish officials closed the Mississippi to American shipping.3

Causes and contributing factors

Westerners were alarmed to see the stronger power of France at New Orleans. Jefferson wrote that “there is on the globe one single spot, the possessor of which is our natural and habitual enemy. It is New Orleans.”2 He prepared for conflict in the Mississippi Valley and sent James Monroe to join Robert Livingston, the minister in Paris, to buy New Orleans and West Florida for as much as $10 million. If that failed, they were to seek a military alliance with Britain.2,3 Then France's plans collapsed. Yellow fever and the fighting of enslaved rebels destroyed the French army in Haiti, and the Yawp says it was this defeat that took away Napoleon’s ability to control the island.4,2 War with Britain also loomed again, and the Miller Center says Napoleon needed money to fight it.3,2 Napoleon gave up his American project.

What happened

The French offered a surprised Monroe and Livingston not only New Orleans but the entire territory for $15 million. This was far beyond their instructions, but they agreed.2 The National Archives notes that the territory was larger than Great Britain, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and Portugal combined, and that Napoleon threatened to withdraw the offer if the Americans delayed.1 The agreement had three parts: the Treaty of Cession, signed on April 30, 1803, and two conventions. The first set the price of 60 million francs ($11,250,000), paid in U.S. bonds at 6 percent interest, and the second provided 20 million francs ($3,750,000) to pay American citizens’ claims against France.1,5 France's negotiator was the treasury minister François Barbé-Marbois, and Napoleon and his foreign minister Talleyrand signed the financial agreement.1,5 Livingston said after signing: “We have lived long but this is the noblest work of our whole lives… The United States take rank this day among the first powers of the world.”7

Jefferson faced a dilemma. He was known as a strict reader of the Constitution, and it said nothing about buying territory.1,2 The sources describe how he resolved it in different ways. The State Department says Jefferson decided to ignore the “legalistic interpretation” and to forgo an amendment, which helped create the principle of implied powers.2 The National Archives says the desire to expand across the continent “trumped his ideological beliefs.”1 The Yawp reports that he asked his cabinet and believed he should be able to “throw himself on the justice of his country” when he served the interests of the people.4 Another account says he argued that his power to make treaties was sufficient.7 In his annual message of October 17, 1803, Jefferson explained the benefits for security and trade.7 The Senate ratified the treaty on October 20 by 24 votes to 7.6

Immediate consequences

The West was elated at the news.2 On November 30, 1803, Spain formally returned Louisiana to France, which held it for twenty days. On December 20, at the Cabildo in New Orleans, France transferred the territory to the United States, and the Americans took possession ten days later.6 Jefferson appointed William C. C. Claiborne and General James Wilkinson to receive it. Claiborne promised the people of Louisiana that they could rely on “the security of your liberty, your property, and the religion of your choice.”6 Because winter travel up the Mississippi was hard, Upper Louisiana, centered on St. Louis, remained in Spanish hands until March 1804. On March 9 the Spanish flag came down and the French flag went up, and the next morning the American flag replaced it, a ceremony remembered as Three Flags Day.6,8

The treaty promised that the people of the ceded territory would be “incorporated in the Union of the United States” and would enjoy “all these rights, advantages and immunities of citizens of the United States,” and in the meantime be protected in their liberty, property, and religion.1 It also committed the United States to carry out the treaties Spain had made with Native tribes until new ones were agreed by mutual consent.1 The expedition of Meriwether Lewis and William Clark soon set out to explore the territory and reach the Pacific.8

Long-term significance

The National Archives calls Louisiana the first major cession in a long series of expansions across the nineteenth century. Within fifty years the borders of the contiguous United States were set with the Gadsden Purchase.1 Each expansion deepened the sectional split over slavery. When Missouri, carved from the Louisiana Purchase, asked for statehood in 1819 with more than ten thousand enslaved people, the Missouri Compromise of 1820 admitted it as a slave state and Maine as a free state and prohibited slavery north of the 36°30’ line in the rest of the Purchase.9,1 The Haitian Revolution that helped make the sale possible had also inspired free and enslaved Black Americans and terrified white Americans.4

The purchase also ignored the people who already lived on the land. The National Archives writes that thousands of American Indians across dozens of tribes lived there, and that as Americans moved west, conflict followed, until Native people were forcibly moved onto reservations and lost vast areas of their lands.1 The treaty itself mentions Native peoples only in a pledge to carry out Spain’s existing treaties with them until new ones were agreed.1

The price per acre is usually quoted as about four cents.1 Dividing $15 million by the State Department’s 530 million acres gives less than three cents (our own arithmetic), so the figure is only an approximation. Jefferson’s decision, the State Department says, contributed to the principle of the federal government’s implied powers.2
