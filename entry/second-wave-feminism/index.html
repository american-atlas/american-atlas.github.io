Second-Wave Feminism

The women’s liberation movement of the 1960s and 1970s

1961–1982

In the 1960s and 1970s a new wave of feminism challenged discrimination against women at work, in law, in education, and in family life.1,2 Many of its activists had worked in the civil rights and labor movements and found that male leaders there were not interested in gender inequality.1 Galvanized by Betty Friedan’s The Feminine Mystique (1963), they founded the National Organization for Women, formed consciousness-raising groups, and fought for equal pay, reproductive rights, and an Equal Rights Amendment.1,3 They won major legal changes, including the use of Title VII against job discrimination and Roe v. Wade in 1973.2 But a conservative countermovement led by Phyllis Schlafly stopped the ERA three states short of ratification.2,4

Why it matters

Second-wave feminism transformed women’s legal status and opportunities and changed American expectations about work, family, and equality, while also provoking a lasting conservative reaction.1,2,4

Goals

Feminists sought equal treatment in jobs and pay, an end to sex discrimination in law, access to contraception and abortion, child care, and equality in marriage.1,2 NOW’s founding statement, written by Friedan, pledged “to bring women into full participation in the mainstream of American society now, exercising all the privileges and responsibilities thereof in truly equal partnership with men.”3 The movement’s central constitutional goal was the Equal Rights Amendment, first proposed in Congress in 1923, which declared: “Equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or any state on account of sex.”2,7

Strategies

Working through government. In the early 1960s an older generation of women worked through official institutions. President John F. Kennedy’s Commission on the Status of Women, chaired by former first lady Eleanor Roosevelt, issued its report in 1963 as an “invitation to action.” It documented discrimination in law, in labor, and in government and called for changes in employment, taxes, and labor law.1 The sources date the commission differently: the American Yawp says it was established in 1961, while the National Women’s History Museum says Roosevelt took the chair in 1960.1,6 The Yawp notes that the report mostly addressed the concerns of middle-class and elite white working women.1 Roosevelt also worked on the Equal Pay Act, passed in 1963.6 Thanks partly to Black feminists such as Pauli Murray, the Civil Rights Act of 1964 included a ban on sex discrimination in employment in Title VII. “If sex is not included,” Murray told members of Congress, “the civil rights bill would be including only half of the Negroes.”2

Naming the problem. Friedan, a former labor journalist and a suburban mother, surveyed her Smith College classmates and interviewed women across the country for five years.3 In The Feminine Mystique she named “the problem that has no name,” the dissatisfaction of many middle-class housewives, and argued that it was a social problem and not a personal failing.1,3

Organizing. In 1966 Friedan, Pauli Murray, and Aileen Hernandez founded the National Organization for Women, with Friedan as its first president, according to the National Women’s History Museum. The Yawp lists Shirley Chisholm among its founders, while the museum’s biography of Chisholm says she was a member.3,1,5 NOW’s first campaign pressed the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission to enforce Title VII, and it ended sex-segregated help-wanted ads.3,8 Women sued to enter jobs reserved for men, and protests pushed the EEOC to issue stronger protections between 1968 and 1971.2 Friedan also helped found the National Association for the Repeal of Abortion Laws in 1969. In 1971 she joined Gloria Steinem, Bella Abzug, Shirley Chisholm, and Fannie Lou Hamer in founding the National Women’s Political Caucus.3,9

The personal is political. Younger women formed consciousness-raising groups that met in homes and women’s centers to talk about discrimination, pregnancy, relationships, and self-image. They argued that individual experiences reflected society-wide sexism, summed up in the slogan “the personal is political.”1

Poor women and women of color. Mostly African American mothers on welfare organized the National Welfare Rights Organization in 1966. Leaders such as Johnnie Tillmon won larger grants and fair hearings before benefits were cut off.1 Shirley Chisholm, a member of NOW, became the first African American woman in Congress, elected in 1968, and in 1972 the first woman and first African American to seek a major party’s presidential nomination.5

Protest and the press. On August 26, 1970, the fiftieth anniversary of woman suffrage, NOW’s Women’s Strike for Equality brought women into the streets to demand equal employment, political equality, abortion rights, free child care, and equality in marriage.1,3 Steinem helped found Ms. magazine, which first appeared in 1971 and became a regular magazine in 1972.9

Opposition

The movement faced opposition from women as well as men. Phyllis Schlafly, a conservative activist, founded the Eagle Forum in 1972 and led the campaign called STOP ERA, for “Stop Taking Our Privileges.”4 She argued that the amendment would take away women’s exemption from the military draft, the “dependent wife” benefits of Social Security for widows, and the preference that mothers received in custody cases.4 Her coalition, the museum estimates, numbered only about 20,000 people, but it was tightly focused, and it lobbied legislators with gifts of homemade bread.4 With the support of evangelical Christians and other religious conservatives, Schlafly spoke for “the millions of happily married women who believe in the laws which protect the family and require the husband to support his wife and children.”2 In 1980 the religious leader Jerry Falwell urged Americans to stand against the Equal Rights Amendment and the feminist movement.2

The movement also divided from within. Women of color challenged white feminists’ racism, and younger radical feminists criticized Friedan for speaking mainly for white, middle-class, educated women and for cooperating with men.1,3

Achievements

Feminists won enforcement of Title VII and stronger EEOC protections, and women moved slowly but steadily into jobs once held by men, into politics, and into corporate management.2 In Roe v. Wade (1973) the Supreme Court ruled 7 to 2 that the “right of privacy … is broad enough to encompass a woman’s decision whether or not to terminate her pregnancy.”2 The ERA passed Congress and went to the states on March 22, 1972. Hawaii ratified it the same day, and thirty states ratified it within a year.2 President Jimmy Carter supported it, proclaimed Women’s Equality Day in 1978, and signed the extension of the ratification deadline from March 1979 to June 30, 1982.7

Unresolved questions

The ERA stalled. Indiana became the thirty-fifth state to ratify in 1977, but no more followed, and the amendment expired in 1982, three states short of the thirty-eight it needed.2,7 The Yawp writes that the defeat showed the limits of a movement that could not fully represent half the country’s population, divided as it was by class, race, culture, and religion.2 Abortion became one of the most divisive issues in American politics, and in 2022, in Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization, the Supreme Court overruled Roe.10
