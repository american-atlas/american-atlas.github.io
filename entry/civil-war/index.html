The Civil War

The war for union and against slavery, 1861–1865

April 12, 1861 – April 9, 1865 · From Virginia and the Mississippi Valley to the border states, the Southwest, and the sea

The Civil War was fought between the United States and eleven Southern states that seceded after Abraham Lincoln’s election in 1860 to form the Confederate States of America, a nation whose leaders openly identified it with slavery.10 It began with the Confederate bombardment of Fort Sumter in April 1861 and ended with Robert E. Lee’s surrender at Appomattox Court House in April 1865.4,6 What started as a war to preserve the Union became, through the actions of enslaved people, Congress, and Lincoln’s Emancipation Proclamation, a war to end slavery.10,5 It was the deadliest war in American history: traditional counts put the dead at about 620,000, and more recent estimates at about 750,000.3,10

Why it matters

The Civil War decided that the United States would remain one nation and that slavery would end. By turning a war for union into a war for freedom, and by enlisting nearly 200,000 Black soldiers and sailors, it began a new definition of American citizenship that Reconstruction and the long civil rights struggle would fight over for the next century.7,9,10

Who fought

The United States, the Union, included twenty Northern and Western free states, from Maine to California and Oregon, led by President Abraham Lincoln.2 Four slaveholding border states, Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri, stayed loyal despite deep divisions and bitter guerrilla fighting; Lincoln reportedly said that to lose Kentucky was “nearly the same as to lose the whole game.”10 The Confederate States of America comprised eleven states, Texas, Arkansas, Louisiana, Tennessee, Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia, Florida, South Carolina, North Carolina, and Virginia, under President Jefferson Davis, with its capital first at Montgomery, Alabama, and from May 1861 at Richmond, Virginia.2,10

The Union had overwhelming material advantages. Its population was about 18.5 million, against 5.5 million free people and 3.5 million enslaved people in the Confederacy; it had more than eleven times as many factory workers, twice as many horses, and far more money in banks and coin.2 The Confederacy led only in crops such as tobacco and rice, and it counted on its cotton, the raw material of Europe’s textile mills, to win foreign support.2,8 Even with close to total conscription, the South could not match the North in numbers, and Southern soldiers were significantly more likely to be killed, wounded, or captured.3

Political background

The war grew out of the long national quarrel over slavery and its expansion into the West, which the Missouri Compromise, the Compromise of 1850, and the Kansas-Nebraska Act had each tried to manage. The election of 1860 broke the political system apart. The Democratic Party split, with Southern Democrats nominating their own candidate, and Lincoln, the candidate of the new Republican Party, which opposed the spread of slavery, won the presidency.10,1

South Carolina acted almost at once. On December 20, 1860, its convention voted 169 to 0 to dissolve its union with the United States.10 Six more states of the Lower South followed, and on February 4, 1861, delegates of the seven seceding states met in Montgomery, Alabama, to organize a new nation and chose Jefferson Davis as president.10 Their own statements left no doubt about the cause. “Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery—the greatest material interest of the world,” Mississippi declared; every rationale for secession could be tied to slavery.10 In his inaugural address in March 1861 Lincoln declared secession “legally void” and promised to hold federal property in the seceded states, though he did not intend to invade them.10

Major phases

The war begins, 1861. Attention fixed on Fort Sumter, a federal fort in Charleston Harbor. On April 12, 1861, Confederate batteries opened fire, and after some thirty hours Major Robert Anderson accepted terms and evacuated the fort.10,4 Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers to serve three months, and the call for troops pushed Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, and Arkansas into the Confederacy.10 The Union adopted General-in-Chief Winfield Scott’s Anaconda Plan, which aimed to strangle the Confederacy with a naval blockade of its ports and control of its rivers, above all the Mississippi.10 Lincoln suspended the writ of habeas corpus, allowing military commanders to arrest secession-minded activists without charge.10 Any hope of a short war died that summer at the First Battle of Bull Run near Manassas, Virginia, just twenty-five miles from Washington, where Confederate forces routed a Union army that had been pushed to advance on Richmond.11,10

A war for union, 1862. In the West, Union victories at Fort Henry and Fort Donelson in February 1862 opened Tennessee to Ulysses S. Grant, and in April the Battle of Shiloh, with almost 24,000 casualties, shocked both sides with its carnage and ended Confederate hopes of blocking the Union advance into Mississippi.12 Led by Secretary of the Navy Gideon Welles, the Union navy steadily tightened the Anaconda.10 In the East, General George B. McClellan’s Peninsula Campaign approached Richmond, but Robert E. Lee, newly in command of the Army of Northern Virginia, drove him back.10 Lee then invaded Maryland, hoping a victory on Union soil might influence the Northern elections and win European recognition for the Confederacy.13 On September 17, 1862, at Antietam Creek near Sharpsburg, the armies fought a battle that cost about 22,700 casualties in a single day. The battle itself was inconclusive, but Lee withdrew to Virginia.13,10

A war for emancipation, 1863. From the first months of the war, enslaved people took freedom into their own hands by escaping to Union lines. At Fort Monroe in Virginia, General Benjamin Butler began accepting people who escaped to his lines, calling them “contraband of war” rather than returning them, and Congress affirmed his policy in the First Confiscation Act.10 Congress abolished slavery in the District of Columbia in April 1862 and in the territories that summer, and the Second Confiscation Act of July 1862 freed people whose enslavers served the Confederacy.10,7 Antietam gave Lincoln the victory he needed: on September 22, 1862, he issued a preliminary Emancipation Proclamation, and on January 1, 1863, he declared all persons held as slaves in the rebellious areas “are, and henceforward shall be free.”5 The proclamation exempted the loyal border states and parts of the Confederacy already under Union control, but it changed the war’s purpose from union alone to freedom.10,5 It also opened the army to Black men. The War Department created the U.S. Colored Troops in May 1863; by the end of the war about 179,000 Black men had served in the army, about a tenth of its strength, and 19,000 in the navy.7 They received less pay than white soldiers, faced murder or enslavement if captured, and mostly served on garrison and labor duty, but they fought in more than four hundred battles and engagements, including Milliken’s Bend, Port Hudson, Fort Wagner, and Nashville, and fifteen Black soldiers received the Medal of Honor.10

Hard war, 1864–1865. In 1864 Grant, now general-in-chief, ordered simultaneous offensives along the whole Confederate frontier, understanding that the South could not win a war of attrition.16 His Overland Campaign through the Wilderness and Cold Harbor attacked Lee’s dwindling army again and again, and from June 1864 he besieged Petersburg, the gateway to Richmond.10 In the West, William Tecumseh Sherman fought his way toward Atlanta, the Confederacy’s great rail and industrial center, and took it on September 2, 1864.16,10 Two months later he cut loose from his supply lines and marched 62,000 men across Georgia to the sea, destroying railroads, factories, and stores to break the South’s will to fight; he reached Savannah in time to present it to Lincoln as a Christmas gift.17,10

Turning points

Three moments did the most to change the war’s course. Antietam (September 1862), though tactically inconclusive, ended Lee’s first invasion of the North, gave Lincoln the occasion for the Emancipation Proclamation, and all but ended the danger that Britain or France would recognize the Confederacy.13,10 Confederate diplomats had expected the cotton trade to force Europe’s hand, but Britain proclaimed neutrality in May 1861, the other powers followed, and no foreign government ever recognized the Confederacy.8

Gettysburg and Vicksburg (July 1863) came within a day of each other. At Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, July 1–3, Lee’s second invasion of the North ended in defeat after three days of fighting that cost some 51,000 casualties, the bloodiest battle of the war.14,10 On July 4, after a 47-day siege, Confederate General John C. Pemberton surrendered Vicksburg, Mississippi, to Grant. With the fall of Port Hudson five days later, the Union controlled the whole Mississippi, and the Confederacy was split in two.15,10 Lincoln had called Vicksburg “the key,” and Jefferson Davis had called it the “nailhead that holds the South’s two halves together.”15

Atlanta (September 1864) came at a moment when Lincoln himself doubted he would be reelected and his opponent, General McClellan, hoped for the votes of war-weary soldiers. Sherman’s capture of the city, together with overwhelming support from Union troops, gave Lincoln an easy victory in November and ensured that the war would be fought to its end.10,16

Outcome

By the spring of 1865 the Confederacy was collapsing. In March, in desperation, the Confederate Congress authorized the enlistment of Black soldiers, but only a few dozen enlisted before the end.10 On April 2 Richmond was abandoned as Jefferson Davis and his government fled.6 A week later, on April 9, 1865, Grant and Lee met in the parlor of a house at Appomattox Court House, Virginia. Grant’s terms were generous: Lee’s men could go home in safety if they pledged to stop fighting and gave up their arms. On April 12 the infantry of the Army of Northern Virginia stacked their weapons, folded their flags, and received parole papers.6 Other Confederate forces surrendered over the following weeks.

Lincoln did not live to see the peace. Less than a week after Lee’s surrender he was killed by an assassin’s bullet.1 In his second inaugural address, delivered on March 4, 1865, he had asked the nation to finish the war “with malice toward none; with charity for all,” and “to bind up the nation’s wounds.”10

Consequences for American society

The war destroyed slavery. The Thirteenth Amendment, passed by Congress in January 1865 after Lincoln made it part of the Republican platform, was ratified on December 6, 1865, and abolished slavery throughout the United States, except as punishment for crime.9 Together with the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments, it began the vast and contested project of Reconstruction: deciding what freedom and citizenship would mean for some four million formerly enslaved people.2,9,10

The human cost was staggering. About 620,000 soldiers died in the traditional count, roughly two percent of the population, and some historians now estimate about 750,000.3,10 More Americans became casualties at Gettysburg alone than in the Revolutionary War and the War of 1812 combined.3 Disease accounted for more than half of all casualties, falling hardest on men from rural areas who had little immunity, and nearly as many men died in prison camps as were killed in the whole of the Vietnam War.10,3 More than two hundred thousand women became widows.10

The war also transformed the nation’s government and society. To fight it, the federal government introduced a draft, suspended habeas corpus, and expanded its power over the economy and the states.10 The first Northern draft provoked the New York City Draft Riots in July 1863, in which white rioters killed about 120 people, including at least eleven Black New Yorkers who were lynched; in the Confederacy, food shortages and inflation drove women to lead bread riots in Richmond and other cities.10 Women North and South ran farms and businesses, served as nurses, and organized sanitary fairs that raised millions of dollars for soldiers.10 Above all, the Union survived, and with it the principle Lincoln had defended from his inaugural address on: that secession was legally void.1,10
