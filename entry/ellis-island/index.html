Ellis Island

The nation’s busiest immigration station, 1892–1924

1892–1924 · New York Harbor, New York and New Jersey

From 1892 to 1924, Ellis Island in New York Harbor was America’s largest and busiest immigration station.1 The Park Service counts over 12 million immigrants processed there, usually in three to seven hours.1,3 For most it was an “Island of Hope,” the first step to a new life; for the rest it was an “Island of Tears,” where families were separated and people were sent back.1 The quota laws of the 1920s cut immigration sharply, and the station spent its last decades mainly detaining and deporting people before it closed in 1954.3,4 Restored as a museum, it is now part of Statue of Liberty National Monument.2

Why it matters

Ellis Island is the best-known symbol of the great wave of European immigration that reshaped American cities around 1900, and of the national debate over who should be allowed in. Its history runs from open entry through the quota laws to wartime and Cold War detention.1,3,4

About the place

Ellis Island was once one of three small “Oyster Islands” in New York Harbor; landfill later enlarged it to 27.5 acres.5,2 Its main building, a fireproof brick structure by the architects Boring and Tilton, opened in 1900, and a hospital complex with contagious disease wards was built on the added land beside it.3 Since a 6–3 Supreme Court ruling, New York has held the original 3.3 acres and New Jersey the 24 acres of landfill.2

History

Before immigration. In the 1670s the English governor of New York granted the island to the customs collector William Dyre, and it passed through a series of owners and names. Pirates were hanged there, giving it the name “Gibbet Island.”5,2 The merchant Samuel Ellis advertised “That pleasant situated island” for sale in 1785 but could not sell it; his heirs still owned it when New York State bought it in 1808 and conveyed it to the United States.5 Before the War of 1812 the army built Fort Gibson there, which never saw action, and the navy later kept a powder magazine on the island until complaints about the danger led Congress to order it removed.5,2

The federal station. The Immigration Act of 1891 moved control of immigration from the states to the federal government and created what became the Bureau of Immigration.3 Ellis Island’s first station, built of Georgia pine, opened on January 1, 1892, and Annie Moore of Ireland was the first immigrant processed.3,2 A fire destroyed it in June 1897; the Park Service dates the fire to June 14 on one page and June 15 on another. No one was killed.2,3 The new main building opened on December 17, 1900.3

Inspection. Doctors of the Public Health Service watched the long lines climbing into the Registry Room for signs of disease or weakness, such as trachoma, tuberculosis, or a poor physique. Anyone suspected was marked in chalk with a letter, such as “H” for heart, and pulled aside for a closer examination.6 Then an immigrant inspector questioned each person against the ship’s passenger list. He could admit only those “clearly and beyond a doubt entitled to enter,” and had to exclude contract laborers, polygamists, paupers, convicted criminals, anarchists, and anyone “likely to become a public charge.”7 Doctors only diagnosed and treated; the decision to admit belonged to the inspectors.6 At the peak, 1.2 million people were examined in a single year.2 President Theodore Roosevelt launched a campaign against corruption and mistreatment of immigrants at the station.2

Quotas. The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 “ended U.S.’s open door immigration policy,” limiting each nationality to 3 percent of its share of the 1910 census, and stranding thousands of immigrants on the island.3 The Immigration Act of 1924 cut the limit to 2 percent of the 1890 census, aimed especially at southern and eastern Europeans, and moved inspection to American consulates abroad, so most immigrants no longer needed to pass through Ellis Island.3

Detention and closing. In the 1930s, for the first time, deportations far outnumbered admissions.4 During World War II, about 1,000 German, Italian, and Japanese “enemy aliens” and their families were held there, while the Coast Guard trained 60,000 enlisted men and 3,000 officers on the island.4 After the Internal Security Act of 1950 the number of detainees rose from 400 to 1,200.4 The last detainees left in 1954 and the island was abandoned.3,2 President Lyndon Johnson later made it part of Statue of Liberty National Monument, and after a restoration announced by President Ronald Reagan it reopened to the public as a museum.2

A different gateway. Ellis Island’s experience was not shared by all immigrants. On the West Coast, the Angel Island Immigration Station, opened in 1910, was “designed to detain, rather than welcome,” and Asian immigrants there faced far harsher treatment.8
