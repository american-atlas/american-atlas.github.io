Loyalists

The Americans who stayed loyal to the British Crown during the Revolution

1765–1783

The Revolution was also a civil war. Loyalists fought Patriots in their own neighborhoods, often “pitting family members against one another,” as the Yawp puts it.1 Some Loyalists were prominent, like Governor Thomas Hutchinson of Massachusetts or William Franklin, the royal governor of New Jersey and son of Benjamin Franklin.4,2 When Britain lost, Loyalists lost their homes and property, and tens of thousands left for Britain, Canada and other corners of the empire.1

Why it matters

Loyalists remind us that independence was not a unanimous choice. Their defeat reshaped the British Empire as well as America: Loyalist exiles settled in large numbers in British Canada, and Black Loyalists helped settle Nova Scotia and Sierra Leone.1

Definition

Loyalists, also called Tories, were colonists who remained loyal to King George III and opposed independence. The American Yawp writes that they “came from all ranks of American society”: royal officials, merchants and members of the Church of England, but also farmers, formerly enslaved people who joined the British in search of freedom, and Native nations allied with the Crown.1,2,3 About sixty thousand Loyalists left America because of the Revolution, many to live the rest of their lives in exile.1

Historical context

Loyalism had many roots. Some people owed their positions to the Crown, some belonged to the Church of England, and some simply believed Britain would win.2,1 New York held many Loyalists, particularly among its merchants and Anglicans.1 Thomas Hutchinson, who opposed the Stamp Act but saw no way that Britain “could have conceded to the claims of America, without admitting their principle of total independence,” had his Boston house wrecked by a mob in 1765.4

For others, loyalty was a path to freedom. Enslaved people who escaped to the British after Lord Dunmore’s proclamation of 1775 became Black Loyalists.1 Four of the Six Nations of the Iroquois, persuaded in part by the Mohawk leader Joseph Brant, fought for Britain because Brant predicted that American independence would cost Native peoples their land.3

Examples

  • William Franklin. New Jersey’s royal governor refused his father’s urging to join the Revolution. He was arrested in 1776, imprisoned in Connecticut, and after his release in 1778 led the Associated Loyalists, who waged guerrilla war in New York, New Jersey and Connecticut. He and Benjamin Franklin never reconciled, and he died in London in 1813.2
  • Thomas Hutchinson. Replaced as governor of Massachusetts by General Thomas Gage after the Boston Tea Party, he went to Britain, became an exile when the war began, and lost his Massachusetts property to the revolutionary government.4
  • Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea). The Mohawk war chief led Loyalist and Native forces in New York and was blamed, unjustly in the Battlefield Trust’s view, for the Cherry Valley Massacre of 1778.3
  • Kings Mountain. On October 7, 1780, Loyalist militia under the British major Patrick Ferguson were routed by Patriot “Overmountain men” in South Carolina. The Battlefield Trust calls the British effort to secure Loyalist support in the South “a failure.”5
  • Boston King. Born enslaved in South Carolina, he escaped to the British in 1780 and after the war went to Canada and later to Sierra Leone.1

The peace and the property

The Treaty of Paris dealt with the Loyalists in Articles 5 and 6. Congress was to “earnestly recommend” that the states restore confiscated Loyalist estates, and there were to be no further confiscations or prosecutions for the part anyone had taken in the war.6 The Yawp writes that the Americans “reneged on this promise,” and states kept seizing Loyalist property through the 1780s.1 Sources differ on what was promised. The State Department’s Office of the Historian says the British won a promise of “restitution of property lost during the war,” while the Battlefield Trust’s biography of William Franklin says Benjamin Franklin “refused compensation or amnesty to Loyalists” at the Paris talks.7,2 The text itself shows a compromise: a recommendation, with no power to enforce it.6

Exile

Some Loyalists went to England, where, the Yawp writes, they found themselves “strangers and outsiders in what they had thought of as their mother country.” Many more settled in Nova Scotia, New Brunswick and Quebec. In 1783 thousands of formerly enslaved Loyalists left with the British army. Some later settled in Sierra Leone, but in the empire they still faced restrictions, including on owning land.1
