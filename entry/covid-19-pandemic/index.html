COVID-19 Pandemic

The coronavirus pandemic in the United States, 2020–2023

January 20, 2020 – May 5, 2023 · Nationwide and worldwide

COVID-19, a disease caused by a new coronavirus first identified in Wuhan, China, in late 2019, reached the United States in January 2020.1,2 Within weeks schools, offices, and businesses closed. Unemployment reached 14.7 percent, the highest since the Great Depression, and hospitals, above all in New York, ran short of supplies.1,2 Vaccines developed at record speed were authorized in December 2020. But new variants kept the virus spreading, and masks and vaccines became politically divisive.1,2,3 By May 2022 more than one million Americans had died. The Miller Center counts more than 1.1 million deaths in all, the worst toll from one disease since the influenza pandemic of 1918.2,4

Why it matters

With more than a million American deaths, COVID-19 was the defining public health crisis of the early twenty-first century and deepened the country’s political divisions.2,4

Historical background

In late 2019 a cluster of patients in Wuhan, a city in China’s Hubei Province, fell ill with an unusual pneumonia that did not respond to standard treatment. The first cases seemed connected to the Huanan Seafood Wholesale Market.1 Chinese health officials identified a new coronavirus as the cause, and scientists in China and Australia published its genetic sequence in January 2020.1 The American Yawp notes that other coronaviruses had been identified and contained in earlier years.2 This one spread before many people showed symptoms, and it was especially dangerous for older people and those with chronic illness.1,2

Causes and contributing factors

On January 23, 2020, China placed Wuhan, a city of 11 million people, under lockdown, but the virus had already escaped through travel.1,2 At the end of January the World Health Organization declared a public health emergency of international concern.1,6 Early American testing was slow. Some of the first CDC test kits produced “untrustworthy results,” and the FDA later allowed other laboratories to develop their own tests to address shortages.1 Soon the CDC was finding cases that could not be traced to travel, a sign that the virus was spreading in American communities.1

What happened

The first wave (spring 2020). The CDC confirmed the first American case on January 20, 2020, in Washington State.1 The CDC reported the first American death in Washington, and the Miller Center dates it to February.1,4 On March 11 the World Health Organization declared a pandemic, and on March 13 the Trump administration declared a national emergency.1 States began shutting down. On March 15 New York City closed its public schools, the largest school system in the country with 1.1 million students.1 Americans stayed home, schools moved online, and in-person work was largely left to “essential workers.”4 On March 27 President Donald Trump signed the CARES Act, which the Miller Center values at $2.2 trillion, with payments of $1,200 per adult, expanded unemployment benefits, and forgivable small-business loans.1,4 Hospitals ran short of gowns, masks, and even body bags, especially in New York.1 States, the Yawp writes, were left to compete with one another for protective equipment.2 By May unemployment had reached 14.7 percent, with 20.5 million people out of work, and on May 28 the recorded death toll passed 100,000.1

Masks, waves, and protests. In the spring of 2020 the CDC recommended that people wear masks outside the home. With medical masks scarce, many made their own, but mask-wearing became politicized.1,2 Hourly workers without paid sick leave often had to choose between a paycheck and their health.2 A wave hit the South in the summer of 2020, and the worst came that winter, when nearly three thousand Americans died every day through January and much of February 2021.2 The Miller Center notes that by April 2020 more Americans had died than people in any other country, a position the country held until the end of the pandemic. Trump downplayed the risks and contracted the virus himself in October 2020.4

Vaccines (December 2020 onward). On April 30, 2020, the administration had launched Operation Warp Speed, which funded six vaccine candidates while they were still in trials. Public health experts credited it with speeding the vaccines.1,4 In November the Moderna and Pfizer-BioNTech vaccines proved about 95 percent effective in trials.1 On December 11 the FDA authorized the Pfizer-BioNTech vaccine, and Sandra Lindsay, a nurse in New York, became the first American outside the trials to be vaccinated.1 Health workers and residents of long-term care facilities went first.1 On March 11, 2021, President Joe Biden signed the $1.9 trillion American Rescue Plan, and by late spring Americans were being vaccinated by the millions.1,4,2 But many refused, swayed by conspiracy theories on social media or by politics.2

Variants (2021–2022). In June 2021 the Delta variant, first identified in India, became dominant in the United States and brought a third wave.1 In late November the World Health Organization named Omicron, first identified in South Africa, a variant of concern. The CDC estimated it was about 1.6 times more transmissible than Delta, and the first American case was found in California on December 1.1 By December 15, 2021, the toll had passed 800,000, and one in every hundred Americans aged 65 or older had died of the virus.1 In May 2022 the official death toll crossed one million.2

Immediate consequences

In 2020 alone about 3.36 million Americans died of all causes, and the age-adjusted death rate rose 15.9 percent. COVID-19 was listed as a cause or contributing cause in about 378,000 deaths and became the third leading cause of death after heart disease and cancer.5 The CDC found the highest overall death rates among Black and American Indian or Alaska Native people, and the highest COVID-19 death rates among American Indian or Alaska Native and Hispanic people, as well as among men and the elderly.5

The pandemic upended work and family life. An estimated 5.1 million women left the workforce when schools and child care closed in 2020. By May 2021, the CDC timeline notes, only 56 percent of American women were working for pay, the lowest share since 1986.1 Many white-collar workers moved to working from home, while essential workers kept going in.2,4 The summer of 2020 also saw nationwide protests over the killing of George Floyd by a Minneapolis police officer, held in the middle of the lockdowns.2

Long-term significance

On May 5, 2023, the World Health Organization declared that COVID-19 was “an established and ongoing health issue” and no longer a global emergency, noting that 13.3 billion vaccine doses had been given worldwide.6 The Miller Center calls it the deadliest disease to strike the United States since the influenza pandemic of 1918 and 1919, with more than 1.1 million American lives lost.4 It also deepened Americans’ divisions over masks, vaccines, and scientific expertise.2,4
