The Great Recession

The financial crisis and deep recession of 2007–2009

December 2007 – June 2009 · Nationwide and worldwide

The Great Recession began in December 2007 and ended in June 2009, the longest recession since World War II. It grew out of a housing bubble fueled by risky “subprime” mortgages that were bundled into securities and sold throughout the financial system. When homeowners began to default, major financial firms such as Bear Stearns and Lehman Brothers collapsed. Home prices fell about 30 percent, stocks fell 57 percent, and unemployment peaked at 10 percent. The federal government and the Federal Reserve intervened on a massive scale to stabilize the financial system.

Why it matters

The Great Recession was the longest recession since World War II, with the largest drop in output of the postwar era, and a defining event for a generation of Americans.

Historical background

From the 1990s into the 2000s, home prices climbed, and housing-related sectors accounted for roughly 40 percent of net private job creation between 2001 and 2005. Lenders offered high-risk “subprime” mortgages and repackaged them into securities, expanding credit and bidding up prices. Decades of financial deregulation, including the repeal in the 1990s of parts of the Depression-era Glass-Steagall Act and the exemption of credit-default swaps from regulation, had loosened restraints on risky practices.

Causes and contributing factors

The housing boom peaked in 2006. As prices fell and homeowners defaulted, losses spread through the heavily leveraged financial system, because mortgages had been bundled and resold so often that it was hard to tell who owned what.

What happened

Major firms such as Bear Stearns and Lehman Brothers disappeared almost overnight. President George W. Bush signed the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act, and the government poured billions into propping up banks. The Federal Reserve cut its key interest rate from 5.25 percent in September 2007 to nearly zero by December 2008 and began buying mortgage-backed securities and other debt to support the housing market and the financial system. President Barack Obama continued the bank rescue and won an $800 billion economic stimulus, the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, in 2009.

Immediate consequences

Millions lost jobs and homes. Resentment of bank bailouts fueled both the conservative Tea Party movement and the left-leaning Occupy Wall Street protests. In 2010 Congress passed the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act to prevent future meltdowns.

Long-term significance

Recovery was slow: as of 2013 unemployment was still above 7 percent. The crisis magnified rising inequality and deepened distrust of financial and political elites, shaping American politics for years afterward.
