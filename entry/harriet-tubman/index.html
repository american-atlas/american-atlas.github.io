Harriet Tubman

Freedom seeker, Underground Railroad conductor, Union scout

c. 1822–1913

Harriet Tubman was born into slavery on Maryland’s Eastern Shore around 1822, escaped in 1849, and then went back again and again to lead family members and friends to freedom, becoming the most famous conductor of the Underground Railroad.1,3 During the Civil War she served the Union Army as a nurse, scout, and spy, and in June 1863 she helped plan and lead the Combahee River Raid in South Carolina, which freed more than 700 enslaved people.1,5 In her later years in Auburn, New York, she supported women’s suffrage and opened a home for elderly and poor African Americans.2,3

Why it matters

Tubman turned her own escape into a lifelong campaign against slavery, risking recapture again and again to free others. Her wartime raid made her the first woman in American history to plan and lead an armed military operation, and her later work for suffrage and the poor made her a symbol of courage that reaches far beyond the Underground Railroad.3,2

Early life and context

Harriet Tubman was born Araminta Ross around 1822 in Dorchester County, on the Eastern Shore of Maryland, one of nine children of Harriet “Rit” Green Ross and Benjamin Ross.2,3 Under slavery the children of an enslaved mother were enslaved too, whatever their father’s status; Ben Ross was free, but Rit and her children were held by a man named Edward Brodess.2 Brodess broke the family apart. He moved Rit and the children to his farm in Bucktown, away from their father, and sold three of Araminta’s older sisters into the Deep South.3,4 At about six she was hired out to other households to care for their children, and she was later made to trap muskrats in the Little Blackwater River, separated from her mother for long periods.3 She never forgot the pain of those separations.3

Around the age of thirteen, while she was at the Bucktown village store, she refused to help an overseer stop a man who was running away. The overseer threw a two-pound weight at the fleeing man; it struck Araminta in the head instead and nearly killed her.3,2 Her mother nursed her as well as she could, but she was soon sent back to work.3 For the rest of her life she suffered from severe headaches, seizures, and sudden “sleeping spells,” and she experienced vivid visions that she understood in religious terms.2,3

As a young woman Tubman managed to gain a little control over her work. She arranged with Brodess to hire herself out in return for a yearly payment, and she worked in the timber fields at Parson’s Creek alongside her father.2,3 There she came to know the marshes and waterways of the Eastern Shore, and she met Black sailors who traveled to Baltimore, Philadelphia, and Delaware and carried news along a hidden network of communication.3 In 1844 she married a free Black man, John Tubman, and took the name Harriet, perhaps in honor of her mother.1,3

Major actions and achievements

In March 1849 Edward Brodess died. To pay his debts, his widow needed to sell some of the people he had held, and Tubman feared being sold away from her family into the even harsher slavery of the Deep South.3 That fall she fled with two of her brothers; an October 1849 newspaper advertisement offered a reward for “Minty” and her brothers, who turned back.6,2 Tubman tried again alone and reached Philadelphia, where she found allies among Black and white abolitionists, including William Still of the Underground Railroad.3,2 “I had reasoned this out in my mind,” she later explained; “there was one of two things I had the right to, liberty or death; if I could not have one, I would have the other.”2

Freedom without her family was not enough. From December 1850 through 1860 she returned to Maryland about thirteen times and led some seventy family members and friends to freedom, and she gave instructions to about seventy more who escaped on their own.1,6 The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 made her work far more dangerous, because it empowered the federal government to deputize ordinary citizens to arrest runaways; Tubman therefore often guided people all the way to St. Catharines, Ontario, in Canada.9,3 She drew on the skills she had learned in the fields, woods, and wharves, on her knowledge of the stars, and on the secret communication networks of free and enslaved Black people.1 In 1851 she returned for her husband, only to find that he had remarried, and in 1857 she brought her elderly parents north.2 William Lloyd Garrison called her “Moses,” and the name stuck.1

Tubman also became a public figure. In the late 1850s she spoke at antislavery and women’s rights meetings, often introduced under a false name to protect her from the Fugitive Slave Act.6 She could not read or write, so her speeches survive only in reporters’ brief descriptions.6 In April 1858 she met the abolitionist John Brown in St. Catharines; he called her “General Tubman,” and she helped him plan and recruit for his attack on Harpers Ferry, though illness kept her from joining the raid itself.7 In April 1860, visiting Troy, New York, she was credited in some accounts with leading the crowd that freed Charles Nalle, a fugitive from Virginia who had been arrested there.6

When the Civil War began, Massachusetts Governor John Andrew believed that her knowledge of the Maryland marshes would be useful in the similar coastal lowlands of South Carolina.3 She arrived at Port Royal in 1862, nursing sick and wounded soldiers and freed people, and soon began scouting and spying behind Confederate lines.1,3 She recruited a network of local Black boatmen and scouts, among them men who had escaped from the rice plantations along the Combahee River and knew where the Confederates had placed mines in the water.5

On June 1, 1863, about three hundred soldiers of the 2nd South Carolina Volunteers, Black troops later known as the 34th U.S. Colored Troops, set out from Beaufort on three gunboats with Colonel James Montgomery and Tubman.5 Early on June 2 they moved up the Combahee, burned plantation houses, rice mills, and other property, and destroyed seven plantations.5 Enslaved people in the rice fields ran and swam toward the boats; Tubman helped organize their escape and spread word to nearby plantations that freedom had come.5 More than 750 people were freed, according to most National Park Service accounts, and in the days that followed Tubman helped recruit more than 150 of them into the Union army.5,3 She became the first woman to plan and lead an armed raid in American military history.3

Beliefs and ideas

Tubman’s courage rested on a deep Christian faith. She trusted the visions that came to her during her spells as guidance from God, and she believed she had seen John Brown in a recurring dream before she ever met him.2,7 The pain of being separated from her family never left her, and her missions centered on freeing her own relatives and friends so that they could live in freedom together.3 After the war she carried the same conviction into the campaign for women’s rights. She worked alongside suffragists such as Susan B. Anthony and Elizabeth Cady Stanton and became a co-founder of the National Association of Colored Women, which demanded equality and the vote for Black women.2,3

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Much of what Americans know about Tubman comes from biographies written in her lifetime, especially Sarah Hopkins Bradford’s Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman (1869) and Harriet, the Moses of Her People (1886). These books helped support Tubman financially, but some of their content was embellished, and historians have had to separate fact from legend.6 The numbers of her journeys and of the people she rescued, for example, vary from one account to another; careful research by historians such as Kate Clifford Larson puts them at about thirteen trips and sixty to seventy people.6 After the war Tubman also pressed the federal government for recognition of her service, arguing that her work on the Combahee River entitled her to a pension.5,4

Legacy

In 1859 Tubman bought a house in Auburn, New York, from Senator William H. Seward, and it remained her home for the rest of her life.1,3 In 1869 she married Nelson Davis, a Civil War veteran, and the couple adopted a daughter, Gertie.3 She raised money with the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church and opened a home for elderly and poor African Americans on her land.2,1 She died of pneumonia on March 10, 1913, and was buried with military honors in Fort Hill Cemetery in Auburn.3,1

Tubman’s memory has only grown since. A ship was named for her during World War II, her portrait has appeared on U.S. postage stamps, and a statue of her stands in Aburi, Ghana.3 Today she is honored by two national historical parks, in Maryland and in Auburn, and by an Underground Railroad byway through the landscape of her escape.1,4
