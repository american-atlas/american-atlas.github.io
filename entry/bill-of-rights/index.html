The Bill of Rights

The first ten amendments to the Constitution

December 15, 1791

The Bill of Rights is the first ten amendments to the Constitution, ratified on December 15, 1791.1 It protects freedom of religion, speech, the press, and assembly, the right to bear arms, security against unreasonable searches, and the rights of people accused of crimes, and it reserves undelegated powers to the states or the people.2 It was added to answer Anti-Federalist critics who feared that the new national government would trample individual liberties. James Madison, once its opponent, guided it through the First Congress.1,3,4 At first it limited only the federal government. The author of the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) meant that amendment to make the Bill of Rights binding on the states as well, a question the Supreme Court long resisted.6,7

Why it matters

The Bill of Rights helped win acceptance of the Constitution, and its guarantees became central to how Americans understand their freedoms. The National Archives notes that the Constitution might never have been ratified without the promise of a bill of rights.8,1 Later generations used the Fourteenth Amendment to argue that its protections bind the states as well.7

Historical circumstances

The Constitution signed in Philadelphia in 1787 contained no list of individual rights. Most delegates held that state constitutions already protected those rights, and James Wilson argued that a bill of rights was unnecessary because the new government had only the powers the Constitution gave it.5 George Mason of Virginia disagreed, and he was one of three delegates still present on the last day who refused to sign because the document lacked a bill of rights.4,5

During ratification the missing bill of rights became the Anti-Federalists’ most powerful weapon. Opponents remembered British violations of colonists’ rights and warned that the Constitution opened the way to tyranny by a distant central government.1,5 Federalists answered that a list of rights was not only redundant but dangerous, since it might suggest that any right left off the list was unprotected.6 In Massachusetts, where ratification was in danger, Federalists agreed to recommend amendments. The state ratified on February 6, 1788, by 187 to 168, and six of the remaining states attached similar recommendations.5,4 In Virginia, Madison promised George Mason’s and Patrick Henry’s followers that a bill of rights would be the first business of the new Congress.3

Madison had come to see real value in such a list. In October 1788 he wrote that these “fundamental maxims of free Government” would give citizens “a good ground for an appeal to the sense of community” against oppression.5 He also saw a political advantage: adding modest amendments might prevent opponents from forcing more drastic changes to the Constitution.4

Principal ideas

On June 8, 1789, Madison introduced his amendments in the House, meeting in Federal Hall in New York. Few members thought amendments urgent, and Madison “hounded his colleagues relentlessly.”4 He proposed nineteen changes and focused on individual rights, leaving aside suggestions that would have restructured the government.3,4 He wanted them woven into the text of the Constitution, but Congress added them as separate amendments.3 The House passed seventeen, the Senate cut them to twelve, and on September 25, 1789, Congress sent twelve articles to the states.4,1

The ten that were ratified fall into three groups.2

Freedoms of conscience and expression. The First Amendment begins, “Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances.”2

Protections against government power. The Second Amendment protects “the right of the people to keep and bear Arms,” linked to “a well regulated Militia.” The Third bars quartering soldiers in private homes in peacetime without the owner’s consent. The Fourth protects against “unreasonable searches and seizures” and requires warrants based on probable cause.2

Rights of the accused. The Fifth guarantees grand juries for serious crimes and protects against double jeopardy and self-incrimination. It also forbids taking “life, liberty, or property, without due process of law” and requires just compensation when private property is taken for public use. The Sixth guarantees a speedy and public trial by jury and the assistance of counsel. The Seventh preserves jury trials in civil cases, and the Eighth forbids excessive bail and “cruel and unusual punishments.”2

Reserved rights and powers. The Ninth declares that listing some rights does not deny others “retained by the people.” The Tenth reserves to the states or the people the powers not delegated to the United States.2

Twelve proposed, ten ratified

Congress proposed twelve amendments, not ten.8,1 The first, on the number of constituents per representative, was never ratified. The second, delaying any change in congressional pay until after an election, sat unratified for two centuries until it was finally adopted on May 7, 1992, as the Twenty-seventh Amendment.1 Articles three through twelve, ratified by three-fourths of the states by December 15, 1791, became the first ten amendments.1,4 On October 2, 1789, President Washington sent copies of the twelve amendments to the states.4,5

What it did not cover

The Bill of Rights restrained only the federal government. The American Yawp points out that its religious-freedom clause did not stop states from supporting churches, and Massachusetts continued to support its Congregational church until 1833.6 Women found no special protection or guarantee of a political voice in it.6

Extending it to the states

After the Civil War the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) declared that no state could deprive any person of “life, liberty, or property, without due process of law.” Its author, Congressman John Bingham of Ohio, intended it to make the Bill of Rights binding on the states.7 Senator Jacob Howard of Michigan likewise said it would extend to the states “the personal rights guaranteed and secured by the first eight amendments.” Historians disagree about how widely that view was shared, and for many years the Supreme Court ruled that the amendment did not extend the Bill of Rights to the states. The National Archives notes that the arguments made in that era became the basis for change in the twentieth century.7

The document itself

The original on display in the Rotunda of the National Archives in Washington is Congress’s file copy of the joint resolution of September 25, 1789, proposing all twelve amendments. It was signed by Speaker of the House Frederick Muhlenberg and by Vice President John Adams as president of the Senate.8,1
