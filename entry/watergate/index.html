The Watergate Scandal

The abuses of power that forced President Nixon to resign, 1972–1974

June 17, 1972 – August 9, 1974 · Washington, D.C.

On June 17, 1972, police arrested five men inside the Democratic National Committee offices in the Watergate complex as they tried to install bugging equipment. One worked for President Richard Nixon’s reelection committee.7 Nixon then joined a cover-up, which reporters, a Senate committee, special prosecutors, and the Supreme Court gradually exposed.3,1 His secret White House tapes proved he had obstructed justice. Facing impeachment, he resigned on August 9, 1974, the first and only president to do so, and a month later his successor pardoned him.5,7,9

Why it matters

Watergate showed how far a president could go in abusing power and covering it up. It also showed that the press, Congress, and the courts could hold him to account, and that the law applied even to the president.1,6,5

Historical background

Nixon, elected in 1968, often felt that enemies were plotting against him. The Miller Center’s Watergate exhibit says he came to believe that Jews, intellectuals, and Ivy Leaguers, along with the press, were conspiring to destroy him. It describes his thinking as an “imitation of the enemy”: he persuaded himself that he was acting in self-defense and could therefore use any means.4 In 1971 the Pentagon Papers, a secret government history of the Vietnam War, leaked to the press. The Nixon administration tried unsuccessfully to stop the New York Times and the Washington Post from publishing them.7 Nixon feared the leak was part of a conspiracy and set up a secret White House investigations unit, the Special Investigations Unit, nicknamed “the Plumbers.”1,4 The Miller Center historian Ken Hughes writes that it was created without congressional authorization and carried out an illegal break-in without a warrant. Two of its members, the former CIA officer E. Howard Hunt and the former FBI agent G. Gordon Liddy, burglarized the office of the psychiatrist of Daniel Ellsberg, the man who leaked the papers.1 Nixon was also recorded ordering a break-in at the Brookings Institution to get files on Vietnam: “Get in and get those files. Blow the safe and get it.”7 The break-in never took place.2

Nixon had also set up a secret White House taping system. The first recordings were made on February 16, 1971, and on that day Nixon told his aides that the tapes were for his private file.3

Causes and contributing factors

The break-in itself began as a routine security call. Frank Wills, a 24-year-old guard at the Watergate Office Building, found doors taped open shortly after midnight and called the police just before 2 a.m. on June 17, 1972.2 The police arrested five men inside the Democratic National Committee headquarters. They found the men trying to install sophisticated bugging equipment.7,2 One of them, James McCord, was a former CIA employee working as a security aide for the Committee to Re-elect the President (known as CREEP).7 Liddy had overseen the burglary from a nearby hotel room, and the FBI quickly linked the burglars to Hunt.3,2

The sources are careful about who knew what. Yawp states that there is no direct evidence that Nixon ordered the break-in, and the Miller Center says it remains uncertain whether he knew of the plan in advance.7,4 What he did next is clear from the tapes. On June 20 he told his aide Charles Colson that “it’s going to be forgotten.”2 On June 23, the same day his chief of staff H. R. Haldeman proposed it, Nixon agreed to have the CIA tell the FBI to “stay the hell out of this,” falsely claiming that the investigation would expose a CIA operation.3,1,5 (One Miller Center essay prints the date of this tape as July 23, 1972. Its other pages, and the order of events they describe, give June 23, six days after the break-in.)1,5,3 Hughes writes that Nixon launched the cover-up to hide other crimes, including the Plumbers’ earlier break-in. This led to the axiom that the cover-up can be worse than the crime.1

What happened

The cover-up (1972). Administration officials paid hush money to the burglars and White House aides.7 On August 1, 1972, the Washington Post reporters Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein reported that a $25,000 check made out to the Nixon campaign had been deposited in a burglar’s bank account.3 Their informant, the FBI’s associate director Mark Felt, was known only as “Deep Throat”; his identity became public years later.3 The Post kept tying the burglars to the White House, but Nixon beat George McGovern in a landslide in November.3,7

The cover-up unravels (1973). In January 1973 seven men went to trial before Judge John Sirica: the five burglars along with Hunt and Liddy. All pleaded guilty or were convicted.3 On March 23, Sirica read in court a letter from McCord saying that defendants had been pressured to plead guilty and keep quiet and that perjury had been committed at the trial.1 Two days earlier White House counsel John Dean had warned Nixon of “a cancer, within, close to the presidency.”3 Dean soon decided to turn state’s evidence, and on April 30 Nixon announced the resignations of Dean, Haldeman, John Ehrlichman, and Attorney General Richard Kleindienst, while presenting himself as innocent.3,1 The Senate had created a select committee under Sam Ervin of North Carolina on February 7, and its televised hearings began on May 17. Attorney General Elliot Richardson appointed Archibald Cox as special prosecutor the next day.3 Public television broadcast the hearings gavel to gavel and rebroadcast them each evening.11 Dean told the committee about the cover-up. On July 16, the White House aide Alexander Butterfield disclosed that Nixon had a voice-activated taping system in the Oval Office.3

The fight over the tapes. Cox and the Senate committee asked for the tapes, and Nixon refused. He said the confidentiality of presidential conversations was at stake.3 In late October he fired Cox. Richardson and his deputy William Ruckelshaus resigned rather than carry out the order, which was called the “Saturday Night Massacre.”3,1 The public outcry forced Nixon to reverse course and turn the tapes over to Judge Sirica. A new special prosecutor, Leon Jaworski, subpoenaed 64 more, including the June 23 tape.1 In March 1974 a grand jury indicted Haldeman, Ehrlichman, Colson, John Mitchell, and three other Nixon aides.3 In April the White House instead released more than 1,250 pages of edited transcripts, which raised more questions than they answered.3 Among the recordings was an 18-minute portion that had been mysteriously erased.3

The end (1974). On July 24, 1974, the Supreme Court ruled 8–0 in United States v. Nixon that Nixon had to turn over the tapes. Chief Justice Warren Burger wrote that the president had no “absolute, unqualified Presidential privilege of immunity from judicial process under all circumstances.”3,5 Three days later the House Judiciary Committee passed the first of three articles of impeachment, for obstruction of justice.3 The Miller Center says that with the release of the tapes “the bottom fell out” of Nixon’s support. Senator Barry Goldwater told him that at most eighteen senators would vote against conviction, too few to save him.1,5

Immediate consequences

On the evening of August 8, 1974, Nixon announced his resignation in a televised address from the Oval Office at 9:01 p.m. He said he no longer had a strong enough political base in Congress and added: “I have never been a quitter. To leave office before my term is completed is abhorrent to every instinct in my body.” He conceded that “if some of my judgments were wrong, and some were wrong, they were made in what I believed at the time to be the best interest of the Nation.”8 Secretary of State Henry Kissinger initialed his letter of resignation at 11:35 a.m. the next day.5 Vice President Gerald Ford took the oath and, in his inaugural remarks, declared, “Our long national nightmare is over.”1 The House never voted on impeachment.7

On September 8 Ford signed Proclamation 4311, giving Nixon “a full, free, and absolute pardon” for all offenses against the United States from January 20, 1969, through August 9, 1974.9 He said that “theirs is an American tragedy in which we all have played a part” and that he must “firmly shut and seal this book.”10 The pardon at once divided the country. A Gallup poll found 62 percent opposed and 31 percent in favor, and Ford’s approval rating fell from 71 to 50 percent.5 Ford pardoned only the man who gave the orders. Mitchell, Haldeman, and Ehrlichman were all convicted and sent to prison, and Hunt served 33 months.5,2

Long-term significance

The White House tapes, the Miller Center writes, ended Nixon’s presidency by proving his involvement in the cover-up. They also fueled a generation’s skepticism about political leaders.6 The American Yawp adds that Watergate did more to unravel public trust than any other scandal of the period, and it helped Democrats win big gains in the 1974 midterm elections. Ford’s pardon damaged his own chances in 1976, which Jimmy Carter won on an image of honesty set against Nixon’s.7 The Miller Center cautions that many things worked against Ford, including unemployment of 7.8 percent and a strong primary challenge from Ronald Reagan.5

Opinion on the pardon changed over time. Gallup found Americans evenly split in 1982 (46 percent to 46 percent) and in favor by 54 to 39 percent in 1986. In 2001 Senator Edward Kennedy, who had opposed it, gave Ford the Profiles in Courage Award for making it possible to begin “the process of healing.”5 Some historians still disagree. Rick Perlstein argues that the pardon treated the presidency as “too big to fail” and set off “a cascade of élite wrongdoing.”5 Nixon spent his later years rehabilitating his reputation through the 1977 Frost interviews, his memoirs, and commentary on foreign policy. His arms control agreements and opening to China helped end the Cold War, but Watergate remains the most memorable part of his career.5,6 He died in 1994. At his funeral President Bill Clinton asked that Nixon be judged on “his entire life and career.”5
