The Federalist Papers

Eighty-five essays defending the proposed Constitution

October 1787 – May 1788 · New York

The Federalist, commonly called the Federalist Papers, is a series of 85 essays written between October 1787 and May 1788 by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay under the shared pen name “Publius.”1 Published mainly in New York newspapers, they urged New Yorkers to ratify the proposed Constitution and explained its provisions in detail.1 Hamilton wrote more than half and Madison twenty-nine.3,2 The essays on faction, checks and balances, and the courts became the classic explanation of American constitutional design, and judges still cite them to understand what the framers intended.1,4,5

Why it matters

New York, the state the essays were written to persuade, ratified by just three votes.6 Whatever their effect on that vote, the Federalist Papers became the classic exposition of the American constitutional system. Because Hamilton and Madison had both been delegates to the Convention, the Library of Congress notes, the essays “are often used today to help interpret the intentions of those drafting the Constitution.”1

Historical circumstances

After the Constitutional Convention finished its work in September 1787, the Constitution had to be approved by special ratifying conventions in the states.6 Critics, the Anti-Federalists, organized against it. They warned that the powerful new government could take away Americans’ liberty, and they made the Convention’s refusal to include a bill of rights a rallying point.6 Citizens argued over the plan in newspapers, letters, sermons, and coffeehouses.6

In New York, where ratification was in doubt, Hamilton recruited Madison and Jay to write essays under the pseudonym “Publius.”2 The essays appeared mostly in two New York newspapers, the New York Packet and the Independent Journal, and were reprinted elsewhere.1 Madison wrote twenty-nine of them between November 1787 and March 1788.2 A bound edition revised by Hamilton appeared in 1788, but the authors were not named essay by essay until an 1818 edition with Madison’s corrections. Because of this history, the Library of Congress notes, authorship, numbering, and wording “may vary with different editions.”1

Principal ideas

  • A test for the world. In No. 1 Hamilton wrote that Americans would decide “whether societies of men are really capable or not of establishing good government from reflection and choice, or whether they are forever destined to depend for their political constitutions on accident and force.”7
  • A large republic controls faction. In No. 10 Madison defined a faction as citizens “united and actuated by some common impulse of passion, or of interest, adversed to the rights of other citizens.” Rather than trying to remove its causes, he argued, a large republic would contain so many interests that no majority could easily unite to oppress others: “Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests.”4
  • Checks and balances. In No. 51 Madison explained that each branch must be able to resist the others: “Ambition must be made to counteract ambition.” He added, “If men were angels, no government would be necessary.”8
  • An energetic executive. In No. 70 Hamilton argued that “Energy in the Executive is a leading character in the definition of good government.”9
  • The courts. In No. 78 Hamilton called the judiciary “the least dangerous” branch, with “neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment,” and argued that courts must treat the Constitution as higher law than ordinary statutes.5
  • No bill of rights. In No. 84 Hamilton argued that a bill of rights was “not only unnecessary in the proposed Constitution, but would even be dangerous.”10
  • Slavery and representation. No. 54 defended the Constitution’s rule counting enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for representation, asking whether the Convention could have been consistent if it had left them out of representation but counted them for taxes.11

The ratification fight

Massachusetts narrowly ratified early in 1788, attaching proposed amendments, and other states followed the same pattern.6 Virginia approved 89 to 79 after nearly a month of debate, and on July 2, 1788, Congress announced that enough states had ratified for the Constitution to take effect. New York ratified by just three votes.6

The bill of rights question

Hamilton’s argument in No. 84 lost. The Federalists had claimed a bill of rights was redundant and even dangerous, but to win ratification they promised amendments. Madison, who had won a seat in the House only by promising his Virginia voters a list of rights, led the effort that produced the Bill of Rights in 1791.6,10
