Montgomery Bus Boycott

The thirteen-month protest that launched the modern civil rights movement

December 5, 1955 – December 20, 1956 · Montgomery, Alabama

The Montgomery bus boycott began on December 5, 1955, four days after Rosa Parks was arrested for refusing to give up her bus seat to a white passenger.1,6 For 381 days Montgomery’s Black residents walked, shared rides, and organized carpools rather than ride segregated city buses.6,3 The protest was planned by the Women’s Political Council and E. D. Nixon and led by the Montgomery Improvement Association. Its president was a twenty-six-year-old minister, Martin Luther King Jr.1,2,7 It survived bombings, mass indictments, and court injunctions, and it ended in victory after the Supreme Court affirmed, in Browder v. Gayle, that bus segregation was unconstitutional.1,3 The boycott made King a national figure and showed that nonviolent mass protest could defeat Jim Crow.1

Why it matters

Montgomery proved that ordinary people acting together could defeat Jim Crow, and it introduced Martin Luther King Jr. to the nation.1,7

Historical background

Montgomery’s buses were rigidly segregated. The front ten seats were reserved for white passengers, and Black riders were expected to give up seats behind them when the bus filled with white passengers.6,7 Black passengers often had to pay at the front and then re-enter by the rear door.1 Drivers claimed the discretion to move the line between Black and white sections.6 Jo Ann Robinson, an English professor at Alabama State College, was ordered off a nearly empty bus in 1949 for sitting in the fifth row.4

The Women’s Political Council (WPC), an organization of Black professional women founded in 1946, made bus segregation a priority after Robinson became its president in 1950.1,4 In March 1954 the council asked Mayor W. A. Gayle to end the practice of making Black riders stand over empty seats and to have buses stop at every corner in Black neighborhoods, as they did in white ones. In a May 21 letter Robinson warned that “twenty-five or more local organizations” had talked of a city-wide boycott.1 Earlier arrests, of fifteen-year-old Claudette Colvin and eighteen-year-old Mary Louise Smith in 1955, did not mobilize the community.1 The American Yawp notes that in 1953 a Black member of the Women’s Army Corps, Sarah Keys, had already challenged segregated interstate buses.7

Causes and contributing factors

On the evening of December 1, 1955, Rosa Parks, a forty-two-year-old seamstress at the Montgomery Fair department store who was active in the local NAACP, was sitting in the first row behind the white section. When the bus filled, driver James Blake ordered her row to stand. Three passengers moved; Parks did not, and she was arrested.6 E. D. Nixon, a Pullman porter, union leader, and former head of the local NAACP, saw her as the ideal plaintiff: “She’s morally clean, she’s reliable, nobody had nothing on her, she had the courage of her convictions.”5 With the white attorney Clifford Durr and his wife Virginia, he bailed her out of jail.1,5

That night Robinson, two students, and a colleague, John Cannon, mimeographed about 52,500 leaflets calling on Black residents to stay off the buses on Monday, December 5.4,1 On December 2 ministers and community leaders met at King’s Dexter Avenue Baptist Church and agreed to support the one-day boycott, which also received unexpected publicity in weekend newspapers and on radio and television.1

What happened

On December 5 about 90 percent of Montgomery’s Black citizens stayed off the buses.1 That afternoon, at Mt. Zion AME Church, leaders formed the Montgomery Improvement Association to continue the protest and elected King, new to the city, as president. Nixon became treasurer.2,5 Parks later observed that King had not been in Montgomery long enough “to make any strong friends or enemies.”1 That evening several thousand people packed Holt Street Baptist Church and voted to continue. “If we are wrong,” King told them, “the Supreme Court of this nation is wrong. If we are wrong, the Constitution of the United States is wrong. If we are wrong, God Almighty is wrong.”1

The MIA’s first demands were moderate: courteous treatment, first-come, first-served seating with Black riders filling from the rear and white riders from the front, and Black drivers on mostly Black routes.1,2 When the city refused and began penalizing Black taxi drivers for helping the boycotters, the MIA built a carpool of about 300 cars, modeled on a 1953 bus boycott in Baton Rouge.1 Weekly mass meetings with sermons and music kept the community together, and money came from collection plates and from supporters in the North, including the fundraising group In Friendship founded by Bayard Rustin, Ella Baker, and Stanley Levison.2,1

Resistance was fierce. In early 1956 the homes of King and Nixon were bombed. King calmed the crowd outside his house: “Be calm as I and my family are. … If anything happens to me, there will be others to take my place.”1 (The American Yawp places the bombings of King’s and Nixon’s homes, along with four Black churches, a year into the boycott.)7 Police harassed Robinson, and the governor eventually ordered state police to guard boycott leaders’ homes.4 In February 1956 the city indicted dozens of boycott leaders under a 1921 Alabama law against conspiracies to interfere with lawful business. The King Institute’s boycott article says more than 80 were indicted and its MIA article says 89. King alone was tried, and in March he was convicted and ordered to pay $500 or serve 386 days in jail.1,2

Women sustained the movement. Robinson edited the MIA newsletter and Johnnie Carr and others ran committees.4,1 Mary Fair Burks of the WPC credited “the nameless cooks and maids who walked endless miles for a year to bring about the breach in the walls of segregation.”1 Rustin and Glenn Smiley advised King on Gandhian nonviolence. “Christ showed us the way,” King said, “and Gandhi in India showed it could work.”1

Immediate consequences

On February 1, 1956, lawyers Fred Gray and Charles Langford filed Browder v. Gayle in federal court on behalf of Aurelia Browder, Susie McDonald, Claudette Colvin, and Mary Louise Smith. Parks was not included, so that the court would decide only the constitutionality of bus segregation.3 In June 1956 a three-judge district court ruled 2 to 1, citing Brown v. Board of Education, that bus segregation was unconstitutional. The King Institute dates the ruling to June 5 and the National Archives to June 4.3,6 On November 13, 1956, while King sat in court defending the carpools against an injunction, he learned that the Supreme Court had affirmed the ruling.3 The MIA kept walking until the order arrived. On December 20, 1956, the community voted to end the boycott, and the next morning King, Ralph Abernathy, E. D. Nixon, and Glenn Smiley rode an integrated bus.1,3 “We came to see that, in the long run, it is more honorable to walk in dignity than ride in humiliation,” King said.1

Long-term significance

The boycott’s combination of mass nonviolent protest and Christian ethics became the model for challenging segregation across the South.1 In January 1957 the MIA became one of the founding organizations of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, which King and other ministers created to coordinate civil rights efforts across the South.2,7 The American Yawp writes that the boycott not only crushed segregation on Montgomery’s buses but energized the entire civil rights movement.7 Parks became known as the “Mother of the Civil Rights Movement.”6 Robinson later called the boycott “the most beautiful memory that all of us who participated will carry to our final resting place.”4
