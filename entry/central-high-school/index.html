Little Rock Central High School

Site of the 1957 desegregation crisis, now a National Historic Site

Little Rock, Arkansas

Little Rock Central High School became the center of a national crisis in September 1957, when nine Black students, the Little Rock Nine, tried to enter under a court-approved desegregation plan.1,2 Governor Orval Faubus sent the Arkansas National Guard to keep them out, and President Dwight D. Eisenhower answered by sending the Army’s 101st Airborne Division to protect them.1,1 The standoff led to Cooper v. Aaron (1958), in which the Supreme Court unanimously ruled that states could not nullify its desegregation decisions.3 Central High is still an operating public high school and is also a National Historic Site run by the National Park Service.4

Why it matters

Central High became a symbol of the struggle over Brown v. Board of Education. The crisis drew a president into using federal troops to enforce a court order against a state’s governor, and it produced Cooper v. Aaron, one of the strongest statements of the Supreme Court’s authority over the states.1,3

About the place

Central High is an active school in the Little Rock School District, so access to the building is limited to students and staff. National Park Service rangers lead guided tours of the grounds.4 Construction of the school, first called Little Rock Senior High School, began in 1926 at a cost of $1.5 million.4 The Park Service visitor center stands across the street on a road named for Daisy Bates, the NAACP leader who guided the nine students.5,2

History

Before the crisis. On May 17, 1954, the Supreme Court ruled in Brown v. Board of Education that segregated schools were unconstitutional.1,3 Central High was not the first Arkansas school to respond. Schools in Charleston and Fayetteville desegregated in the fall of 1954, Hoxie in 1955, and Bentonville in 1956.4 The Little Rock School Board adopted a gradual plan, and nine Black students were scheduled to enter Central High in September 1957.3 They were recruited by Daisy Bates, president of the Arkansas NAACP: Minnijean Brown, Terrence Roberts, Elizabeth Eckford, Ernest Green, Thelma Mothershed, Melba Pattillo, Gloria Ray, Jefferson Thomas, and Carlotta Walls.2

The governor’s stand. Just before school opened, Governor Orval Faubus sent the Arkansas National Guard to the school and placed it “off limits” to the Black students. The Supreme Court later found that the school authorities had not asked for this and that no crowds or violence had occurred until then.3 Sources date the Guard’s deployment slightly differently: the Supreme Court gives September 2, the day before the students were due, while the King Institute dates it to September 4, the first day they tried to enter, when a white mob gathered outside.3,2 NAACP lawyers, among them Thurgood Marshall, won a federal injunction against the governor.2 On September 23 the students entered under police protection, but they were removed that morning because officers could not control the crowd.3,2

Federal troops. Martin Luther King Jr. telegraphed Eisenhower urging him “to take a strong forthright stand in the Little Rock situation.”2 On September 24, 1957, Eisenhower signed Executive Order 10730 and spoke to the nation on radio and television.1 The next day federal troops escorted the nine students into the school.3 The King Institute writes that Eisenhower acted “reluctantly,” aware that Little Rock was becoming an international embarrassment.2 Regular Army troops stayed until November 27, 1957, and were replaced by federalized National Guardsmen for the rest of the year.3 Eight of the nine finished the year; the Supreme Court later quoted a lower court’s finding of “repeated incidents of more or less serious violence directed against the Negro students and their property.”3 At the end of the school year Ernest Green became the first Black student to graduate from Central High, with King in the audience.2

*Cooper v. Aaron. In 1958 the school board asked to delay desegregation for two and a half years because of “chaos, bedlam and turmoil.” On September 29, 1958, the Supreme Court refused in an opinion signed by all nine justices.3 It held that the rights declared in Brown “can neither be nullified openly and directly by state legislators or state executive or judicial officers, nor nullified indirectly by them through evasive schemes for segregation,” and that Brown* was “the supreme law of the land.”3

The closed schools. Faubus then closed all four of Little Rock’s public high schools in the fall of 1958 rather than continue desegregation.2,6 The King Institute writes that the schools reopened after a December 1959 ruling.2 The American Yawp notes that Little Rock’s schools “closed rather than allow integration.”6
