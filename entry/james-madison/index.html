James Madison

Architect of the Constitution and fourth president

1751–1836

James Madison, a Virginia planter and enslaver, did more than anyone to design the Constitution of the United States. His exertions earned him the title “Father of the Constitution,” though he insisted the document was the work of “many heads & many hands.”1 He drafted the Virginia Plan that framed the Convention’s debates, wrote twenty-nine of the Federalist Papers, and steered the Bill of Rights through the first Congress.2,1 With Thomas Jefferson he founded the Republican Party, which opposed Hamilton’s Federalists.2,1 As Jefferson’s secretary of state and then as president from 1809 to 1817, he led the country through the troubled years before and during the War of 1812.1,2

Why it matters

Madison’s ideas about large republics, factions, and checks and balances, set out in the Federalist Papers, remain central to American constitutional thought. The Miller Center calls him the most relevant constitutional philosopher in American history.1,9,10 His notes of the Constitutional Convention are its most important record.5

Early life and context

James Madison was born on March 16, 1751, at Belle Grove, his mother’s family plantation at Port Conway in King George County, Virginia. He was the eldest of twelve children of James Madison Sr. and Nelly Conway Madison.2,7 He grew up on the family estate in Orange County that came to be called Montpelier, a plantation worked by a large enslaved population.2,1 After schooling from his mother, at a school run by the Reverend Donald Robertson, and from a tutor, he went in 1769 to the College of New Jersey, now Princeton, rather than the College of William and Mary.2 He finished four years of study in three, getting by on “the minimum of sleep & maximum of study.” Under the college’s president, John Witherspoon, he absorbed the philosophy of the Scottish Enlightenment and read Hume, Adam Smith, and Montesquieu.2,1 He graduated in 1771 and stayed on to study Hebrew, perhaps thinking of the ministry.2

Back in Virginia, often in poor health and without direction, Madison found his cause in the Revolution and in religious liberty. In December 1774 he joined the Orange County Committee of Safety, and in 1776 he was elected to the Virginia Convention, which wrote the new state’s constitution.2 Disturbed by the persecution of Baptists, Presbyterians, and other dissenters by authorities tied to the established Anglican Church, he amended George Mason’s Virginia Declaration of Rights so that it guaranteed not mere toleration but the “full and free exercise” of religion.2 He lost reelection in 1777 because he refused to treat voters to whiskey, which he considered buying votes.2

In 1780, at twenty-nine, Madison became the youngest member of the Continental Congress, where he took copious notes and tried without success to give Congress its own revenue through a 5 percent tax on imports.2,1 In the Virginia House of Delegates he opposed Patrick Henry’s bill to tax citizens to support Christian teachers. His anonymous “Memorial and Remonstrance Against Religious Assessments” (1785) argued that religion “must be left to the conviction and conscience of every man.” The bill was defeated, and in January 1786 Madison won passage of Jefferson’s Virginia Statute for Establishing Religious Freedom.2

Major actions and achievements

The Constitution. Madison studied the failures of earlier confederacies and concluded that the United States needed a strong national republic.2 His Virginia Plan, adopted in May 1787 as the Convention’s agenda, proposed three branches of government and a two-house legislature based on population.2 He won on representation by population in the House and on separate executive and judicial branches, but he lost on equal representation in the Senate and on a congressional veto over state laws, and for a time he doubted the new government could succeed.2 Throughout the Convention he kept detailed notes of the debates.8,5

The Federalist. At Alexander Hamilton’s invitation, Madison wrote twenty-nine of the essays signed “Publius” between November 1787 and March 1788.2 In Federalist No. 10 he argued, against the common view that only small republics could last, that a large republic would contain so many factions that no single majority could easily oppress the rest: “Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests.”2,9 In Federalist No. 51 he explained the logic of checks and balances: “Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. … If men were angels, no government would be necessary.”10 In Federalist No. 54 he defended counting enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for representation and taxation.2 At the Virginia ratifying convention of June 1788 he answered Patrick Henry, and to meet George Mason’s objection that the Constitution lacked a bill of rights, he promised to make one the first business of the new Congress.2

The Bill of Rights. Elected to the House of Representatives, Madison became, in the Miller Center’s words, a sort of prime minister to President Washington.1 In June 1789 he proposed nineteen amendments. Congress reduced them to twelve, and the states ratified ten, which took effect as the Bill of Rights on December 15, 1791.2,11 Madison had wanted the changes woven into the text of the Constitution itself, but they were added as separate amendments.2

Party leader and secretary of state. Madison soon broke with Hamilton over the national debt, the Bank, and relations with Britain. In 1790 he and Jefferson helped broker the deal that traded Southern support for federal assumption of state debts for a capital on the Potomac.2 By 1792 he described the country as divided into “republican” and “anti-republican” parties, and he and Jefferson led the Republicans.2,1 He married Dolley Payne Todd, a widow, in 1794, and she became a political partner who did much to define the role of first lady.2,1,6 Against the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798 he wrote the Virginia Resolutions, which defended freedom of the press and asserted that states might “interpose” against unconstitutional laws.2 As Jefferson’s secretary of state from 1801 to 1809 he oversaw the Louisiana Purchase and persuaded Jefferson to adopt the Embargo of 1807, which proved unpopular and ineffective.2,1

Beliefs and ideas

Madison believed that liberty was best protected not by small, homogeneous republics but by a large federal republic whose many interests would check one another, and by a government whose separate branches would restrain each other.9,10 He was a lifelong champion of religious freedom.2 He favored congressional over presidential power and wanted the presidency to keep a republican simplicity.8 Over time his views on federal power shifted. He opposed Hamilton’s national bank in 1791 but accepted a second national bank after the War of 1812.2

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Mr. Madison’s War. As president, Madison faced British and French attacks on American shipping, British impressment of American sailors, and a Native confederacy in the Ohio Valley led by Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa.4,1 He won election in 1808 with 122 electoral votes to 47, and on June 18, 1812, he signed a declaration of war against Britain.3,2 He was reelected that fall over DeWitt Clinton, 128 electoral votes to 89, despite Federalist opposition to “Mr. Madison’s War.”3,2 His invasions of Canada failed in 1812 and did little better in 1813, though Oliver Hazard Perry won control of Lake Erie.2,4 In August 1814 British troops routed American militia at Bladensburg, occupied Washington, and burned the White House and the Capitol. Dolley Madison and the enslaved valet Paul Jennings were among those who saved Gilbert Stuart’s portrait of Washington.2,6,4 The defense of Fort McHenry at Baltimore inspired Francis Scott Key’s poem that became the national anthem.4 New England Federalists met at the Hartford Convention. The Miller Center says they considered secession, while Encyclopedia Virginia says only that the meeting prompted rumors of it.4,2 The Treaty of Ghent, signed in December 1814, cost the United States no territory and no concessions to British demands, and Andrew Jackson’s victory at New Orleans helped Americans remember the war as a triumph.2,4 Historians still debate whether Madison was a panicky wartime leader or, as some recent scholars argue, a resolute one.8 He had also been criticized in 1812 for spending $50,000 of public money on the papers of a British spy, John Henry, which failed to prove the Federalist plot he suspected.3

Slavery. Madison enslaved more than one hundred people. He called slavery an evil and supported gradual, compensated emancipation tied to resettling freed people outside the country, and in 1833 he became president of the American Colonization Society.2,5 But he did not free the people he held, he doubted that Black and white Americans could live together in freedom, and in 1834 he sold sixteen enslaved people to a relative in Louisiana to pay debts.2 The Miller Center calls him “an anti-slavery enslaver” whose complicity was less visible than Jefferson’s but more tangled than Washington’s, since Washington freed the people he enslaved in his will.5,8

Legacy

After the war Madison, once an opponent, signed a charter for a Second Bank of the United States and accepted protective tariffs. On his last day in office he vetoed an internal improvements bill because no constitutional amendment authorized such spending.2 He retired to Montpelier in 1817. He advised on farming, served as rector of the University of Virginia from 1826 to 1834, and organized his notes on the Constitutional Convention, which he hoped would support Dolley after his death.2,5 In his last years he denied that his Virginia Resolutions justified South Carolina’s nullification doctrine.2 He died at Montpelier on June 28, 1836. His enslaved servant Paul Jennings later wrote that “he ceased breathing as quietly as the snuff of a candle goes out.”2 His final message urged that the “Union of the States be cherished and perpetuated.”1 Facing debts, Dolley later sold Montpelier and the enslaved community that lived there.5

Scholars usually rate Madison higher as a constitution-maker and legislator than as president.8 Some have asked whether George Washington, whose presence gave the Convention its legitimacy, deserves the title “Father of the Constitution” as much as Madison. The Miller Center suggests that Madison might as fairly be called a father of the nation itself, for persuading thirteen states to become one.8
