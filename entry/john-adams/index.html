John Adams

Revolutionary leader, diplomat, and second president, who kept the young nation out of war with France

1735–1826

John Adams was a blunt, brilliant Massachusetts lawyer who became one of the leading voices for American independence, a diplomat who helped negotiate the peace with Britain, the nation’s first vice president, and its second president.1,2 In the Continental Congress he nominated George Washington to command the army, led the faction pushing for independence, and served on the committee that prepared the Declaration.2 As president he faced an undeclared naval war with Revolutionary France; he built up the navy and signed the Alien and Sedition Acts, but he refused to let the conflict become a full war and made peace instead.1,5,7 Split from his own Federalist Party, he lost the election of 1800 to Thomas Jefferson. He died on July 4, 1826, the same day as Jefferson.3,6

Why it matters

John Adams helped lead the colonies to independence, secured the peace that recognized it, and as president kept the new nation out of a war it might not have survived, at the cost of his own reelection. His peaceful handover of power to a political rival in 1801 set an important precedent.2,3,7

Early life and context

Adams was born on October 30, 1735, into a comfortable but not wealthy farming family in Braintree, Massachusetts, about fifteen miles south of Boston. His father, a deacon, farmed and made shoes and hoped his son would become a minister.2 Adams entered Harvard at fifteen and graduated in 1755, then taught school in Worcester while studying law.2 He began practicing in Boston in 1758; he had one client his first year, but by 1770 he may have had the largest caseload of any lawyer in the city.2 In 1764 he married Abigail Smith, the daughter of a Weymouth minister, beginning one of the great partnerships in American history.2,9

Major actions and achievements

The Boston Massacre trial. In 1770 Adams agreed to defend the British soldiers charged after the Boston Massacre, a decision that angered many patriots. Through his defense, none of the accused was sent to jail.2,9

Revolutionary leader. Adams was at first reluctant to join the protests against the Stamp Act, fearing for his law practice and distrusting radicals such as his cousin Samuel Adams, but he gradually became an open foe of British policy.2 He went to the First Continental Congress in 1774 and the Second in 1775. In June 1775 he nominated George Washington to command the new Continental Army, and he became the leader of the faction pushing for independence.2 He served on more committees than any other member, about ninety, chairing twenty, and headed the Board of War.2 In June 1776 he was named to the committee that prepared the Declaration of Independence. On July 3 he wrote Abigail that the day of independence would be celebrated “with Pomp and Parade… from one End of this Continent to the other.”8

Political thinker. In Thoughts on Government (1776) Adams argued that the executive, legislative, and judicial powers must be separated to prevent tyranny.2 In his Defence of the Constitutions (1787) he warned against both unchecked democracy and an aristocracy that could become an oligarchy, and he called for a strong executive. Some contemporaries wrongly believed his later essays favored a hereditary monarchy.2 During a brief visit home in 1779 he helped draft the Massachusetts Constitution of 1780.2

Diplomat. From 1778 Adams served in France and the Netherlands, where he won a needed loan, and with Franklin, John Jay, and Henry Laurens he negotiated the Treaty of Paris that ended the war.2 In 1785 he became the first U.S. minister to Britain. He came home in 1788 after ten years abroad.2

Vice president. Adams was elected vice president in 1789 and again in 1792, an office he found “devoid of power.”2,3 In 1796 he defeated Jefferson by 71 electoral votes to 68, and under the rules then in force Jefferson became his vice president.3

Beliefs and ideas

Adams distrusted both concentrated power and unchecked majorities. The Miller Center describes him as one of the “natural aristocrats” who believed leaders should be chosen for “superior reason and virtue,” and who thought the executive should stand above party politics.7 He favored property requirements for voting, believing that “the rich, the well-born, and the able” should lead.1 His views on the place of women drew a famous challenge from Abigail. When she urged him in 1776 to “Remember the Ladies,” he replied that “We know better than to repeal our Masculine systems.”10

Controversies, limits, and criticism

The Quasi-War. France, angered by Jay’s Treaty with Britain, began seizing American ships. When French officials demanded bribes from Adams’s envoys in what became known as the XYZ Affair, Congress built up the navy, created the Navy Department, and cancelled the treaties of alliance and commerce signed with France during the Revolution, and an undeclared naval war followed.5,9 Many Federalists hoped for full war.5

The Alien and Sedition Acts. In 1798 the Federalist Congress passed four laws, which Adams signed. Three targeted immigrants, most of whom voted Democratic-Republican: they lengthened the residency requirement for citizenship from five to fourteen years and gave the president power to detain or deport aliens. The Sedition Act punished “subversive speech” with fines and prison.4 There were fifteen indictments and ten convictions under it.4 Jefferson and Madison secretly drafted the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions declaring the acts void.4 The Miller Center notes that Adams neither openly advocated the acts nor personally enforced them.7

Fries’s Rebellion. New taxes to pay for the military buildup led farmers in Pennsylvania to resist tax collectors. Their leaders were sentenced to death for treason, but Adams pardoned them, ignoring Hamilton’s call for a strong show of force.4,7

Legacy

Adams sent a new peace mission to France in 1799 over the objections of much of his party, and he fired two cabinet members who opposed his policy.3,5 The peace he sought split the Federalists. Alexander Hamilton published a pamphlet calling Adams unfit to be president, and in 1800 Adams finished third behind Jefferson and Aaron Burr.3 The Miller Center adds that the three-fifths clause helped the South, and that without it Adams might have won.3 He made a number of last-minute “midnight appointments” of Federalist judges, which led to Marbury v. Madison.3

In retirement at Peacefield, his farm in Quincy, Adams wrote constantly. In 1812 friends brought him and Jefferson back into correspondence, and the two exchanged hundreds of letters.6 Both died on July 4, 1826. Near noon, close to the time Jefferson died, Adams woke and said, “Thomas Jefferson survives.” He died that evening. Born in October 1735, he was ninety, though the Miller Center gives his age as ninety-one.6 His son John Quincy Adams had been elected president in 1824.6

Historians have found his presidency hard to judge. His aloofness and independence isolated him politically, but most agree he was right not to expand the naval war with France into a full war that might have broken the young union.7
