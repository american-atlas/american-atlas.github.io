George Washington

Commander of the Continental Army and first president

1732–1799

George Washington commanded the Continental Army through eight years of war, presided over the convention that wrote the Constitution, and served as the first president of the United States.1,2,3 Twice he gave up power when he could have kept it, by resigning his command in 1783 and by retiring after two terms as president, and those choices, more than any battle, made him the “indispensable man” of the founding.2,5,1 A Virginia planter who held hundreds of people in slavery, he arranged in his will to free those he owned, but only after his wife’s death.2,5

Key facts

Roles
Virginia militia officer in the French and Indian War; Member of the Virginia House of Burgesses (from 1759); Commander in chief of the Continental Army (1775–1783); President of the Constitutional Convention (1787); First president of the United States (1789–1797)
Born
February 22, 1732 (February 11 by the old calendar), at Popes Creek, Westmoreland County, Virginia
Commander in chief
Elected by Congress in June 1775; resigned his commission to Congress in December 1783
Elected president
Unanimously by the Electoral College in 1789 (69 votes) and 1792 (132 votes), the only president elected unanimously twice
Precedents
Created the cabinet; retired after two terms, a limit later written into the 22nd Amendment
Enslaved people
His will freed the people he held in his own right, after Martha Washington’s death
Died
December 14, 1799, at Mount Vernon, of an acute throat infection

Why it matters

Washington’s greatest contribution was his restraint. Americans trusted him with immense power because he had given it up once before, and his conduct as president created the cabinet, the two-term tradition, and the dignity of the office.5,2 His slaveholding is the most troubling part of his legacy. He came to see slavery as wrong but freed the people he owned only in his will, and he never spoke against slavery in public.5

Early life and context

Washington was born on February 22, 1732, at Popes Creek farm in Westmoreland County, Virginia. By the Old Style calendar then used in the British Empire, the date was February 11.1 His father, Augustine, owned nearly 3,000 acres of tobacco land, and George was the first child of his second wife, Mary Ball.1,2 When George was eleven his father died. His older half-brothers inherited most of the estate, including the land that became Mount Vernon, while George received a smaller farm and ten enslaved people. He never had the English schooling his brothers enjoyed, and he later admitted his “consciousness of a defective education.”2,1 Through his half-brother Lawrence’s marriage into the powerful Fairfax family, he learned surveying; at sixteen he joined a Fairfax survey of the Shenandoah Valley, and at seventeen he became the official surveyor of Culpeper County.1,2 In 1751 he went with Lawrence to Barbados, his only trip outside North America, and survived smallpox there, which left him immune to a disease that would ravage the Continental Army. The Miller Center says the illness probably left him unable to father children, while Encyclopedia Virginia notes that the only modern study found no link between smallpox and male infertility.1,2

The French and Indian War. In 1753 Virginia’s lieutenant governor, Robert Dinwiddie, sent the twenty-one-year-old Washington into the Ohio country to order the French out.1,2 In May 1754, leading about 140 Virginians and Native allies, he attacked a French party at Jumonville Glen, and its commander, Joseph Coulon de Jumonville, was killed. The French called it an assassination, and the skirmish helped ignite the French and Indian War.1,2 Weeks later the French forced him to surrender at Fort Necessity.1 In 1755 he served as an aide to General Edward Braddock, whose army was routed near the Monongahela River; Washington had two horses shot from under him and four bullets through his coat.1 He then commanded Virginia’s frontier forces until resigning his commission at the end of 1758.2

Planter and politician. On January 6, 1759, Washington married Martha Dandridge Custis, a wealthy widow with two young children, and took control of the Custis estate of nearly 20,000 acres.1 The couple settled at Mount Vernon, which he eventually expanded to nearly 8,000 acres and a mansion of 11,000 square feet. Martha brought 84 enslaved people to the plantation.2 Elected to the House of Burgesses in 1758, Washington grew frustrated with the British merchants who set the prices for his tobacco and his purchases; he switched from tobacco to wheat and backed boycotts of British goods.1,2 In 1769 he wrote to George Mason that “our lordly Masters in Great Britain will be satisfied with nothing less than the deprication of American freedom.”1

Major actions and achievements

Commander in chief. Washington attended the Second Continental Congress in uniform. On June 15, 1775, the day after it created the Continental Army, Congress chose him as commander in chief; as a Virginian who had never served in the British army, he could unite the colonies.2,1 At Cambridge he found a disorganized army of 14,000 men short of powder, tents, and clothing.1 In March 1776 he forced the British out of Boston by placing cannon hauled from Fort Ticonderoga on Dorchester Heights.1,2 Defeated around New York that summer and fall, he retreated across New Jersey. Then, on the night of December 25–26, 1776, he crossed the icy Delaware River and surprised the Hessian garrison at Trenton, and a week later he struck again at Princeton.1,2

His record as a battlefield commander was mixed. He lost at Brandywine and Germantown in 1777 and lost Philadelphia, and the Miller Center faults him for battle plans too complicated for his army.1,2 But he understood that the cause would survive as long as the army did, and he avoided risking it in a decisive battle, aiming instead to “protract the war.”1,2 At Valley Forge in the winter of 1777–1778 some 2,000 of his men died of disease, hunger, and cold, yet the army held together, and Baron von Steuben’s drills made it a disciplined force.1 After first banning Black soldiers, Washington accepted free Black men in 1776 and later approved the recruitment of Black troops in Rhode Island.1,2 In 1781, with the French army under Rochambeau and the French fleet under de Grasse, he trapped General Charles Cornwallis at Yorktown, Virginia. A white flag went up on October 17, and Cornwallis formally surrendered on October 19.2,1

Giving up power. In March 1783, when unpaid officers at Newburgh, New York, talked of defying Congress, Washington addressed them. Fumbling for his glasses, he said, “Gentlemen, you must pardon me, for I have not only grown gray but almost blind in service to my country,” and the threat of mutiny dissolved.2,1 In December 1783 he returned his commission to Congress at Annapolis. In an age of military dictators and kings, the Miller Center notes, his voluntary surrender of power “was extraordinary.”2

The Constitution. Alarmed by Shays’s Rebellion and the weakness of the Articles of Confederation, Washington feared that “we are fast verging to anarchy and confusion.”1 He was elected president of the Constitutional Convention in May 1787 and attended every session, though as presiding officer he did not take part in the debates.2,1 Delegates shaped the presidency with him in mind; as Pierce Butler of South Carolina recalled, they defined its powers “by their opinions of his virtue.”1 His signature and support helped win ratification.2

The first president. Elected unanimously, Washington took the oath in New York City on April 30, 1789.1,3,7 “I walk on untrodden ground,” he wrote. “There is scarcely any part of my conduct which may not hereafter be drawn into precedent.”1 When the Senate would not give quick advice on an Indian treaty, he turned instead to his department heads: Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson, Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton, War Secretary Henry Knox, and Attorney General Edmund Randolph. On November 26, 1791, he held the first cabinet meeting.3 He appointed eleven Supreme Court justices.3 He backed Hamilton’s plans to assume the states’ war debts and create a national bank, which he signed into law on February 25, 1791, over Jefferson’s objections.3 When western Pennsylvanians rose against the federal whiskey tax in 1794, he called out militias from four states and rode out to inspect them himself. The Whiskey Rebellion collapsed, and he later pardoned the convicted rebels.3,1 In the West, after the defeat of Arthur St. Clair’s army in 1791, General Anthony Wayne defeated the Native confederacy at Fallen Timbers in 1794, and its peoples were pushed west.3

Abroad, Washington kept the young nation out of the war between revolutionary France and Britain. He proclaimed neutrality in April 1793, demanded the recall of the French minister Edmond Genêt, and sent Chief Justice John Jay to London. Jay’s Treaty of 1794 required British troops to leave their western forts but was bitterly unpopular, while Thomas Pinckney’s treaty with Spain opened the Mississippi River to American trade.4,6 Reelected unanimously in 1792, he refused a third term in 1796. In his Farewell Address, published on September 19, 1796, he warned Americans “against the baneful effects of the spirit of party” and against entangling their peace “in the toils of European ambition.”3,7,8

Beliefs and ideas

Washington believed that the Revolution would be lost if the new nation could not govern itself. “What a triumph for the advocates of despotism,” he wrote in the 1780s, “to find that we are incapable of governing ourselves.”1 He favored a strong central government, sided with Hamilton against Jefferson on finance, and claimed to stand above party, though his administration became identified with the Federalists.1,3 He valued republican virtue (self-sacrifice, decorum, and restraint) and modeled the presidency on it.5

His views of slavery changed over his life. Before the war he bought and sold enslaved people without apparent hesitation; the service of Black soldiers during the Revolution challenged his ideas about race. Afterward he resolved not to buy or sell more people and to keep families together.2 By the late 1780s, Encyclopedia Virginia concludes, “a sense of slavery’s injustice began to come over him.”1

Controversies, limits, and criticism

Washington’s slaveholding stands at the center of modern debates about his legacy. As president he lived in Philadelphia, where Pennsylvania law freed enslaved people after six months of residence; he and Martha rotated their enslaved workers back to Virginia to keep them from gaining freedom.9 When Ona Judge, Martha’s maid, escaped in 1796, and the chef Hercules fled in 1797, the Washingtons tried to recapture them, without success.9 He never spoke against slavery in public, fearing, the Miller Center suggests, that the South might leave the Union.5

His other actions drew criticism too. Although he favored protecting Native lands to avoid war, he enforced the Treaty of Fort Harmar, which forced Native nations from their homelands, and his army’s victory at Fallen Timbers opened their lands to white settlers.3 Jay’s Treaty provoked violent protests and helped divide Americans into rival parties.1,4 And in 1798, recalled to command a provisional army during the Quasi-War with France, he forced President John Adams to accept Hamilton as his second in command, a step the Miller Center calls a blow to civilian authority over the army.5

Legacy

Washington retired to Mount Vernon in 1797 and devoted himself to scientific farming, crop rotation, mule breeding, and a profitable whiskey distillery.1 He died on December 14, 1799, of an acute throat infection. “I die hard,” he said, “but I am not afraid to go.”1 His will freed the people he owned in his own right after Martha’s death. The Miller Center calls it “a forceful statement about the morality of slavery from the grave.”5,1

Eulogized as “First in war, first in peace, and first in the hearts of his countrymen,” he remains the preeminent Founding Father.1 Every president after him followed his example of serving only two terms until Franklin Roosevelt, and the Twenty-second Amendment later made the limit law; every president has also worked with a cabinet.5 The national capital, the Washington Monument, and Mount Rushmore all honor him.5
