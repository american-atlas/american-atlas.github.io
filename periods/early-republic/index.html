Period 5 · 1789–1828

The Early Republic

The first decades under the Constitution tested whether a large republic could survive.1,2 Americans built new institutions and divided into the first political parties. In 1800 they peacefully handed power from one party to another.1 They doubled the nation’s territory and fought a second war with Britain.2,4 Meanwhile slavery spread west with cotton, and Native nations faced mounting pressure on their lands.3,2

Inventing a government. When George Washington took office in 1789, almost everything about the new government had to be invented. The Judiciary Act of 1789 created the federal courts, and Washington began meeting with his department heads as a cabinet.7,8 Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton’s plans for federal assumption of state debts and a national bank won approval over the objections of Thomas Jefferson and James Madison, who argued that the Constitution did not authorize a bank.1 Their disagreement hardened into the first party system of Federalists and Democratic-Republicans.1 The Bill of Rights was ratified in 1791.1

The revolution of 1800. Under John Adams, the Alien and Sedition Acts and the president’s quarrels with his own party left the Federalists widely reviled by 1800.1 The election of 1800 ended in a tie between Jefferson and his running mate, Aaron Burr, which the House broke on the thirty-sixth ballot. Adams then left office peacefully, the first transfer of power between parties.1 Chief Justice John Marshall meanwhile claimed for the Supreme Court the power of judicial review in Marbury v. Madison (1803).1

A continental nation. Jefferson’s purchase of Louisiana from France in 1803, which the American Yawp calls “the largest real estate deal in American history,” was made possible partly by the Haitian Revolution, which broke Napoleon’s hold on Haiti.2 The Louisiana Purchase sent the Lewis and Clark Expedition west to the Pacific with the help of Native hosts such as Sacagawea.2,9 In present-day Indiana the Shawnee brothers Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa built a Native confederacy to stop American expansion.2

A second war with Britain. British seizures of American ships and sailors, and British support for Native resistance, led to the War of 1812.4 American invasions of Canada failed and the British burned Washington, but the Treaty of Ghent restored the prewar boundaries. Andrew Jackson’s victory at New Orleans made him a national hero.4 The war was a disaster for the Native nations: Tecumseh was killed in 1813, and Jackson forced the Creeks to cede nearly 23 million acres.4

Markets, cotton and slavery. After the war a “market revolution” remade the nation. The Erie Canal, finished in 1825, linked the Great Lakes to New York City and launched a canal boom.3 In the South, Eli Whitney’s cotton gin let planters expand cotton production, and the enslaved population grew from less than 700,000 in 1790 to more than 1.5 million by 1820.3 The Supreme Court strengthened national power in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819).6

Warnings. The Missouri crisis of 1819–1821 split the Democratic-Republican Party along sectional lines, and Jefferson heard in it “a fire bell in the night.”5 In 1823 the Monroe Doctrine warned European powers away from the Americas, and its makers had limited power to enforce it.10 By the 1820s the age of the founders was giving way to the democratic politics of Andrew Jackson.5
