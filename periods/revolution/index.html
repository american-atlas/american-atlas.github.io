Period 4 · 1763–1789

Revolution and Independence

In a single generation, Britain’s North American colonists went from celebrating victory in the Seven Years’ War to waging war against their king.1,2 Protests over taxation and self-government became a revolution, the Revolution produced a new nation, and the new nation, after a shaky start under the Articles of Confederation, wrote the Constitution that still governs it.2,3,5

Victory and its costs. The period opens in 1763, when the Seven Years’ War ended with Britain in control of most of eastern North America.1 The war doubled Britain’s national debt, and the American Yawp writes that victory gave Britain “a larger empire than they could control.”2,1 The Proclamation of 1763 closed the western lands to settlement, and Native nations led by Pontiac rose against British forts.2,1

Taxes and resistance. To raise revenue and tighten control, Parliament passed the Sugar Act and Currency Act in 1764 and the Stamp Act in 1765, the first direct tax it had ever laid on the colonists.2 Colonists answered with resolutions, a congress of nine colonies, boycotts, and crowd violence, insisting that only their own elected representatives could tax them.2 Parliament repealed the Stamp Act but tried again with the Townshend duties, and resistance spread to new groups, including women who organized boycotts and spinning clubs.2 After the Boston Massacre of 1770 and the Boston Tea Party of 1773, Parliament’s Coercive Acts punished Massachusetts. Instead of isolating Boston, they drew the colonies together and led to the First Continental Congress in 1774.2

War and independence. Fighting broke out at Lexington and Concord in April 1775. The Second Continental Congress created the Continental Army under George Washington while still petitioning the king.2 In 1776 Thomas Paine’s Common Sense made the case for independence, and on July 2 Congress voted for it; two days later it adopted the Declaration of Independence.2 The war was long and hard. After the American victory at Saratoga, France signed a treaty with the United States in 1778, turning the rebellion into a global war.2 In 1781 American and French forces trapped the British at Yorktown, and the Treaty of Paris recognized American independence in 1783.2

Who won, and who lost. The Revolution divided Americans. About sixty thousand Loyalists left the country.2 The Six Nations of the Haudenosaunee split, some fighting for Britain and some for the Americans, and Native peoples on all sides lost land.4 Lord Dunmore’s 1775 proclamation offered freedom to enslaved people who joined the British, and thousands escaped to British lines, while slavery itself survived in the new nation.2 Women such as Abigail Adams ran farms and businesses in wartime, amid labor shortages and inflation.2

A new government. The Articles of Confederation, in force from 1781, created a league of sovereign states whose Congress could not tax or regulate trade.5,2 The Confederation produced the Northwest Ordinance, but debt, trade disputes, and unrest such as Shays’ Rebellion convinced many leaders that a stronger union was needed.5,3 The Constitutional Convention of 1787 produced the Constitution. After a fierce public debate, including the Federalist Papers, it was ratified and took effect in 1789, and the Bill of Rights followed in 1791.3,6
